Why MP4 — and when you shouldn't convert at all

The converter turns whatever you drop on it into an H.264/AAC MP4. Here is what that format buys you, what it costs, and the one case where running a file through it makes things worse.

The short version

MP4 with H.264 video and AAC audio is the most universally playable video format ever made, and it decodes in dedicated hardware on virtually every device. You pay for that with a bigger file than modern codecs would need, and — importantly — with a quality loss every time you re-encode. If a file is already H.264/AAC in an MP4, converting it again only makes it worse.

First: “.mp4” is a container, not a format

This trips up almost everyone. .mp4 is a box. What matters is what is inside the box — the video codec, the audio codec, and how they are arranged. Two files can both end in .mp4 and have almost nothing in common.

Where MP4 is genuinely better

It plays everywhere, using hardware

H.264 decoding is built into the silicon of essentially every phone, TV, tablet, laptop and browser made in the last fifteen years. That means smooth playback and smooth scrubbing at almost no CPU cost — and on a laptop, far less battery burn. No other codec comes close on reach.

+faststart makes web video actually work

An MP4 contains an index describing where every frame lives. By default that index sits at the end of the file, so a browser must download the whole thing before showing the first frame. +faststart moves the index to the front. On a website this is the difference between video that starts instantly and video that looks broken.

yuv420p is the “just works” layout

Fancier colour layouts (4:4:4, 10-bit) look better on paper and are rejected by a surprising amount of software, including some editors and most TVs. yuv420p is the safe common denominator.

Predictability across a pile of odd sources

VHS captures, DVD rips, and phone clips with variable frame rates all behave differently. Normalizing them into one consistent shape is the single most effective cure for audio drifting out of sync partway through an edit.

Where MP4 costs you

Generation loss — the one that actually matters

This is a lossy-to-lossy conversion. The video is decoded back to raw pixels and compressed again from scratch, and detail thrown away in that second pass is gone permanently. It is not a repackage.

The practical rule: if the Info panel says a file is already H.264 with AAC audio in an MP4, do not run it through the converter. You will get a slightly worse file for your trouble. The same applies to the audio — the sound is re-encoded even when the original was perfectly good.

H.264 is old, and old means bigger

The codec dates from 2003. Newer codecs reach the same visual quality in substantially less space:

You are paying roughly 30–50% in file size for compatibility. For video that strangers will open on unknown devices, that is usually the right trade.

“Editor-ready” is a compromise, not the ideal

H.264 is a delivery codec. It stores most frames as differences from their neighbours, so to display one frame at an arbitrary point the editor must decode a run of surrounding frames. That is why scrubbing through H.264 can feel sluggish.

True editing codecs — ProRes, DNxHR — store every frame whole. They scrub instantly and produce enormous files. MP4 is the universal choice; it is not the fastest to edit choice.

Some things get dropped

Only the first video track and the first audio track survive the conversion. Subtitles, commentary tracks, alternate languages and chapter markers do not — worth knowing if you are feeding it a DVD rip or an MKV. Sources with 10-bit colour or HDR are also flattened to standard 8-bit.

The exact command

No mystery here — this is what runs, in your browser, on your machine:

ffmpeg -i input \
  -c:v libx264 -profile:v high -pix_fmt yuv420p \
  -crf 18 -preset medium \
  -c:a aac -b:a 192k -ar 48000 \
  -movflags +faststart \
  output.mp4

-crf is the quality dial and it is backwards from what you might expect: lower means better. 16 is visually lossless for practical purposes, 18 is the default and near-transparent, 20 trades a little detail for a noticeably smaller file. Grainy sources such as VHS captures inflate at any CRF, because film grain is random and random data does not compress.

Rules of thumb

  • Already H.264/AAC MP4? Don't convert. Check with the Info button first.
  • Odd old format — AVI, WMV, MKV, VOB, MOV from a camera? Convert. This is exactly the case MP4 is for.
  • Putting it on a website? Convert, for +faststart alone.
  • Serious colour grading or heavy effects work? MP4 will do, but a ProRes or DNxHR intermediate will edit far more comfortably.
  • Archiving the only copy you have? Keep the original too. A converted file can always be made again from the original; the original cannot be recovered from the conversion.
