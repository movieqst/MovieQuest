# Sekigahara, Keichō 5, 9th month, 15th day (21 October 1600): research brief

For the Battles of History reenactment. Compiled 2 to 3 November 2026.

## How this brief was made, and its limits

- **Web pages could not be opened.** The session's network blocked every outside site: WebFetch failed on ja.wikipedia.org and britannica.com, and curl failed on gsi.go.jp, openstreetmap.org and tabi-mag.jp. **Everything below comes from search-engine summaries of the pages listed in the Sources section, not from reading the pages.** The summaries sometimes say plainly that a page's full text was not visible. Wherever that matters, it is noted.
- No primary source was read in the original. When this brief says "primary", it means a modern author or article *reports* what a named contemporary document says. The exact wording was not checked.
- Some background facts (dates of Hideyoshi's death, Ieyasu's appointment as shōgun, the Osaka campaigns, family crests) are standard in every reference work, but this session did not confirm them in a search result. They are tagged **[std]** so the team knows to check them before publication.

### Tags
- **[est]** established: agreed by historians, or stated as fact by the reference works and official sites below.
- **[prim: …]** primary: from a contemporary or near-contemporary document, which is named. "Reported by X" means it was seen only through a modern summary.
- **[later]** later account: Edo-period chronicles and family histories written decades later and often embellished (e.g. *Sekigahara Gunki Taisei*, *Tokugawa Jikki*, the Ii/Kuroda family traditions), or a story found only in such works.
- **[disp]** disputed: the competing views are given, with who holds them.
- **[estim]** estimate: a number or position that is approximate.
- **[std]** standard reference fact, not re-verified in this session.
- **[unver]** could not be verified at all. Do not use without checking.

Source keys (e.g. **S-Turnbull**) are listed at the end.

### Calendar
- The battle date is Keichō 5, 9th month, 15th day, which converts to **21 October 1600** (Gregorian). (S-Britannica, S-Substack-dates) [est]. One Britannica page gives 20 October. That is most likely a conversion slip, and every other source gives 21 October. (S-Britannica) [disp, minor]
- Japanese dates below are written as month/day of Keichō 5 (e.g. 9/15). Gregorian equivalents are given only where a source gave them.

## 1. Background

### Hideyoshi's death and the regency
- Toyotomi Hideyoshi died in Keichō 3, 8/18 (September 1598). He left his son Hideyori, aged five, under a regency council. [std]
- The council was the **Five Elders** (*go-tairō*): Tokugawa Ieyasu, Maeda Toshiie (who died in 1599), Mōri Terumoto, Ukita Hideie and Uesugi Kagekatsu. Under them were the **Five Commissioners** (*go-bugyō*), among them Ishida Mitsunari, Natsuka Masaie, Mashita Nagamori, Maeda Gen'i and Asano Nagamasa. [std]
- Ishida was a commissioner, a rank below the Elders. To move great lords he had to borrow the authority of Elders such as Terumoto and Hideie. (S-vegcale-Ishida, summarising research) [est]

### The 1599 crisis
- In the **political crisis of Keichō 4, intercalary 3rd month (1599)**, the incident traditionally called the "attack of the seven generals", Ishida withdrew from government business and retired to his castle at **Sawayama**. This weakened his position. (S-vegcale-Ishida) [est]
- The seven generals traditionally named include Katō Kiyomasa, Fukushima Masanori, Kuroda Nagamasa and Hosokawa Tadaoki. [std]
- Behind the crisis lay the split between the "military" faction (*budan-ha*) and the administrators that grew out of the Korean campaigns. Kasaya Kazuhiko stresses the alliance Ieyasu formed with these Toyotomi military lords (S-Kasaya-review). [est as an interpretation]

### Ieyasu's rise
- Ieyasu moved into Osaka Castle (the West Enceinte) and acted more and more as the head of government. [std]
- The traditional story says Ishida built the anti-Ieyasu coalition. Recent work disputes this; see "Who led the West?" below.

### The Aizu (Uesugi) campaign and the rising in the west
- In the summer of 1600 Ieyasu led an army north-east against **Uesugi Kagekatsu** at Aizu, accusing him of preparing for war. [std]
- While Ieyasu was away, Mōri Terumoto answered a call from three of the commissioners, left Hiroshima for Osaka, and **entered the West Enceinte of Osaka Castle on 7/19**. (S-gendai-Takahashi) [est]
- Mitsunari Junji (光成準治) has revised the traditional picture of Terumoto as a "passive figurehead". He argues that Terumoto was actively involved: *Shigaku Zasshi* 113/12 (2004), *Mōri Terumoto* (Minerva, 2016) and *Sekigahara zen'ya*. (S-vegcale-Ishida, S-Kadokawa) [est as a research position]

### The Oyama council (Oyama hyōjō, 7/25)
- **Traditional view.** At Oyama in Shimotsuke, Ieyasu's Toyotomi-vassal generals, led by Fukushima Masanori, swore to turn west against Ishida. [later / disp]
- **Shiramine Jun** argues the council is fictional. He says that the Ieyasu letter to Fukushima on which it rests was later re-dated to 7/24 (he dates it 7/19), and that Fukushima cannot have been at Oyama on 7/25. (S-agora-Oyama) [disp]
- **Honda Takashige** answers that a council at Oyama on 7/25, with Fukushima starting west on the 26th, fits the dated letters of 8/3 from around Odawara or Mishima better. The two have continued to exchange criticism. (S-agora-Oyama) [disp]
- **Kasaya Kazuhiko** treats whether the council existed as one of the main points of controversy (*Ronsō Sekigahara kassen*, Shinchō sensho). He argues that when the council met, Ieyasu's side saw the enemy only as Ishida and Ōtani. The Osaka commissioners joining Ishida afterwards changed everything. (S-Kasaya-review, S-agora-Oyama) [disp]
- **For narration:** say "according to the traditional story" and note that historians argue over whether the council took place.

### The siege of Fushimi Castle
- Fushimi was held for Ieyasu by **Torii Mototada**, who died when it fell on **8/1 (8 September 1600)**. Sources give the siege as **10 or 11 days**. (S-Fushimi-search: summaries of reference pages) [est for the outcome; estim for the length]
- The stories of Mototada's farewell to Ieyasu, and of the "blood ceiling" boards later reused in Kyoto temples, are **[later]** traditions. Their sources were not checked in this session. [unver]

### Gifu Castle, Ōgaki and the approach
- The Eastern Tōkaidō vanguard (Fukushima, Ikeda Terumasa and others) took **Gifu Castle from Oda Hidenobu** (Nobunaga's grandson). (S-Fushimi-search) [est]
- The usual date for Gifu's fall is **8/23** [std, not confirmed in a search result].
- The Eastern vanguard then occupied the high ground of **Akasaka**, threatening **Ōgaki Castle**, where Ishida and the Western main body were. (S-Fushimi-search) [est]
- Takahashi Yōsuke shows that **Kobayakawa Hideaki and Kyōgoku Takatsugu were in touch with Ieyasu's envoys before 8/23**. (S-vegcale-Kobayakawa, citing S-Takahashi) [prim, as reported]
- **Ieyasu reached Okayama (later called Katsuyama) at Ōgaki on 9/14**, and on the morning of the 15th set up at Momokubari-yama. (S-kankou-Momokubari, Gifu Prefecture tourism) [est]
- A skirmish at the **Kuise River** on 9/14 is traditional [std]. Its details are [later].

### The night move from Ōgaki
- **Traditional view.** Ieyasu "lured" Ishida out of Ōgaki by letting it be known that he would march on Sawayama and Osaka. The Western army marched by night in rain to Sekigahara. [later / disp]
- **Shiramine and Takahashi** argue instead that the Ōgaki force left to rescue **Ōtani Yoshitsugu at Yamanaka**, once Kobayakawa's betrayal became clear. Their evidence is a draft letter by Kikkawa Hiroie saying in effect that "the Ōgaki men withdrew because Ōtani's camp at Yamanaka had become insecure". (S-agora-Shiramine) [prim: *Kikkawa Hiroie jihitsu shojō-an*, 9/17, as reported; disp]
- Takahashi, using **Itasaka Bokusai's *Keichō nenchū Bokusai-ki***, writes that Ishida, Ukita, Shimazu and Konishi left Ōgaki on the night of 9/14 and moved **towards Yamanaka in the rain**. (S-agora-Shiramine, S-gendai-Takahashi) [prim/near-contemporary memoir; Bokusai was Ieyasu's physician]
- The Shiramine/Takahashi reading of Kobayakawa depends partly on the Inaba Masanari entry in the *Kan'ei shoka keizuden*. Critics point out that this genealogy was written to honour the Inaba family and cannot be taken at face value. (S-agora-Shiramine) [disp]

### Tokugawa Hidetada at Ueda
- Ieyasu's son and heir **Hidetada** led the Tokugawa main force (**c. 38,000**) along the Nakasendō. He was held up at **Ueda Castle by Sanada Masayuki**, whose garrison sources give as **2,000 to 2,500**. (S-Ueda-search) [est for the outcome; estim for the numbers]
- He reached Ueda around 9/5 (Wikipedia-derived summary) and **arrived after the battle**. Bad weather and a delayed messenger are also blamed. (S-Ueda-search) [est for the outcome; estim/disp for the causes]
- Hidetada's late arrival, usually given as 9/20 [std], and Ieyasu's anger at him are traditional. Neither was confirmed in a search result. [unver]
- **Consequence.** The Tokugawa house's own main army missed the battle. The fighting was largely done by **Toyotomi-vassal lords** (Fukushima, Kuroda, Hosokawa and others) and by Ieyasu's own corps. (S-Kasaya-review, S-bunshun-Kasaya) [est]

### Who led the West?
- **Traditional view.** Ishida Mitsunari was the Western commander-in-chief. Britannica and the guidebooks still describe the West as under his leadership. (S-Britannica, S-Turnbull-search) [later / disp]
- **Revised view.** The **nominal commander-in-chief (*sōdaishō*) was Mōri Terumoto**, an Elder, who sat in Osaka Castle with Hideyori and never came to the battlefield. Ukita Hideie was in effect deputy commander. Ishida was the organiser in the field but held no formal command over the great lords. (S-vegcale-Ishida summarising Mitsunari Junji; S-Kasaya-review) [est in current Japanese scholarship]
- **Takahashi Yōsuke** goes further. He argues that no source shows Ishida preparing the rising, and that the coalition was assembled by others. ("The ringleader of the West was not Ishida Mitsunari", S-gendai-Takahashi-2) [disp]
- How the Western high command actually made decisions (a council of Terumoto, Hideie and the commissioners, or Ishida in practice) is **not settled**. (S-vegcale-Ishida) [disp]

## 3. The battlefield

### The ground
- Sekigahara is a narrow basin in western **Mino** (Fuwa District, Gifu Prefecture), where the **Nakasendō** (from Ōgaki and Tarui in the east, to Ōmi in the west) meets the **Hokkoku Kaidō** (north-west towards Echizen) and the **Ise Kaidō** (south-east down the Makita valley). It is the site of the ancient **Fuwa barrier** (*seki*), which gave the place its name. [std]
- The **Fuji River** (藤川) runs north to south across the west side of the valley, between Ōtani's Yamanaka position and Matsuo-yama. [std]
- Older names: a letter by the court noble **Konoe Sakihisa**, written five days after the battle, and a letter by **Mogami Yoshiaki** dated 10/8 call the field **Aono-ga-hara** (青野カ原), not Sekigahara. The 1338 battle on the same ground was also "Aonohara". (S-Akita-076, S-Substack-dates) [prim: Konoe Sakihisa letter (Yōmei Bunko) and Mogami letter (Akita family papers), as reported]
- Turnbull warns that the familiar "valley of death" map, with Ieyasu walking into a horseshoe of Western armies on the hills, descends from the 1893 General Staff map, which Shiramine criticises on many points. (S-Turnbull) [disp]

## 4. The battle, hour by hour

### What the contemporary documents support (the established outline)
- On 9/15 the Western forces that had left Ōgaki overnight fought the Eastern army at Sekigahara/Yamanaka, and were **routed the same day**. [est]
- A **letter co-signed by Ishikawa Yasumichi and Hikosaka Motomasa to Matsudaira Ienori, dated 9/17** (two days after the battle), says that **Ieyasu engaged at Sekigahara at about 10 a.m. (the hour of the snake, 巳刻)**. Takahashi and others read it as saying that Kobayakawa, Wakisaka and Ogawa "betrayed" the West **as the fighting began**, and that the West was beaten **at once**. (S-president-75298, S-vegcale-Kobayakawa) [prim, as reported; the exact wording not seen]
- **Ieyasu's own letter of 9/15 to Date Masamune**, written that day, is reported as placing the fight "in the mountains" in Mino and not naming Sekigahara. (S-Substack-dates) [prim, as reported]
- The often-quoted wording "at the hour of the horse (noon) today, the 15th, at Yamanaka in Nōshū we gave battle" (今十五日午刻、於濃州山中及一戦) **could not be confirmed** in any search result. [unver; check in *Tokugawa Ieyasu monjo no kenkyū* or *Date-ke monjo*]
- **Kikkawa Hiroie's draft letter of 9/17** reports that the Ōgaki men withdrew because Ōtani's camp at Yamanaka had become insecure, and that Kobayakawa's betrayal had become clear early. (S-agora-Shiramine, S-Ishikawa-search) [prim, as reported]
- A Tokugawa-side source quoted by the US National Park Service describes gun smoke blotting out the sun. The manuscript is not named. (S-NPS) [unver]
- **Few detailed primary sources survive**: there is "no very detailed primary source, letters or diaries" for the course of the battle. (S-nippon-fr) [est]
- The **Tokitsune-kyōki** (言経卿記, diary of the courtier Yamashina Tokitsune), the *Tokiyoshi-kyōki* (時慶記) and the *Gien Jugō nikki* were searched for, but **no entry on the battle could be found or confirmed**. They record news reaching Kyoto, not the fighting itself. [unver]
- No Jesuit account of the battle was found either. Father **Francesco Pasio's annual letter of 1601** was written in the wake of the battle and describes Ieyasu's reprisals against the defeated lords (S-Jesuit-search). Its account of the battle itself was not seen. [prim exists; contents unver]

### When did Kobayakawa turn? (the toi-zutsu question)
- **Traditional view (Edo chronicles, the 1893 General Staff account, most popular books).** Kobayakawa hesitated until about noon. Ieyasu's "probing shots" forced him to decide. [later]
- **Kasaya Kazuhiko** still argues the toi-zutsu happened, as a threatening volley. He reasons that, with the East already winning, such a provocation looks reckless, so Ieyasu must have fired to avoid a greater risk (*Ronsō Sekigahara kassen*; *Sensō no Nihonshi 17*). (S-vegcale-Kobayakawa, S-Kasaya-search) [disp]
- **Shiramine Jun** (2014, *Shin-kaishaku Sekigahara kassen no shinjitsu*; 2019) and **Mitsunari Junji** hold that the toi-zutsu is a **later invention**. They argue that Kobayakawa had committed to the East **before the battle**: he occupied Matsuo-yama on 9/14 and drove out its Western garrison (Itō Morimasa). Their evidence is the **9/17 Ishikawa–Hikosaka letter** and Kikkawa's letter, which they read as saying that he **attacked Ōtani as soon as the fighting began**. Mitsunari adds that the battle started by accident before Kobayakawa had declared himself. (S-vegcale-Kobayakawa, S-agora-Shiramine, S-gendai-Shiramine-2019, S-Jcastle-Matsuo) [disp]
- **Takahashi Yōsuke**, *Shin Sekigahara* (Kōdansha gendai shinsho), says plainly "Kobayakawa did not betray *during* the battle". The deal was made in negotiation beforehand. (S-gendai-Takahashi, S-vegcale-Kobayakawa) [disp]
- **Goza Yūichi** takes Kobayakawa's hesitation and the dramatic scene to be embellishment from pro-Tokugawa historiography. (Wikipedia summary in S-Kobayakawa-search) [disp]
- **Stephen Turnbull** (Osprey blog, 2019) follows Shiramine. He says the earliest accounts place the "treachery" at the start of the battle, calls the probing shots "a complete myth", and notes that this **"shrinks the whole process of the battle by several hours"**. (S-Turnbull) [disp]
- Another argument against the story is distance: Matsuo-yama is far from Ieyasu's HQ for a volley to have been aimed at it. (S-tabi-Matsuo) [disp]
- **Watanabe Daimon**, author of *Sekigahara kassen zenshi 1582–1615*: his position on the toi-zutsu **could not be confirmed** in this session. Summaries quote him only on Kobayakawa's earlier career, calling the story that Ishida slandered Kobayakawa out of Chikuzen "a plain error". (S-vegcale-Kobayakawa, S-NDL-Watanabe) [unver for the toi-zutsu]
- **The toi-zutsu appears only in compilations made from the mid-Edo period on**, more than half a century after the battle, and in no contemporary source. (S-vegcale-Kobayakawa) [est as a statement about the sources]
- **Recommendation for the site.** Tell the toi-zutsu as "the famous story", then explain that letters written days after the battle suggest Kobayakawa attacked early, perhaps right at the start.

### How long did it last?
- **Traditional:** from about 8 a.m. to 2 to 4 p.m., about six hours, with the turning point around noon. (S-bunshun, S-serai-end, S-duration-JP) [later]
- **Revised:** if the Ishikawa–Hikosaka letter is right that fighting began about 10 a.m. and the defections came at the start, the decisive fighting was much shorter, "several hours shorter" in Turnbull's words. (S-Turnbull, S-president-75298) [disp]
- A popular counter-argument says "Sekigahara was a surprisingly short battle" is a myth, and gives 4 to 5 hours of fighting. (S-duration-EN) [disp]
- This session could not confirm the precise claim, sometimes repeated, that the fight was over in "two hours". [unver]

### The Shimazu breakout
- Shimazu Yoshihiro's force **did almost no fighting in the main battle**, which is the standard view in research. (S-Shimazu-JP) [est]
- When the West collapsed, Yoshihiro broke **forward through the Eastern army** instead of fleeing west, then turned south-east onto the **Ise road**. (S-Senganen, S-Shimazu-search) [est]
- **Sutegamari** (捨て奸): small rear-guard groups sat down and fought to the death, one after another, to delay the pursuit. The Senganen (Shimadzu family) site says it is "commonly believed" to have been used, but that **"contemporary accounts paint a slightly different picture"**: the fighting was too heavy for an orderly sutegamari. (S-Senganen) [disp]
- **Shimazu Toyohisa**, Yoshihiro's nephew, was killed as rear-guard commander. He is given as 28 or 31 years old in different sources. (S-Gifu-library-Toyohisa, S-Shimazu-JP) [est for the death; disp for the age]
- His death place is marked at the Shimazu mound in Ōgaki/Kamiishizu (S-kankou-Shimazuzuka) [est as a local tradition].
- **Ii Naomasa** was wounded in the pursuit, traditionally by a Shimazu gunshot, and **died in 1602**. It is often said the wound contributed to his death. [std; not confirmed in a search result; the cause of death is disp]
- **Numbers:** of **c. 1,500** men, **"fewer than 80"** came home with Yoshihiro (Senganen). Other versions: c. 300 at the start of the breakout, 80 at the end (samurai-emblem); "a few dozen" (Japanese summaries). (S-Senganen, S-Shimazu-search, S-Shimazu-JP) [estim]
- Yoshihiro picked up his wife and Tadatsune's wife at Osaka (Senganen: "freed … from Osaka Castle"; other summaries: at Sumiyoshi) and **reached Satsuma by sea**. Senganen says the journey home took **19 days**. (S-Senganen) [est for the outcome; estim for the details]

## 5. Casualties and aftermath

### Casualties
- **No reliable count exists.** [est]
- Britannica: **up to 32,000** Western dead; Tokugawa losses unknown but much smaller. (S-Britannica) [estim]
- The Wikipedia infobox, as mirrored: East **4,000 to 10,000** killed, West **8,000 to 32,000** killed, plus about **23,000 defected**. (S-strength-search) [estim; wide]
- Gifu Prefecture says the number buried in the head mounds is **"not certain"**. When the Tōkaidō railway was built in the Meiji period, large quantities of bones were found at the West Head Mound. (S-kubizuka) [est]

### Ishida, Konishi and Ankokuji
- All three were captured after fleeing, paraded, and **beheaded at Rokujō-gawara, Kyoto, on Keichō 5/10/1 (6 November 1600)**. Their heads were displayed at the **Sanjō bridge**. (S-execution-search: Samurai Archives gives 10/1; others give 6 November) [est]
- One summary dates Konishi's death to "October 27". This conflicts with the other sources and is probably a calendar mix-up. [disp, minor]
- **Konishi**, a Christian, is said to have **refused suicide** for religious reasons and was therefore executed. (S-execution-search) [est as the standard account]
- Ishida's capture in the Ibuki hills by **Tanaka Yoshimasa**'s men, and the story that on the way to execution he refused a persimmon as "bad for the phlegm" (*tan no doku*), are **[later]** anecdotes. Neither was checked in a source this session. [unver]

### Redistribution of domains
- **Kaieki** (confiscation) of **about 88 houses** that sided with the West. One summary says 248 houses lost their status in all from Sekigahara through the Edo period. (S-aftermath-search) [estim]
- Total land moved or confiscated: **6.3 million koku**, "about a third of the realm" (one source); **"over 6 million koku"** (another). The figure of 4.16 million koku sometimes given was **not found**. (S-aftermath-search, S-aftermath-JP) [estim, disp]
- **Mōri Terumoto:** cut from **c. 1.2 million koku** (eight provinces, 1,205,000) to two provinces, Suō and Nagato. The new figure is given as **298,000** in one source and **"just under 370,000"** in another. The difference is probably between the official assessment and later reassessment (not confirmed). (S-aftermath-search, S-aftermath-JP) [disp]
- **Mōri–Kikkawa rift:** Kikkawa's dealings with the East left a feud between the Mōri main house and the Kikkawa house that **lasted to the end of the Edo period**. (S-kankou-Mori) [est as the local account]
- Ii and Honda had sworn written oaths (*kishōmon*) promising to leave the Mōri domain intact. Terumoto thanked them, and on 9/22 swore to hand over the Osaka West Enceinte. On 9/25 Ikeda, Fukushima, Kuroda, Asano and Tōdō jointly swore that the Ii–Honda promise was genuine. (S-Hiroshima-castle) [prim, as reported] The promise was then largely broken, and the domain was cut. [est]
- **Uesugi Kagekatsu:** cut from about 1.2 million koku at Aizu to about **300,000 at Yonezawa**. Some sources give the earlier figure as 1 million. (S-aftermath-search) [est for the result; disp for the starting figure]
- **Ukita Hideie:** lost Bizen, Bitchū and Mimasaka (**574,000 koku**). He was spared death at the plea of Maeda Toshinaga and others, **exiled to Hachijō-jima**, and died there in **1655** aged 84 after about 50 years. (S-aftermath-JP) [est]
- **Fukushima Masanori:** moved to **Aki (Hiroshima), 495,000 koku**. **Ikeda Terumasa:** moved to **Harima (Himeji), 520,000 koku**. Their old lands went to Tokugawa kin and hereditary vassals. (S-aftermath-JP) [est]
- **Tozama and fudai:** after the battle, lords who had been Ieyasu's equals or allies (**tozama**, "outside" lords) generally got larger but more distant domains. Hereditary Tokugawa vassals (**fudai**) received the strategic lands around Edo, Kyoto and the main roads. [std; the general pattern is est, not confirmed in a search result]
- **Kasaya's point:** the battle **did not by itself settle** the contest between East and West. The Toyotomi house survived in Osaka with Hideyori. (S-Kasaya-review) [est as an interpretation]

### Later events
- Ieyasu was appointed **sei-i taishōgun in 1603** (Keichō 8/2/12). [std]
- **Osaka campaigns: Winter 1614, Summer 1615**. The Toyotomi house was destroyed in 1615. [std]
- The Tokugawa shogunate lasted until **1868**. Britannica calls the battle the event that "set up the machinery" of that shogunate. (S-Britannica) [est]
- **Long-term memory:** the Mōri (Chōshū) and Shimazu (Satsuma) domains, both on the losing side in 1600, led the overthrow of the shogunate in 1867–68. A popular story says the grudge was kept alive for 250 years. [std for the fact; the "grudge" story is later tradition, not checked here]

## 6. Weapons and tactics

All [std] unless sourced; this session found little specific material.

- **Arquebuses (teppō).** Matchlock guns, introduced in the 1540s, were by 1600 carried in large numbers by ashigaru in every army. The East's opening is traditionally an exchange of gunfire (the Ii volley at Ukita). The Ishikawa–Hikosaka letter and Ieyasu's letter speak only of "fighting", not of gunfire. [std; gunfire in the opening is later]
- **"Smoke blotted out the sunlight"**, quoted by the NPS from an unnamed manuscript. (S-NPS) [unver]
- **Spears (yari).** Long pike-spears for ashigaru formations, shorter spears for mounted and foot samurai. [std]
- **Honda Tadakatsu's spear "Tonbogiri"** ("dragonfly-cutter") and his **deer-antler helmet** are famous. [std, later tradition]
- **Mounted samurai.** Rode with retainers on foot. Large-scale cavalry charges are debated by historians, and many samurai fought dismounted. [std; disp]
- **Ashigaru.** Foot soldiers armed with guns, bows and spears. They were the bulk of every army. [std]
- **Sashimono.** Small banners worn on a frame on the back, in the lord's colours and crest, to identify units. **Nobori** were tall unit banners. **Uma-jirushi** were commanders' standards. [std]
- **Ii "Red Devils" (*aka-zonae*).** The Ii contingent wore red-lacquered armour throughout. It is a feature of the Hikone-type screens. (S-screens-JP: Noda Hiroko's study of the Hikone screen) [est]
- **Field fortification.** Ishida's position on Sasao-yama had palisades (*saku*). Today's reconstruction is modern. (S-Japan-Travel / Gifu tour pages) [est that a fortified camp existed; details later]
- **Ōtani's earthworks.** The Ōtani position was built on the steep slope above Wakamiya Hachiman **about a month before the battle**. (S-tabi-Otani) [est as a site tradition]
- **Matsuoyama Castle** was a real mountain castle. Baileys (*kuruwa*), ditches and earth ramparts survive. (S-tabi-Matsuo) [est]

## 7. Stories and people for narration

Each item notes whether it is history or legend.

1. **The fog.** The armies wait blind in a fog-filled valley. **[later]** It is in every popular account, but no primary source for it was found here. Narrate it as "tradition says".
2. **Ii Naomasa jumps the start** to win the first blow for his son-in-law Tadayoshi, and Fukushima is furious. **[later]** (S-tabi-Ii) It is told on the battlefield's own signs.

## 9. Disagreements between sources, and what is not known

1. **When Kobayakawa turned.** At noon after Ieyasu's warning shots (Edo tradition; Kasaya still defends the shots), or at the start of the battle (Shiramine, Mitsunari, Takahashi, Turnbull, Goza, based on the 9/17 letters)? Or was he committed even earlier, on 9/14, when he seized Matsuo-yama? **Disputed; the evidence leans towards early.**
2. **Start time and length.** About 8 a.m. (tradition; Goza) or about 10 a.m. (Ishikawa–Hikosaka letter)? Six hours, or much less? The end is given variously as 2, 3 or 4 p.m. **Disputed.**
3. **Where the fight happened.** Contemporary letters say "Aono-ga-hara" or "Yamanaka" and do not use "Sekigahara". Shiramine and Takahashi place the decisive fighting around **Yamanaka** and Ōtani's position. They read the Ōgaki march as a rescue of Ōtani, not as falling into Ieyasu's trap. Shiramine also proposes a new location for Ōtani's camp. The classic positions map comes from the 1893 General Staff and is questioned. **Disputed.**
4. **Who led the West.** Mōri Terumoto was the nominal commander-in-chief, with Ukita as deputy. How much Ishida directed things is open, and Takahashi denies he was even the ringleader. **Ishida as "commander-in-chief" is the traditional view; current scholarship rejects it in form and debates it in substance.**
5. **The Oyama council.** Did it happen on 7/25 (Honda Takashige; and Kasaya, though he gives it a different meaning), or is it fiction (Shiramine)? **Disputed.**
6. **Troop numbers.** 75,000 against 120,000 (traditional paper strengths) or roughly equal 80,000-odd per side (other estimates). All contingent figures go back to Edo lists and the 1893 General Staff work. A summary mentions a minority claim of only about 30,000 in all, without naming its author. **Unknown in detail.**
7. **Casualties.** From 5,000 to 32,000 dead, with no reliable count. **Unknown.**
8. **Fog, the lunch box, the persimmon, Ōtani's curse, the helmet cords.** These are traditions with no contemporary support found. The helmet-cords saying is very likely misattributed to Ieyasu.
9. **Sutegamari.** The Shimadzu family's own site says contemporary accounts differ from the classic rear-guard picture. **Disputed in detail.**
10. **Mōri figures after the battle.** 298,000 or c. 369,000 koku. Total land confiscated: 6.3 million koku, or other figures. **Varies by source.**
11. **Kyoto diaries and Jesuit letters.** The *Tokitsune-kyōki*, *Tokiyoshi-kyōki* and Pasio's 1601 annual letter exist, but **their Sekigahara entries were not read or confirmed** in this session.
12. **Watanabe Daimon.** His views on the toi-zutsu, troop numbers and duration **could not be confirmed**. His book *Sekigahara kassen zenshi 1582–1615* (Sōshisha) should be checked directly.
13. **Coordinates.** Only Sasao-yama, Matsuo-yama, the Ōtani/Yamanaka area, the museum and a general battlefield point have a coordinate from a source, and two of these rest on a single source each. The rest have only addresses. **Geocode the Gifu Prefecture addresses on the GSI map before drawing the game map.**
