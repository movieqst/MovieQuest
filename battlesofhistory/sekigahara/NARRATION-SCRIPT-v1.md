## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Sekigahara, fought on the 21st of October 1600,
in central Japan, between the Eastern Army of Tokugawa Ieyasu and the Western Army raised in the name of the young heir
of Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

## Introduction

### 1. BATTLE OF SEKIGAHARA
- On a wet autumn day in 1600, in a narrow valley where three roads meet, the largest armies Japan had ever seen fought for control of the country. By evening, Tokugawa Ieyasu was the most powerful man in Japan. *(established)*
- The famous story of this battle was written down long afterwards. This introduction separates what the letters of the time say from what later chronicles added. Watch the colour of each tag. *(reconstruction)*

### 2. After Hideyoshi
- Toyotomi Hideyoshi had united Japan after a century of civil war. When he died in 1598, his son Hideyori was five years old. *(established)*
- Hideyoshi left the country in the hands of a council: five great lords, the Elders, and below them five Commissioners. The strongest of the Elders was Tokugawa Ieyasu. *(established)*
- Within a year the council was breaking apart. Ieyasu moved into Osaka Castle and began to act as the ruler of the country. *(established)*

### 3. Two coalitions
- In the summer of 1600 Ieyasu marched north-east against a rival lord, Uesugi Kagekatsu. While he was away, his enemies rose in the west. *(established)*
- The traditional story makes the Commissioner Ishida Mitsunari the leader of the West. Historians today point out that its official commander was the great lord Mōri Terumoto, who stayed in Osaka Castle with the boy Hideyori. Ishida was its organiser in the field. *(established; who really led the West, disputed)*
- Many of Hideyoshi's own generals sided with Ieyasu. This was not a simple war of loyal men against traitors: Toyotomi followers fought on both sides. *(established)*

### 4. The road to Sekigahara
- The West took Fushimi Castle near Kyoto after a long siege. Its commander, Torii Mototada, died defending it for Ieyasu. *(established)*
- The East took Gifu Castle and advanced on Ōgaki, where the Western army was gathered. *(established)*
- Ieyasu's son and heir, Hidetada, was leading the main Tokugawa army along the mountain road. He was held up besieging Ueda Castle, and arrived after the battle was over. *(established)*

### 5. The valley
- Sekigahara means "the plain of the barrier". It is a small basin, a few kilometres across, where the great road through the mountains meets the roads to the north and to Ise. *(established)*
- Around it stand low hills: Mount Sasao, Mount Matsuo, Mount Nangū. Whoever held them looked down on the road. *(established)*
- The letters written just after the battle do not even call it Sekigahara: one names the field Aonogahara, another Yamanaka, the village at its western end. *(letter)*

### 6. The armies
- No one counted the armies. The traditional figures are about seventy-five thousand men for the East and over a hundred thousand for the West. Many on the Western side never fought. *(estimate)*
- They fought with long spears, bows, swords and matchlock guns, which had come to Japan from Portuguese traders sixty years before. Each lord's men carried his own banners and crests. *(established)*
- The positions on our map follow the traditional plan of the battle, first drawn in 1893. Historians now question parts of it. *(reconstruction)*

## The reenactment (20 to 21 October 1600)
All the chapters play on the battle map of the valley. The dates are Gregorian; the Japanese calendar of the time gives the 14th and 15th day of the 9th month.

1. **The night march** · Night, 20 October
   In the rain, the Western army leaves Ōgaki and marches west through the dark to Sekigahara. Why it left is debated: the old story says Ieyasu lured it out; some historians now think it went to help the Western lord Ōtani Yoshitsugu, whose camp at Yamanaka was in danger. *(established; the reason, disputed)*

2. **Matsuo-yama** · 20 October
   The day before, Kobayakawa Hideaki, a young Western lord with the largest army in the valley, takes the hill fort on Mount Matsuo and turns out its garrison. He has already been talking secretly with Ieyasu's side. *(letter; his intentions, disputed)*

3. **The East follows** · Night, 20 October
   The Eastern army follows. By dawn its lords stand in the valley, facing the Western camps on the hills, with Ieyasu on a low hill behind them. *(established; the exact positions, reconstruction)*

4. **The battle begins** · About ten in the morning
   A letter written two days later says the fighting began at about ten in the morning. Fukushima Masanori's men attack Ukita Hideie, the strongest Western lord in the field, while other Eastern lords attack Ishida on Mount Sasao. *(letter)*

5. **Kobayakawa attacks** · Morning
   The same letter says that Kobayakawa, and the lords below his hill, turned against the West as the fighting began. Kobayakawa's army comes down from Mount Matsuo and falls on Ōtani Yoshitsugu. *(letter; the timing, disputed)*

6. **Ōtani** · During the fighting
   Ōtani, who had expected treachery, holds for a time. Then four smaller lords beside him, Wakisaka, Kutsuki, Ogawa and Akaza, turn on him too. His army is destroyed, and Ōtani takes his own life. *(established)*

7. **The army that did not move** · During the fighting
   On Mount Nangū, behind the Eastern army, the great Mōri army does not come down. Its vanguard commander, Kikkawa Hiroie, has a secret agreement with the East, and blocks the road. The lords behind him never fight. *(established)*

8. **The West breaks** · During the fighting
   With its western flank gone, the Western line falls apart: Konishi's men, then Ukita's, then Ishida's. Their commanders flee into the mountains. *(established; the order, later account)*

9. **The famous story** · Tradition
   Tradition tells it differently. A thick fog fills the valley at dawn. Ii Naomasa slips past Fukushima's men to fire the first shot. For hours the battle hangs in the balance, while Kobayakawa watches from his hill. At noon Ieyasu loses patience and has his guns fire at Mount Matsuo, and only then does Kobayakawa come down. On Mount Nangū, the Mōri messengers are told the army is eating its lunch. These stories were written down in the Edo period, long after the battle; the letters of the time do not mention them. *(later account)*

10. **The Shimazu breakout** · After the collapse
    The Shimazu, from the far south of Japan, had stood aside from the fighting. Now, surrounded, Shimazu Yoshihiro does not flee west. He breaks forward, straight through the Eastern army, and escapes down the road to Ise. His nephew is killed covering the retreat; of about fifteen hundred men, only a few dozen reach home. *(established; the numbers, estimate)*

11. **Ieyasu on the field** · After the fighting
    By the afternoon the battle is over. Ieyasu views the heads of the enemy dead, as was the custom, and orders the dead buried in two great mounds, which still stand in the valley. *(established)*

12. **Ishida** · Days later
    Ishida Mitsunari is caught hiding in the mountains. With Konishi Yukinaga and the monk Ankokuji Ekei, he is paraded through Kyoto and beheaded on the 6th of November. *(established)*

## The outcome
- Ieyasu won a complete victory. No reliable count of the dead survives; estimates run from several thousand to over thirty thousand. *(estimate)*
- Ieyasu took the lands of about eighty-eight lords who had fought against him, and cut down others. The Mōri, whose army never fought, lost most of their domain. *(established; the figures, estimate)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- In 1603 the Emperor named Ieyasu shōgun. His family would rule Japan from Edo, the city that became Tokyo, for more than two hundred and sixty years. *(established)*
- The Toyotomi house survived in Osaka until Ieyasu destroyed it in 1615. *(established)*
- The Shimazu and the Mōri never forgot Sekigahara. More than two and a half centuries later, their descendants led the movement that overthrew the Tokugawa in 1868. *(established; the link to Sekigahara, later account)*
- Historians still argue about the battle: how long it lasted, when Kobayakawa turned, and who really led the West. The letters written in its first days are shorter, and stranger, than the famous story. *(disputed)*

## Notes for review
- **The date.** 21 October 1600 is the Gregorian date of Keichō 5, 9th month, 15th day. One Britannica page says 20 October; every other source gives the 21st.
- **The 1868 line** (Satsuma/Chōshū and Sekigahara) is a well-known theme. The link is a later interpretation, so it is tagged that way. It can be cut.
- **Ishida's persimmon story and "After victory, tighten your helmet cords" are left out.** The second is credited to Hōjō Ujitsuna, not Ieyasu.
- **The head-viewing** is mentioned in one line, without detail.
- **The famous-story chapter (9)** gathers the fog, Ii's first shot, the noon volley and the lunch box, so the main reenactment can follow the letters.
- **For the Japanese version:** names will be written in kanji with readings checked. Times and dates will be read in Japanese style (慶長五年九月十五日).
