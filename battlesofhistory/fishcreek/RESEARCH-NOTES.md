# Fish Creek 1885: research notes

The Battle of Fish Creek, or Tourond's Coulee (Coulée des Tourond), was fought on Friday 24 April 1885 during the North-West Resistance (called the North-West Rebellion in older and official writing).

Written 7 November 2026 as a companion to `../batoche/RESEARCH-NOTES.md`. These are notes for the narration and the map, not page text.

## How to read these notes

**Tags.** Each claim carries one tag and one or more source IDs, which resolve to the bibliography at the end:
- **established**: most sources agree.
- **primary: X**: a participant or a document from 1885.
- **later account: X**: a memoir, oral or family history, a museum text or a later history.
- **disputed**: sources disagree. The versions are given side by side.
- **estimate**: a number or position worked out by us or by a source.

## 1. Before the battle

### Duck Lake to Clarke's Crossing
- **26 March 1885:** fighting broke out at Duck Lake. Less than a month later, soldiers were in action at Fish Creek. H89 counts this rapid move of troops on the unfinished CPR line as a real achievement. *(established; H89 p. 21)*
- **6 April:** Middleton left Fort Qu'Appelle for Batoche, about 200 miles north. He had western units, more infantry, artillery and cavalry from Winnipeg, and Boulton's Scouts from Russell, Manitoba. *(later account; ESK°, PC-OV°)*
- **Pace:** about 20 miles a day. They reached the Humboldt telegraph station after five days. *(later account; ESK°)*
- **Weather:** H89 reproduces the *Illustrated War News* print "'A' Battery in the Touchwood Hills — Stuck in a Snow-Bank" (18 April 1885). *(primary: Illustrated War News, in H89 p. 102, fig. 70)*
- **17 April:** the column stopped at Clarke's Crossing on the South Saskatchewan to wait for troops from the east. *(later account; ESK°)*
- **The split:** at Clarke's Crossing, Middleton divided his force, about 900 men by then, and advanced up both banks. *(established; ESK°, PC-OV°, H89 pp. 22, 107–109)*
- **Who commanded the west bank:**
  - Parks Canada's overview says Lord Melgund. *(later account; PC-OV°)*
  - H89 says the west-bank group was 370 men under Lieutenant-Colonel Montizambert. *(later account; H89 pp. 22, 109)*
  - The Saskatchewan encyclopedia's French's Scouts entry says the 10th Royal Grenadiers, the Winnipeg Field Battery and French's Scouts formed the west-bank column under Montizambert. *(later account; ESK-FS°)*
  - These versions may fit together: Melgund (chief of staff) went with the west-bank column and gave orders there, while Montizambert was its senior unit commander. This is **disputed** and is open question 1.
- **23 April:** the columns moved off from Clarke's Crossing, about 90 km (56 miles) south of Batoche. *(later account; WIKI° via search)*
- **That night:** Middleton's east-bank column camped at the **McIntosh farm**, about 10 km (6 miles) south of Fish Creek. Dumont's scouts reported it that night. *(later account; ATLAS°)*
- **The verdict on the split:** splitting the force on both banks is the decision Middleton was most criticised for. Critics argued that he would have done better at Fish Creek with Montizambert's 370 men beside him. Hildebrandt defends it with Callwell's *Small Wars*. He also concedes that Middleton misjudged whether each half could stand on its own. After Fish Creek the force stayed together. *(later account; H89 pp. 107, 109)*

### Dumont's plan and the argument with Riel
- **Dumont's way of fighting:** he wanted to harass the columns from wooded coulees and bluffs as they moved through unknown country. H89 says these tactics had "proven" themselves at Duck Lake and were "proven again at Fish Creek". Dumont deferred to Riel, however. *(later account; H89 p. 16)*
- **His proposals:** early attacks on Fort Carlton, Prince Albert and Battleford for supplies. Scouts on both banks would find the columns, and small groups under captains, organised as for the buffalo hunt, would ambush them where they were weakest. *(later account; H89 p. 91)*
- **Dumont's own words** (as given in H89, from his 1889 dictated account):
  - "we were then 350 men all told, of whom 200 were armed". He proposed to harass the troops by night and "above all prevent them from sleeping".
  - Riel refused. Riel said this was "too much like the Indians", and that they might fire on their French-Canadian friends.
  - Dumont "yielded to Riel's judgement", trusting "his faith and his prayers".
  - *(primary: Dumont, quoted in H89 pp. 91–92)* The 350/200 figure is for the whole Métis force, not Fish Creek.
- **Fish Creek as the exception:** Riel allowed only one guerrilla action against the Field Force, the one at Fish Creek. He did not allow harassment after it. *(later account; H89 p. 91)*
- **Leaving Batoche, 23 April:** Dumont set out with about 200 men, and Riel went with them. *(later account; WIKI° via search; H89 p. 22 gives "200 Métis" for the battle)*
- **Who turned back:**
  - One version: on news that police were advancing on Batoche from Prince Albert, Riel returned there with about 50 men. *(later account; WIKI° via search)*
  - Another: Riel stayed behind with 50 men from the start. *(later account; classroom resource°)*
  - A third: Dumont's brother **Édouard Dumont** was left to guard Batoche. *(later account; ATLAS°)*
  - So Riel went part of the way and then turned back with about 50 men after an alarm about the police. This is **disputed in detail**, and Dumont's own text should settle it (open question 2).
- **The ambush:** Dumont's scouts found Middleton camped at the McIntosh farm. The Métis chose to wait at Tourond's Coulee, where the trail dropped into the creek.
  - Dumont put about **130 men** in a hollow on the left bank of the creek and hid the horses in the woods.
  - He took **about 20 horsemen** further down the creek's path. The idea was to let the column into the coulee and then strike it.
  - *(later account; ATLAS°; Dumont via GEO°)*
- **Dawn reconnaissance:** Dumont said it was "daybreak" before they came in sight of Middleton at the McIntosh farm. *(primary: Dumont, quoted in GEO°; check against GDS)*

The Saskatchewan encyclopedia says French's Scouts were about two miles away on the left (west) bank when the fighting began. *(later account; ESK-FS°)*

**Guns.** The Field Force had four RML 9-pounders in all: two with "A" Battery and two with the Winnipeg Field Battery. Each had a 3,000-yard range and weighed close to a ton with its carriage. *(established; H89 p. 33)* That puts **two guns on each bank** at the start (estimate from the above). The Gatling did not arrive until the *Northcote* came on 5 May, so it was **not at Fish Creek**. *(established; H89 p. 30)*

**Arms.** Infantry had the single-shot Snider-Enfield. H89 judges it inferior to the Métis lever-action Winchesters, but says the 9-pounders had "no counterparts" on the Métis side. *(later account; H89 p. 21)* Most Métis did not have Winchesters (see below).

**The men.** H89 calls the volunteers "farmers, clerks and shopkeepers". Many had never fired a shot in anger, and some had never fired at all. Middleton doubted them privately and in telegrams to Caron, and his fears were "confirmed at Fish Creek … where the raw troops saw their first action". *(later account; H89 pp. 21–22)*

### The defenders
**Numbers.**

| figure | source |
|---|---|
| 200 Métis, entrenched | H89 p. 22 |
| about 200 rode out from Batoche | WIKI° |
| 150 Métis, Dakota and Cree | RTP° plaque, PC-NHS° |
| about 130 in the hollow plus about 20 with Dumont | ATLAS° |
| "less than 300" (Middleton's later wording) and "280" (a contemporary history) | quoted in search° |
| "fewer than sixty" Métis | WIKI° (Dumont article) |

By the end, about **47 remained** in the coulee *(ESK°, ATLAS°)*, or only **seven** with Gabriel when the reinforcements came *(ATLAS°)*.

All of this is **disputed**. For the narration we suggest: about 150–200 set the ambush, and only about 50 were left by afternoon, perhaps far fewer.

**Who they were.**
- Métis under Gabriel Dumont.
- **Dakota** from Whitecap's (Wapahaska's) band. A Whitecap Dakota source says Whitecap and Dakota men, his sons among them, had joined Métis heading for Batoche. *(later account; WD°)* The search did not show directly that Dakota fought at Fish Creek. H89's figure of about **20 Dakota with Whitecap** is for the whole campaign, from trial evidence. *(later account; H89 pp. 19–20)*
- Two Dakota killed at Fish Creek appear in Wikipedia-derived casualty boxes *(WIKI°; open question 5)*.
- **Cree:** the plaque and Parks Canada name Cree among the fighters *(RTP°, PC-NHS°)*. One summary says the guns drove "the Cree" away early, which limited their part. *(later account; social-media summary of WIKI°, weak)*

**Arms.** H89 says the Métis had lever-action Winchesters *(p. 21)*, but at Batoche only about 40 of 50–60 men had rifles, and "the rest had double-barrelled shotguns" *(H89 p. 72, for 12 May)*. The Gabriel Dumont Institute has a rusted 12-gauge double-barrelled shotgun found at Tourond's Coulee. *(later account; GDI-12979°)* Ammunition was short. Dumont said he set the grass on fire when he saw he had only seven cartridges left. *(primary: Dumont via GEO°; check against GDS)*

**Horses.** Dumont's men rode to the coulee, and the horses were tied up in the woods in the ravine. *(later account; ATLAS°; LG° Gazette despatch, via search)*

**Positions.** Concealed in the scrub and trees of "a small dog-legged coulee at a point where the Fish Creek Trail slopes down across the creek" *(H89 p. 22)*. Dumont had most of his men dig rifle pits there *(later account; search extract of a contemporary history°)*. H89 notes the Batoche approaches from Fish Creek were also lined with pits *(H89 p. 16)*.

### Melgund's crossing: the scow
- **Middleton's own account** (as quoted by Black, 1913): "The scow … unfortunately was not in proper position, having been sent early that morning to our camp for forage for the left column."
  - Melgund went down to the river. Although he "could not make out clearly what was said", he "rightly concluded that they were wanted to cross" and set about crossing at once.
  - Melgund "with the Tenth Grenadiers, under Captain Mason, crossed the river and were extended along to the right centre".
  - *(primary: Middleton, quoted in BLACK° ch. 23)*
- **Britannica:** the west-bank troops took most of a day to cross on a makeshift ferry and arrived too late for the fighting. *(later account; BRIT°)*
- **Port Hope history:** part of the second column was brought across during the battle but came too late to take much part. The rest of the 90th and the Winnipeg Battery then moved up to reinforce the Grenadiers, and the Winnipeg Battery shelled from the bluffs. *(later account; PHH°)*
- **Saskatchewan encyclopedia:** French's Scouts' column arrived after the battle was over. *(later account; ESK-FS°)*
- **Verdict:** **disputed**. Part of the west-bank force, at least some Grenadiers and Melgund, crossed by scow and was in the line in the afternoon. The rest crossed too late for the fighting. Whether the Winnipeg Field Battery's guns crossed and fired that day is unclear (open question 3). No source found gives the clock time of the crossing.
- **Where:** the Gabriel Dumont Institute holds a photo dated 28 September 1948 titled "Where Middleton's West-bank troops crossed S. Sask. River during Battle of Fish Creek" (GDI 04808°). Finding its view would fix the crossing point.

### Did the Tourond buildings burn? (sprite question)
**Short answer:** yes, the Tourond home was destroyed. The best evidence is that the soldiers' guns set it on fire or shelled it during the battle. A family account says the troops burned it.
1. **Peters's album caption: "Firing the first house at Fish Creek with 9 pounders."** A photograph taken by an "A" Battery officer during the battle shows the guns firing at a house. This strongly suggests a farm building was a target on 24 April. *(primary: Peters album I, LAC, Frederic Hatheway Peters fonds; GDI 04393°)*
2. **Parks Canada:** the Tourond homestead was destroyed in the battle, while the fighters sheltered in the coulee below. The site page asks visitors to find the spot "shelled into rubble by the Canadian's nine pounder cannon". *(later account; PC-NHS°, PC-site°)*
3. **Gabriel Dumont Institute, family history:** Madame Tourond "watched helplessly as the troops burned her home, slaughtered her livestock, and pillaged her family's possessions". The family clock was hidden from the soldiers. This does not say whether it happened on the 24th or during the two-week camp. *(later account: family / GDI-TT°)*
4. **Archaeology:** Parks Canada found the probable cellar of Madame Tourond's home, with artifacts, and the old Fish Creek Trail. *(later account; PC-MP°, HP°)*
5. **The 1885 Curzon lithograph** (our cover) shows smoke from a burning building. *(primary: 1885 print, LAC C-002425; artist's impression)*

**For the map:** the burning-farm sprite is justified. Show the house hit by the guns and burning from late morning. Footnote that the family remembers the soldiers burning it. Exactly when it caught fire, during the shelling or later, is **not settled** (open question 6).

## 7. Human stories (true and sourced; check the ° items)

1. **The cap.** A bullet went through Middleton's fur cap as he rode along the line. He later told it lightly, in the same tone as the bullet that hit his shaving mirror at Batoche. *(DCB-Mid°; H89 p. 66)*
2. **The aide and his horse.** Capt. Wise, sent back to hurry up the main body, had his horse shot under him from the bluffs. Then he was shot in the foot peering over the edge of the ravine. *(primary: Middleton's despatch°; Boulton via search°)*
    - Curzon's eyewitness panel, in our `images/record-incidents-1885.jpg`, panel 3: "Captain Wise, A.D.C., undertakes to avenge the death of his charger, and himself gets shot". *(primary: IWN 1885, after F. W. Curzon)*
    - The despatch says "hit", the caption says "death". The horse was probably killed. *(estimate)*
2b. **The first man killed: Private Alexander M. Ferguson, 90th Winnipeg Rifles.**
    - Curzon panel 2: "The death of Private Ferguson, 90th Batt., the first man shot dead". *(primary: IWN 1885)*
    - A period portrait caption calls him "the first Volunteer killed in the Battle of Fish Creek". *(primary: IWN caption, via GDI°)*
    - A soldier's letter written the next day says "Dr. Ferguson's son" was the first killed. *(primary: letter quoted in search extract°; source to identify)*
    - RWR lists Pte A. W. Ferguson as killed on 24 April. The Winnipeg tomb spells the name "A. M. Fergusson". *(RWR°, MHS-Vol°)*
    - "First man killed" is 1885 reporting, not something a modern history confirms. The "A" Battery gunners may have fallen at the same time. *(disputed in detail)*
    - "Funeral of Two Members of the 90th Battalion at Winnipeg" (*IWN*) probably shows two Fish Creek dead brought home. Who they were is not confirmed (GDI 05417/05418°).
3. **First time under fire.** Hildebrandt: the volunteers were clerks, farmers and shopkeepers, many of whom had never fired a shot; Fish Creek was their first action. *(H89 pp. 21–22)*
4. **The first battle photographs.** Capt. Peters photographed his own guns going into action at about 10:15, then soldiers crawling to the brink ("First at Fish Creek"), the "rush" where "A" Battery lost heavily, the place where the ponies were killed, "Sewing up the dead", "Some of our wounded" and the camp. *(primary: Peters album, LAC; GDI 04844, 04391, 04392, 04850, 04851°)*
5. **The horses.** The soldiers could see the tied horses better than the men in the pits, and about 55 were killed. For a people of the buffalo hunt it was a heavy loss, and it cut their mobility. *(LG°, ATLAS°, DCB-Dum°)*
6. **Seven cartridges and a grass fire.** Dumont, down to a handful of cartridges, lit the prairie so the wind carried fire and smoke onto the soldiers. *(primary: Dumont via GEO°; PC°)*
7. **The women at Batoche.** Marguerite Caron, wife and mother of men in the coulee, is said to have made Riel send the reinforcements. *(later account; ATLAS°)*
8. **Riel praying.** After the stalemate, Riel went into the woods at Batoche to pray. *(later account; MNO°)* Not the same scene as Patrice Tourond holding Riel's hand on 12 May (H89 p. 72); do not merge them.
9. **The widow's clock.** The Tourond family hid its clock from the soldiers. The clock survives in the Margaret Tourond-Townson collection. *(later account; GDI-TT°)*
10. **The burials.** The *Canadian Pictorial and Illustrated War News* of 9 May 1885 put on its cover soldiers standing before the graves of three comrades killed at Fish Creek ("Solemn Scene After the Battle of Fish Creek"). *(primary: IWN, GDI 05421/05422°)*
    - **What the print shows** (our copy, `images/record-solemn-scene-1885.jpg`, signed "W.D.B."): the Field Force burying its dead beside open graves, with a clergyman reading the service, a firing party, a band, and mounted scouts keeping watch behind. *(primary: 1885 print; an artist's composition made in Toronto from correspondents' sketches and reports, so the details are illustrative)*
    - **When:** no source found gives the date. The print appeared on 9 May. Baker was buried "beside his comrades" after he died on the morning of 26 April, which means the first dead were buried by 26 April (probably 25 April). *(estimate; MHS-Baker°)*
    - **Where:** at Middleton's camp about 1 km from the coulee. A gravestone for some of the militia dead survives on the camp and graves property. Parks Canada's 2007 plan treats that property as separate from the 36-ha battlefield. *(later account; PC-OV°, PC-MP°)*
    - **Who officiated:** **not found.** The print shows a clergyman, but the sources found name none. The Rev. Mr. Gordon, who held a service at Batoche on 10 May, only "arrived that afternoon from Winnipeg", so he cannot have been at Fish Creek on 25 April (H89 p. 66). Open question 12.
    - Baker of Boulton's was buried "beside his comrades" on 26 April. *(MHS-Baker°)*
    - A gravestone for some of the dead is still at the NHS. *(PC-OV°)*
    - Two of the 90th were taken home and buried at Winnipeg ("Funeral of Two Members of the 90th Battalion at Winnipeg", IWN; GDI 05417/05418°).
    - The Volunteer Monument in Winnipeg (1886) and the St. John's Cathedral cenotaph remember the 90th's dead. *(MHS-Vol°, VAC°)*
11. **The wounded's journey.** No steamer came until 5 May, so the wounded were jolted about 42 miles to Saskatoon in improvised ambulances and nursed in settlers' homes. *(BLACK°, MARR°)*
12. **A cold night.** Drizzle turned to sleet as the soldiers withdrew. *(ESK°)*
13. **A note on birch bark.** Capt. Drury wrote his casualty note of 29 April on birch bark. It survives in the RCA Museum. *(RCA°)*
14. **The first Permanent Force dead.** The three "A" Battery gunners were the first men of Canada's regular force killed in action. *(RCA°)*

## 8. Aftermath

**The halt.**
- Middleton stayed at Fish Creek about two weeks. He regrouped, drilled his recruits, and waited for the HBC steamer *Northcote* to bring reinforcements and supplies. *(established; H89 p. 22; RTP°)*
- Meanwhile he reconnoitred toward Gabriel's Crossing (5 May) and "Artillery practice after the battle at Fish Creek" was photographed. *(H89 pp. 23, 30; fig. 25, LAC C-3461)*
- **5 May:** the *Northcote* landed at Fish Creek with two companies of the Midland Battalion (80 men), Van Straubenzie and Capt. Howard with the Gatling. *(established; H89 pp. 29–30)*
- **7 May:** the force marched the 13 miles to Gabriel's Crossing. *(established; H89 pp. 31–33)* ESK° says 8 May; the 8th was the march from Gabriel's Crossing toward Batoche. Use 7 May.

**Why he stopped.**
- Fish Creek shook his confidence in his men. He doubted openly whether they could manoeuvre under fire. He had wounded to move and supplies to bring up.
- From then on the force stayed together, and he used zarebas at night.
- Hildebrandt defends the zareba with Callwell; Canadian critics wanted a quicker, face-to-face fight. *(later account; H89 pp. 22, 107–109)*

**Effect on Batoche.**
- On the Métis side, Riel allowed no more ambushes. Dumont later said it would have been easy to cut up the troops in the ravines and on the flanks between 26 April and 4 May, "And Riel never consented to let them do it". *(primary: Dumont, in H89 p. 30)*
- The Métis fell back on the entrenched village. Hildebrandt: "Fortunately for the Field Force, the guerilla tactics so effective at Fish Creek were not employed again." *(later account; H89 p. 79)*
- The lost horses and spent ammunition weakened Batoche's defence. *(DCB-Dum°)*

**How each side judged it.**
- **Métis:** a success. Hildebrandt ranks Duck Lake and Fish Creek as their "greatest military successes". *(H89 p. 92)*
- **Parks Canada:** a psychological victory for the Métis, with fewer losses and Middleton's advance delayed. *(HP°)*
- **Militia histories:** a check. Callwell, Stacey and Hildebrandt say Middleton "regained the initiative after Fish Creek". *(H89 p. 107)*
- **Wikipedia:** a Métis victory with the government advance halted. *(WIKI°)*
- **Middleton (before Batoche):** said the hunters were "only formidable when you play their game, 'bush fighting'". *(H89 p. 79, on Batoche; context only)*

**The site today.**
- Battle of Tourond's Coulee / Fish Creek National Historic Site: 36 ha along Fish Creek, about 25 km south of Batoche. *(PC-NHS°)*
- Renamed from "Battle of Fish Creek"; the dual name honours the Métis name. *(established)*
- Features: the probable cellar of the Tourond house, the old Fish Creek Trail, Middleton's camp, and a gravestone for some of the militia dead. A separate monument stands near the battle site. *(PC-MP°, HP°, PC-OV°)*
- Run with Batoche NHS, which the management plan suggests as the place to show Tourond-cellar artifacts. *(PC-MP°)*
- Parks Canada point: 52.53901, -106.152659.

## 13. Stanley, *The Birth of Western Canada* (1936; 1960 edition), pp. 352–359 (read from the site owner's screenshots, 7 November 2026)

Stanley wrote from the official records and Dumont's own account (his notes, not checked here). Use for facts only; his 1936 wording ("half-breed tactics" etc.) is not used.
- **The march:** Middleton halted at Clarke's Crossing on 17 April, about 40 miles from Batoche, after about 180 miles in eleven days; the Royal Grenadiers caught up there, bringing the column to about 800 men. Cold: "fearfully cold" nights on the march (p. 356). The force was then split, the second column taken across the river (p. 356).
- **Scouts:** the Métis followed Middleton's march through Indian and Métis scouts; one, Jérôme Henry, travelled with the troops as a government freighter (p. 356).
- **Dumont's plan and Riel:** Dumont's account (in French, p. 357): about 350 men in all, 200 of them armed; he proposed harassing the troops by night, which Riel refused as too savage ("trop sauvage"), saying the soldiers were not to be treated so. Riel feared weakening Batoche with the Mounted Police at Prince Albert, and Dumont had been wounded at Duck Lake: Riel's memorandum of 22 April (p. 357).
- **23 April:** Dumont set out with **about 200 Métis, Cree, Saulteaux and Sioux (Dakota)**; Riel went with them and held religious services at the halts. When news came that the Mounted Police were coming out of Prince Albert, Riel went back with **fifty men** (p. 358). [new: the Saulteaux]
- **The plan:** the next morning Dumont with **20 men** went to within half a mile of Middleton's camp; the **main body of 130** waited in the coulee of Fish Creek. The plan was to draw the troops into the coulee and fire on them from the trees. Dumont: "Je voulais les traiter comme on traite les buffles" — "I wanted to treat them the way you hunt buffalo" (p. 358). [confirms the 130 + 20]
- **24 April:** Middleton broke camp; Boulton's Scouts went ahead, Middleton and his staff with them. The scouts found the traces of camp fires; Dumont's advance party fired on them; Boulton's men dismounted and fired back, and Dumont's men fell back to the edge of the coulee. The defenders fired on the soldiers as they came over the rise. They tried to drive the troops back by setting the prairie on fire, "without success". The heavy fire, especially the artillery's, discouraged many; deserters were many, and **at the end only fifty-four men** were left (p. 359). The defenders held the coulee at the end of the day (p. 359).
- **Maxime Lépine** (p. 359, quoting Lépine): **"We prayed all the day, and I think prayer did more than the bullets."** A Métis voice for the narration (source: Stanley's note, to check).
- **Stanley's verdict:** indecisive; Middleton not defeated but no victory; the left wing across the river helpless; the attack on Batoche delayed over a fortnight (p. 359).
- **Forces of 1885 (p. 352):** the 90th Winnipeg 317, the 91st 452, the Winnipeg Light Infantry 327, the Winnipeg Field Battery 62, a cavalry corps 32; in all 5,334 soldiers plus 2,648 staff, transport, commissariat, medical and other corps (7,982), and about 500 Mounted Police; nine guns and two machine guns. (Mulvany has the Winnipeg battery at 52 and the 90th at 304 in Middleton's columns: different counts.)

- **More from pp. 350–365:** Middleton was ordered west on 23 March and reached Qu'Appelle by 2 April (p. 351). Eastern strengths (p. 351): "A" Battery, Quebec, 120; the Royal Grenadiers, Toronto, 265; the Infantry School 92. **After Fish Creek, Riel sent runners to Poundmaker and Big Bear** asking them to come to Batoche (p. 363). Riel's letter to Poundmaker (in French, p. 365) calls Fish Creek a "victory", says the volunteers fought "not only as brave men but as heroes", and asks for two hundred to three hundred men. A Métis view of the result for the aftermath, attributed to Riel's letter.

**For the narration:** add the Saulteaux to the defenders (Métis, Cree, Saulteaux and Dakota); Dumont's "the way you hunt buffalo" plan (intro 4 or event 2); Lépine's "We prayed all the day…" (outcome or event 14); "fifty-four at the end" beside "fewer than fifty" (event 9, disputed). Map p. 355 (the military columns, Bond) shows the march routes only, not the battlefield.

## 14. Black, *History of Saskatchewan and the Old North West* (1913), ch. XXIII "The Battle of Fish Creek" (read in full, 7 November 2026; electriccanadian.com/history/saskatchewan/sask/chapter23.htm; public domain)

Black quotes Boulton's *Reminiscences* (1886), Middleton's articles in the *United Service Magazine* and Dumont's own account. It settles several open points (1913 wording such as "Halfbreeds" and "rebels" is not used).
- **The scouts:** Boulton's men found a camp "not long vacated and the fires still smouldering", fires for about two hundred men. Boulton: "Left wheel, gallop" at thirty or forty mounted men by a bluff, who bolted into a ravine 150 yards away; "Halt! Dismount! Extend in skirmishing order and lie down"; "Fire away, boys, and lie close; never mind if you don't see anything." The defenders "would pop up from the ravine, take a snap shot, and disappear in an instant." Hit in turn: Captain Gardiner (twice), Langford (twice), Bruce, and D'Arcy Baker, mortally, in the chest. Captain Wise sent to bring up the main body; his horse shot. [primary: Boulton]
- **A First Nations fighter** came forward dancing and shouting his war cry, was shot and lay in the open all day. [Black; one account]
- **The guns:** Peters brought two guns into action; after a few rounds Middleton withdrew them, as they did little against fighters in cover. Later Drury took one gun with part of C Company across the ravine to the left to take the pits from behind: no effect; a gunner wounded; Boulton's horse shot under him. [Middleton]
- **The grass fire:** on the right of the Canadian line, under cover of the smoke, the defenders tried to drive the volunteers back; Middleton admitted the moment was serious. **Teamsters under Bedson beat out the flames.** [Middleton]
- **The bugler:** William Buchanan, "a little bugler of the Ninetieth", carried ammunition along the line calling in his "childish shrill voice": "Now, boys, who's for more cartridges?" [Middleton] — good for the narration (event 10).
- **The scow (settles the crossing):** it had been sent early to the camp for forage; it could barely hold sixty men; with no wire rope it was rowed with oars cut with axes, against the current, through deep mud, boulders and **blocks of ice**, between banks about **a hundred feet high**. **About 250 men and two guns of the Winnipeg Battery, with their wagons and horses, crossed without an accident** (Lord Melgund and Major Jarvis). Melgund, with Montizambert's agreement, made a wagon laager, moved down the river, saw someone on the far bank shouting and gesticulating, and guessed they were needed. [Middleton] — the Winnipeg guns DID cross.
- **The attack on the pits:** Captain Ruttan's party (gunners and the 90th), then Lieutenant Swinford's, with Lieutenant-Colonel Houghton and Captain Wise, went down into the ravine: three killed, five wounded, Swinford mortally. Wise was later shot in the ankle. [Middleton]
- **The end:** "By about 3 p.m., with the exception of an occasional shot from the pits, all firing had ceased." Captain Mason and others asked to rush the pits again; Middleton refused, writing that he "could not help having a feeling of admiration and respect for their stubborn defense when deserted by their comrades." [Middleton] — **a time and a striking quote for the narration.**
- **The band of the 90th** brought in the wounded under fire. Middleton: the men "had borne their baptism of fire well". [Middleton]
- **Casualties (Black):** under 400 engaged; ten killed or died of wounds, forty wounded. **Killed:** Gunner D. H. De Manolly, Gunner W. Cook, Privates A. W. Ferguson, James Hutchins, George Wheeler, William Ennis. **Died of wounds:** Lieutenant Charles Swinford, Corporal John Code, Private Arthur J. Watson, Trooper D'Arcy Baker. Middleton claimed 11 defenders killed or died, 18 wounded and three First Nations dead on the field; Black, from the Batoche parish register and the monument, says **four Métis were killed**. Defenders never more than **130**. [Black]
- **The wounded:** the *Northcote* arrived only on 5 May, so they were carried in improvised ambulances about **forty-two miles** to Saskatoon under Dr. Willoughby, escorted by Boulton's scouts. [Black] — confirms the outcome line.
- **Dumont's account (via Black):** set out on the evening of the 23rd with **two hundred**: Métis, a few French Canadians, Saulteaux, Cree and Sioux (Dakota). "Riel accompanied us, and in the halts he made us say our beads." Supper at **Roger Goulet's farm**; Noël Champagne and Morise Carrière brought word that the police might come by the Qu'Appelle road; Édouard Dumont, holding Batoche, wanted Gabriel or Riel back with thirty men; **Riel went back with fifty.** At daybreak Dumont and **Napoléon Nault** scouted to within half a mile of Middleton's camp at McIntosh's (4 a.m.). **130 men** in the coulee opposite Tourond's; Dumont with **twenty horsemen** for a second ambush on the flank, given up when he saw tracks his men had left on the trail. **At 7:30 his advance guard came under fire.** Many fled; Dumont rallied **sixty-two**: 47 in the main ravine, 15 with him in a side coulee; some say 45 remained. **Isidore Dumont started an old song of Napoleon the First, and all joined in the chorus.** Riel let no reinforcements go during the battle; towards evening **Édouard Dumont refused to stay and came with eighty horsemen**, when the fighting was nearly over. Dumont withdrew to Batoche **carrying his dead and wounded**. [Dumont, via Black]

**For the narration (draft 4, before recording):** first shots at **about 7:30** (Dumont) beside Parks Canada's "about 9"; Boulton's charge and "lie close"; the teamsters beating out the fire; the bugler Buchanan; the scow: 250 men and two guns, through mud and ice; Middleton's "admiration and respect for their stubborn defense"; "by about 3 p.m." firing ceased; the Napoleon song; Édouard's eighty (Dumont) beside Parks Canada's seventy; the dead and wounded carried home.
