## intro-1.mp3
*Intro screen 1: PEGASUS BRIDGE*

PEGASUS BRIDGE. Night of 5–6 June 1944 , Bénouville, Normandy. The first fight of D-Day, and one of the most precise operations of the war: six gliders, 181 men, two bridges, and sixteen minutes past midnight. The British side wrote it up in detail and the men told it for fifty years. The German side is thinner: a coastal division’s records and a few veterans. The tags show which is which.

## intro-2.mp3
*Intro screen 2: Why two bridges mattered*

Why two bridges mattered. The Allied landings of 6 June 1944 put five armies on the beaches of Normandy. The eastern end of the line was Sword Beach, and east of Sword ran two waterways side by side from Caen to the sea: the Caen Canal and the River Orne. The only crossings between Caen and the coast were two bridges on the road from Bénouville to Ranville, a few hundred metres apart. If the British held them, the German armour east of the Orne could not reach the beaches, and the airborne division dropped east of the river could be reinforced. If the Germans blew them, that division was cut off. The bridges had to be taken intact, by surprise, before the Germans could fire the demolition charges. That meant gliders, landing in silence, within yards of the targets, in the dark.

## intro-3.mp3
*Intro screen 3: The British force*

The British force. The task went to D Company of the 2nd Ox and Bucks, an airlanding battalion of the 6th Airborne Division, under Major John Howard: 139 of his own men, thirty sappers of the Royal Engineers, and twelve glider pilots of the Glider Pilot Regiment. 181 men in six Horsas, three for each bridge. The glider pilots were the key. Staff Sergeant Jim Wallwork, flying the lead glider, had to put thirty men down in a field the size of a football pitch, in the dark, beside a bridge, by stopwatch and compass alone. He did it to within fifty yards. Air Chief Marshal Leigh-Mallory called it the finest feat of flying of the war. Behind them came the 7th Parachute Battalion, dropping north of Ranville at 00:50 to take over the bridges, and, from the beach at dawn, Lord Lovat’s 1st Special Service Brigade of commandos, due to march the six miles from Sword and relieve them by noon. The men carried Sten guns, Bren guns, grenades and the PIAT, a spring-loaded anti-tank projector with a range of a hundred yards. Howard had one PIAT team per platoon and no artillery at all.

## intro-4.mp3
*Intro screen 4: The German garrison*

The German garrison. The bridges were guarded by about fifty men of the 716th Infantry Division, a coastal-defence formation of older men and conscripts from across occupied Europe, under Major Hans Schmidt, with a 50 mm anti-tank gun, machine guns in trenches, and the bridges wired for demolition. The explosives were not in place. The charges were stored nearby and were to be fitted when an attack was expected. Nobody expected one that night: the weather was bad, the sentries were two, and the garrison was asleep in the village. Ten miles away lay the 21st Panzer Division, the only armoured division near the coast, with more than a hundred Panzer IV tanks. Its orders were to counter-attack any landing, but it could not move without permission from above, and permission did not come for hours. How many Germans fought at the bridges in the night is not recorded. Our garrison and the tanks that came are a reconstruction from the two accounts.

## intro-5.mp3
*Intro screen 5: The ground*

The ground. The canal bridge was a steel lifting bridge, a bascule, with a counterweight tower at its west end, carrying the road from Bénouville on the west bank to the strip of land between the waterways. The river bridge, four hundred yards east, was a plain girder bridge to Ranville. Between the canal and the river lay flat marshy pasture cut by ditches and a pond: landing zone X, where three gliders were to land nose to the canal bridge. East of the river bridge lay landing zone Y. The villages of Bénouville and Le Port stood on the west bank, Ranville on the east. Our map is a reconstruction from the sketch maps in Howard’s report and the regimental history: one pixel to a metre, with the villages, the tower and the gun pit where the accounts put them.

## intro-6.mp3
*Intro screen 6: Deadstick*

Deadstick. The operation was named Deadstick, the pilots’ word for landing with no engine. The gliders cast off over the coast at 00:07 and came down through cloud, turning twice by stopwatch, to arrive out of the dark at ninety miles an hour, braked by a parachute and a skid. The first glider hit the ground at sixteen minutes past midnight, forty-seven yards from the canal bridge. The second and third followed within a minute. At the river bridge two of three arrived; the sixth, released in the wrong place, landed eight miles away on the wrong river. Choose how to see it: watch the night unfold as the two sides tell it, or take command of the bridges, or of the garrison and the tanks that must take them back.

## event-bridges-1.mp3
*The bridges, event 1: Deadstick*

Sixteen minutes past midnight. Out of the dark at ninety miles an hour, the first Horsa hits the ground, bounces, smashes through the wire and stops with its nose forty-seven yards from the canal bridge. The second and third land within a minute. Nobody on the bridge has heard a thing.

## explain-bridges-1.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 1: Deadstick*

The gliders were released at 6,000 feet over the coast and flew seven miles by stopwatch, making two timed turns in cloud. Wallwork, in the lead, aimed for the wire at the end of the field to stop the glider before the embankment: he hit it, and the two pilots went through the windscreen. Everyone aboard was stunned for a few seconds; some of the men thought they were dead.

## event-bridges-2.mp3
*The bridges, event 2: Over the bridge*

Brotheridge’s platoon is out of the wreck and running for the bridge. A sentry fires a flare; the platoon charges across, firing from the hip, grenades into the pillbox and the trenches. Brotheridge is hit by a burst from the machine gun at the far end and falls at the west bank. The bridge is taken in three minutes.

## explain-bridges-2.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 2: Over the bridge*

The plan was speed: no stealth after the landing, just the shortest run to the bridge before the sentries could reach the detonator. Corporal Bailey put a grenade through the slit of the pillbox; Lieutenant Wood’s platoon cleared the trenches on the east bank. Den Brotheridge, hit in the neck, died within the hour, the first Allied soldier killed by enemy fire on D-Day.

## event-bridges-3.mp3
*The bridges, event 3: Ham and Jam*

Four hundred yards east, two gliders have landed beside the river bridge. Fox’s platoon rushes it; a machine gun fires a few bursts and the crew run. Sweeney’s platoon arrives at the run from a field seven hundred yards short. The sappers report the demolition chambers empty. Howard’s radio sends the code words: Ham, the canal bridge; Jam, the river bridge. Both taken intact.

## explain-bridges-3.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 3: Ham and Jam*

The explosives were stored in a shed and had never been fitted to the bridges, against standing orders, because the garrison did not expect an attack. The sappers cut every wire they could find anyway. The sixth glider, Priday’s, had been cast off in the wrong place and landed on the River Dives, eight miles away; its platoon fought its way back over two days.

## event-bridges-4.mp3
*The bridges, event 4: The staff car*

A staff car and a motorcycle come down the road from Ranville at speed: Major Schmidt, commanding the bridge garrison, returning from an evening in the town. The British at the river bridge shoot up both. Schmidt is wounded and captured, and tells his captors in good English that they will all be thrown back into the sea.

## explain-bridges-4.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 4: The staff car*

Schmidt was the one German officer who knew the demolition plan. Captured within fifteen minutes of the landing, he could not raise the alarm or order the charges fitted. The German side had no commander at the bridges for the rest of the night; the counter-attacks that came were organised from outside.

## event-bridges-5.mp3
*The bridges, event 5: The drop*

Half an hour later the sky to the north-east fills with the 7th Parachute Battalion, dropping to take over the bridges. The drop is scattered by wind and flak; companies land miles apart and in the flooded fields. A third of the battalion will never reach the rendezvous.

## explain-bridges-5.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 5: The drop*

The 6th Airborne Division dropped east of the Orne to seal off the flank of the invasion. The 7th Battalion’s job was the bridges, and it got there, but with 200 men instead of 600, and without its mortars and machine guns, which had come down in the water. Howard’s company would have to hold longer than planned.

## event-bridges-6.mp3
*The bridges, event 6: Tanks*

Engines in Bénouville. Two tanks of the 21st Panzer Division come up the road from the south with infantry behind them, toward the T-junction a hundred yards from the bridge. Sergeant Thornton is in a ditch at the junction with the company’s last PIAT and two bombs. He lets the lead tank come to thirty yards and fires. It stops, burns, and its ammunition explodes for the next hour.

## explain-bridges-6.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 6: Tanks*

The PIAT was a spring-loaded tube that threw a three-pound bomb a hundred yards, and it was feared for its unreliability. Thornton later said he was shaking so much he was sure he would miss. The burning tank lit the crossroads and blocked the road; the second tank withdrew, and the Germans, hearing the explosions, believed the British had anti-tank guns. No tank tried the road again that night.

## event-bridges-7.mp3
*The bridges, event 7: Holding the west bank*

Howard’s platoons hold the crossroads, the café and the trenches on both banks. German patrols probe from Bénouville and Le Port and are driven off. The wounded are carried into the café, where the Gondrée family hand out the champagne they have kept buried since 1940.

## explain-bridges-7.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 7: Holding the west bank*

Howard had fewer than 150 men to hold two bridges and the approaches on both sides, with no artillery. He pulled the platoons into tight positions at the bridge ends, kept the PIAT at the crossroads, and waited for the paratroopers. The German response was piecemeal: no one above the garrison knew yet what had happened.

## event-bridges-8.mp3
*The bridges, event 8: The paratroopers arrive*

Lieutenant Colonel Pine-Coffin crosses the river bridge with what he has gathered of the 7th Battalion, about two hundred men, and takes over the position. A Company goes into Bénouville, B Company into Le Port. Howard’s company falls back to the bridges as the reserve.

## explain-bridges-8.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 8: The paratroopers arrive*

From this point the bridges were held by a battalion rather than a company, but a weak one: no mortars, no machine guns, few radios. The paratroopers’ task was to hold the west bank, the villages, so that the Germans could not reach the bridges with their guns. It was in the villages, not at the bridges, that most of the day’s fighting happened.

## event-bridges-9.mp3
*The bridges, event 9: Dawn attack*

First light. German infantry of the 21st Panzer Division attack Bénouville from the south with mortar fire, and press into the village. A Company of the paratroopers fights them house to house and holds. Snipers in the church tower at Le Port pick at anyone who moves on the bridge.

## explain-bridges-9.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 9: Dawn attack*

The 21st Panzer Division had finally been released, but its main body was sent west of the Orne toward the beaches, not at the bridges. What came to Bénouville was a battalion-sized attack supported by mortars, enough to press the paratroopers hard for several hours but not to break through to the bridge.

## event-bridges-10.mp3
*The bridges, event 10: The gunboat*

Two German gunboats come down the canal from Ouistreham, the leading one firing a 20 mm cannon at the bridge. A PIAT bomb from the east bank hits it below the wheelhouse; it slews into the bank and its crew are taken prisoner. The second boat turns back for the sea.

## explain-bridges-10.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 10: The gunboat*

The boats were from the naval base at Ouistreham, sent to find out what was happening at the bridge. It was the second time that morning the PIAT, the weapon nobody trusted, had decided a fight. The commandos landing on Sword at that moment were four hours away.

## event-bridges-11.mp3
*The bridges, event 11: The bomb*

A German fighter-bomber comes in low along the canal and drops a bomb on the canal bridge. It hits the tower, bounces off, and falls into the water without exploding. The bridge is not touched again from the air.

## explain-bridges-11.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 11: The bomb*

A hit would have cut the road to the beaches for the airborne division. The dud was pure luck, and both accounts record it. Through the morning the Germans tried the bridge with tanks, infantry, a gunboat, snipers and an aircraft, and every attempt was too small and too late.

## event-bridges-12.mp3
*The bridges, event 12: Noon*

The attacks on Bénouville slacken. The paratroopers are down to half strength in the village and have lost most of their officers, but the Germans have not reached the bridge. From the north comes the sound of the fighting at Sword, and then, faintly, bagpipes.

## explain-bridges-12.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 12: Noon*

Lovat’s brigade had landed at 08:40 and fought its way through Colleville and the gun batteries behind the beach. Lovat had promised Howard he would be at the bridge by noon. He was two minutes late, and apologised for it.

## event-bridges-13.mp3
*The bridges, event 13: The commandos*

At one o’clock Lord Lovat’s commandos come down the road from Le Port, green berets instead of helmets, Piper Bill Millin playing Blue Bonnets over the Border at their head. They cross the canal bridge under sniper fire at a walk, as Lovat ordered, losing several men on the span, and shake hands with the airborne on the far side.

## explain-bridges-13.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 13: The commandos*

Lovat marched his men across at the walk, pipes playing, as a point: the beach and the bridge were joined. The snipers in Le Port were still active and the crossing cost lives. Howard’s report records the relief at 13:00; Lovat’s own account says 13:02 and apologises for the delay.

## event-bridges-14.mp3
*The bridges, event 14: Relieved*

The commandos cross the river bridge to Ranville and the rest of the brigade follows through the afternoon. In the evening the Warwickshires of the 3rd Division arrive from the beach to take over the bridges, and Howard’s company marches to Ranville. It has lost two killed and fourteen wounded on the bridges. The 7th Battalion has lost sixty.

## explain-bridges-14.mp3
*The bridges, explain this moment 14: Relieved*

The bridges stayed in British hands for the rest of the campaign and the 6th Airborne Division held the flank east of the Orne for two months. The canal bridge was renamed Pegasus after the airborne badge; the river bridge, later, Horsa. Howard’s company fought on in Normandy until September, and lost many more.

## outcome-bridges.mp3
*The bridges: historical outcome (debrief)*

Both bridges were taken intact within a quarter of an hour of the landing and held until the commandos arrived at 13:00 and the 3rd Division in the evening. D Company lost two killed, Brotheridge and Lance-Corporal Greenhalgh, who drowned in the pond, and fourteen wounded in the assault. The 7th Parachute Battalion lost about sixty killed and wounded holding Bénouville and Le Port. German losses at the bridges are not recorded; the garrison was scattered, one tank and one gunboat destroyed, and the counter-attacks turned back. The 21st Panzer Division, the only armour near the coast, was held back for hours by the chain of command and then sent west of the Orne. The bridges kept its eastern half from the beaches. The canal bridge was renamed Pegasus Bridge in 1944 after the badge of the airborne forces. Howard, Wallwork and Lovat came back every June for the rest of their lives; the café is still run by the Gondrée family.
