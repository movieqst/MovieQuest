**The approach:**
- **One reenactment,** from May to November 1991. The first chapters play on the area map (Vinkovci to the Danube) and the town chapters on the town map.
- **Who did what is named by unit:** "the JNA" (the Yugoslav People's Army, the federal army), "the Territorial Defence", "volunteer groups", "Croatian forces", "the National Guard", "the police". The script never makes "the Serbs" or "the Croats" the actor.
- **The town "fell"** or was "captured". "Liberation" appears only as what one side said.
- **Every number says what it counts and who counted it.** Numbers from different counts are never added together.
- **No graphic detail.** The killings at Ovčara are told plainly in the debrief, as the court found them, with no pictures of victims.
- **Both sides' people are heard:** a Croatian woman in her cellar, a Serb woman of Vukovar, an MSF doctor, a Serbian reporter, and a JNA veteran.

**Tags** (shown after each line on the page):
- **established**: not seriously disputed; supported by the named source.
- **ICTY finding**: found by the judges of the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia. Accusations and witness statements are not findings.
- **Croatian account**: from a Croatian institution, historian, participant or newspaper.
- **Serbian account**: from a Serbian institution, participant, newspaper or the JNA. (A Serbian human-rights group is named as such.)
- **disputed**: sources disagree; the line says who says what, or uses wording true in every version.
- **estimate**: an approximate number, with what it counts.
- **reconstruction**: our own placing on the map of something no source draws exactly.

**Clocks.** Each chapter shows its date in 1991. Where sources give different dates, the clock shows a span ("early November").

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Vukovar, fought in the autumn of 1991 in a town on the
river Danube, in eastern Croatia. On one side were the Yugoslav People's Army, the JNA, with Territorial Defence units and
volunteer groups. On the other were Croatian defenders: the National Guard, the police and local volunteers. The siege lasted
about three months, and when it ended the town lay in ruins. This is a recent war, and people in Croatia and in Serbia still
feel it deeply. We will tell you what is known, and who says what.

## Introduction

### 1. BATTLE OF VUKOVAR
- From late August to the eighteenth of November 1991, the JNA and the forces fighting with it besieged the town of Vukovar. Its defenders held out for about three months before the town fell. *(established)*
- Thousands of people were killed or went missing, soldiers and civilians, and most of the town was destroyed. After the fall, prisoners were taken from the town's hospital and killed. An international court later judged that crime. *(established; ICTY finding)*

### 2. Yugoslavia breaks apart
- Yugoslavia was a country of six republics in south-eastern Europe. Its army, the JNA, belonged to the whole federation. *(established)*
- In 1990 and 1991 the federation came apart. On the twenty-fifth of June 1991, the parliaments of Croatia and Slovenia declared independence. Croatia's decision was put on hold for three months, and took full effect on the eighth of October. *(established)*
- About one person in eight in Croatia was a Serb. Many feared what an independent Croatia would mean for them. In areas with many Serbs, local leaders set up their own authorities and barricades, and armed groups formed on both sides. *(established)*
- The JNA said it was keeping the peace and defending Yugoslavia. Croatia's government saw it as an army taking Serbia's side. Historians still argue about when the JNA stopped standing between the two. *(disputed)*

### 3. A town on the Danube
- Vukovar lies where a small river, the Vuka, meets the Danube. Across the Danube is Serbia. *(established)*
- In 1991 the town had about twenty-one thousand Croats and fourteen thousand Serbs, and people of other backgrounds: Hungarians, Rusyns and others. Neighbours, classmates and families crossed these lines. *(established)*
- It had a baroque old town, a hospital, a busy river port, and, just to the north, Borovo Naselje, a factory town built around one of Yugoslavia's biggest shoe factories. On a hill by the Danube road stood a tall water tower. *(established)*
- The two sides name the battle differently. In Croatia it is the defence of Vukovar. In 1991, the JNA and Serb leaders called its capture a liberation. This page uses neutral words: the battle, the siege, the fall. *(established)*

### 4. The two sides
- The attackers were the JNA, the federal army, with tanks, artillery and aircraft. With it fought the local Serb Territorial Defence and volunteer groups, among them Željko Ražnatović's Serb Volunteer Guard, known as Arkan's Tigers, and men linked to the Serbian politician Vojislav Šešelj. *(established; ICTY finding)*
- The CIA later estimated that more than thirty-six thousand JNA, Territorial Defence and volunteer troops were around Vukovar after reinforcements. That counts the whole campaign, not the men in action on any one day. *(estimate)*
- The defenders were the Croatian National Guard and the police, with local volunteers and a smaller force called HOS. They were organised as the 204th Vukovar Brigade. The CIA estimated two to two and a quarter thousand of them. A Croatian list gives about eighteen hundred men when the brigade was founded. *(estimate; Croatian account)*
- These numbers count different things at different times. They are not a head-to-head count of the two sides. *(established)*

### 5. How this page tells it
- This war is recent. Croatian and Serbian accounts often disagree, and some questions are still in the courts. *(established)*
- After each line you will see a tag. "Established" means the sources agree. "ICTY finding" means the judges of the international court found it to be true. "Croatian account" or "Serbian account" tells you whose account it is. "Disputed" means the sources disagree, and "estimate" marks an approximate number. *(established)*
- The front lines on the map are traced from a map made by the CIA in 2002, from its own research. The map of the land and the town is a painting made for this page, showing them as they looked before the siege. *(established; reconstruction)*
- The page tells what happened to people on both sides, without graphic detail. *(established)*

## The reenactment (1991)
Chapters 1 to 6 play on the area map; chapters 7 to 15 move between the area map and the town map. The front lines of 14 September, 30 September and 10 November are the CIA's. Units, arrows and pockets on other days are our reconstruction from the CIA's dated arrows and the histories. *(reconstruction)*

1. **Borovo Selo** · 2 May – 24 August 1991
   On the second of May, Croatian police went into Borovo Selo, a village just north of Vukovar, where armed local Serbs had set up barricades. In the fighting, twelve Croatian policemen were killed. Borovo Selo, the village, is not Borovo Naselje, the factory town. Through the summer, barricades went up across eastern Slavonia. The JNA and local Territorial Defence units took positions around Vukovar, and inside the town, defenders organised under a series of commanders. By August, shells had already damaged the hospital's operating rooms. *(established; Croatian account)*

2. **The siege begins** · 25 August – 13 September 1991
   The twenty-fifth of August is the date usually given for the start of the battle: a large attack from several sides, and shelling almost every day from then on. At the start of September, Mile Dedaković, a former JNA lieutenant colonel known as Jastreb, the Hawk, took command of the town's defence. The hospital moved its patients into the basement. Its own history notes: "Power and water sometimes fail; generators are switched on." People lived in cellars. A Croatian woman, Manda Patko, remembered: "There were days when you couldn't even come out from the basement." A Serb woman of Vukovar, quoted as Kafa, remembered her street's cellar before she left on the seventh of September: "All our neighbors were in one cellar and all stuck together. Croats, Serbs and Ruthenians." A track through the corn fields still linked the town with the outside. *(established; Croatian account; disputed for the date of Dedaković's command)*

3. **Battle for the barracks** · 14 September 1991
   Across Croatia, Croatian forces began to blockade the JNA's barracks. At Vukovar, the JNA attacked from the south, from the direction of Negoslavci, and broke through to its own barracks in the south of the town. The barracks stayed a JNA stronghold inside the town. A JNA brigade, the 1st Guards Mechanised Brigade, held the southern edge of the town until early October. The map shows the CIA's front line for this day. *(established)*

4. **Trpinjska cesta** · 14 – 18 September 1991
   In the north, the JNA's 51st Mechanised Brigade attacked from the direction of Trpinja toward Borovo Naselje, down the long main road, Trpinjska cesta. Some of its tanks were M-84s, a Yugoslav version of the Soviet T-72. The defenders of Borovo Naselje, led by Blago Zadro, fought them with mines and small teams with hand-held anti-tank weapons. Croatian veterans say that on the eighteenth of September "two large columns of tanks" came down the road and were driven back. In Croatia the road became known as a tank graveyard. *(established; Croatian account)*

5. **The offensive from Šid** · 20 – 25 September 1991
   Around the twentieth of September, the JNA's 1st Guards Mechanised Division advanced from Šid, in Serbia, into eastern Croatia. On the twenty-fifth, the JNA garrison left its barracks at Vinkovci, the next town to the west. In Vukovar, the defenders were organised as the 204th Vukovar Brigade. Not every soldier wanted this war. A Serbian human-rights group records that about two thousand reservists from Kragujevac went home from Šid. On the twenty-third of September, a reservist, Vladimir Živković, drove an armoured vehicle to the Federal Parliament in Belgrade in protest. *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account)*

6. **Two new commands** · 30 September – early October 1991
   From the thirtieth of September, the JNA split the battle into two commands. Operational Group South, under Colonel Mile Mrkšić, had its headquarters in a house in Negoslavci. The court later found that Mrkšić had sole command of its combat operations. Operational Group North was led by General Mladen Bratić. The core of Operational Group South was the Guards Motorised Brigade, from Belgrade. With the JNA fought the local Territorial Defence and volunteer groups. The new front line held Vukovar and Borovo Naselje together in one pocket. On the third and fifth of October, the JNA attacked Nuštar, the village on the road to Vinkovci, and was pushed back. *(established; ICTY finding; Croatian account)*

7. **The corn-field road** · October 1991
   On the first of October, the JNA captured Marinci and cut the road west. From then on, people, ammunition and the wounded moved at night along tracks through the corn fields, by way of Bogdanovci. Croatian forces outside tried again and again to break through to the town, but could not open a lasting corridor. Dedaković went out to Vinkovci to lead the relief from outside. In mid-October, Branko Borković, a former JNA captain, took command inside the town. *(established; Croatian account; disputed for the exact date of Borković's command)*

8. **The northern storm** · 16 – 18 October 1991
   On the sixteenth of October, the JNA attacked Borovo Naselje and Trpinjska cesta from several directions at once. Blago Zadro, the commander in Borovo Naselje, was killed. On the seventeenth and eighteenth, there was fighting at Lužac, between Borovo Naselje and the town, including over a temporary floating bridge on the Vuka. *(established)*

9. **The convoy** · 19 October 1991
   For one day, a neutral medical convoy got through. It was organised with Médecins Sans Frontières, Doctors Without Borders. At twenty past eleven in the morning on the nineteenth of October, it left the hospital with more than a hundred badly wounded patients, along a narrow road through the corn fields. MSF counted a hundred and nine; the hospital, a hundred and twelve. On the way back, a mine injured two MSF nurses. It was a single convoy, not an opened road, and the siege went on. *(established; disputed for the number)*

10. **Bogdanovci holds out** · late October – early November 1991
    West of the town, the village of Bogdanovci guarded the corn-field route. On the twenty-ninth of October, the JNA renewed its attack on it. New Croatian units fought outside the ring, west of the town and toward Nuštar. The CIA later judged that the relief efforts were too weak and badly coordinated. Some people in Croatia later claimed that their own government had given Vukovar up. Historians have not shown that it was intended. *(established; disputed)*

11. **Lužac and the river** · 2 – 9 November 1991
    At the start of November, the JNA attacked from the Danube side and took Lužac and the port, cutting the town in two. General Mladen Bratić, commander of Operational Group North, was killed in early November. On the eighth, another JNA attack pushed into Borovo Naselje. On the ninth, the defenders there pulled back to new positions. *(established; disputed for the exact dates; Croatian account)*

12. **The ring closes** · 10 November 1991
    On the tenth of November, Bogdanovci fell. The corn-field route was gone, and nothing could get in or out. The new front line, which the map shows, cut Borovo Naselje off from Vukovar. *(established)*

13. **Pockets** · 11 – 17 November 1991
    On the evening of the twelfth of November, Croatian forces outside made one last attack toward Marinci to break through. They had too few troops, and it failed. On the thirteenth, the defenders lost the grain silo in Borovo Naselje. From then on, each part of the town fought on its own. The CIA describes the attackers' infantry, tanks and engineers working together better in this last phase. A Serbian reporter with the attacking side, Jovan Dulović, saw something else among them. He wrote in his diary on the fourteenth of November: "I don't see who's in command, who obeys who." *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account)*

14. **The fall** · 18 November 1991
    On the eighteenth of November, the defenders of Mitnica, in the south-east of the town, surrendered first. They insisted on surrendering to the JNA, not to the Territorial Defence. The court records that the Croatian forces in the area of Vukovar "capitulated on 18 November 1991". The same day, in Zagreb, an agreement put the JNA in charge of evacuating the hospital, with international organisations to watch. In 1991 the JNA and the Serb authorities called the day a liberation. On this page, we say the town fell. The siege had lasted about three months. *(ICTY finding; established)*

15. **After the guns** · 18 – 20 November 1991
    In the days after the surrender, people from the town, civilians and defenders, were gathered and questioned at Velepromet, a warehouse in the south of the town. On the nineteenth of November, the JNA's Operational Group South took control of the hospital. The European monitors were kept out, and the Red Cross was given only limited access. On the twentieth, at a bridge, Major Veselin Šljivančanin stopped a Red Cross official, Nicolas Borsinger. The court found that he deliberately delayed the Red Cross. The hospital's director, Dr Vesna Bosanac, was questioned through the night, and then taken prisoner. She was released in a prisoner exchange on the tenth of December. In Borovo Naselje, the organised defence ended on the nineteenth of November, and the last groups gave up by the twentieth. *(ICTY finding; established; disputed for the last surrender)*

## The outcome
- Vukovar fell on the eighteenth of November 1991, after about three months of siege. *(established; ICTY finding)*
- Much of the town was destroyed. *(established)*
- Croatian records count four hundred and twenty-two soldiers of the Vukovar brigade dead, four hundred and fifty-seven missing, and seven hundred and seventy-seven wounded. Some books add the dead and the missing together. *(Croatian account)*
- The Croatian Memorial-Documentation Centre of the Homeland War counts about eleven hundred civilians killed. *(Croatian account)*
- Croatian researchers, using Serb records that are incomplete, count three hundred and fifty Serb residents of the Vukovar area who died in 1991: two hundred and three Territorial Defence members and a hundred and forty-seven civilians. *(Croatian account; estimate)*
- No agreed total exists for the JNA's dead. Figures from different sources are far apart, and none has been confirmed. *(disputed)*
- These numbers come from different counts, so they cannot be added together. *(established)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- **Ovčara.** On the twentieth of November, from about ten in the morning, men were taken from the hospital. Among them were wounded patients, hospital staff and defenders of the town. They were driven by bus to the JNA barracks, and from there, between one and two in the afternoon, to a hangar on the Ovčara farm, about five kilometres south-east of the town. That evening, no later than nine o'clock, the JNA guard was withdrawn on Colonel Mrkšić's order. Members of the local Territorial Defence and of volunteer units then killed the prisoners and buried them in a mass grave. *(ICTY finding)*
- The court found that a hundred and ninety-four named people were murdered. Later, two hundred bodies were recovered from the grave. The victims were prisoners. Whatever some of them had done before, the court found that killing them was a crime. *(ICTY finding)*
- Thousands of people from the Vukovar area were held in camps in Serbia, such as Sremska Mitrovica and Stajićevo. Serbian human-rights groups have documented how badly they were treated. *(established)*
- At the international court in The Hague, Mile Mrkšić was sentenced to twenty years. Miroslav Radić, a company commander, was acquitted. Veselin Šljivančanin's sentence was raised on appeal, then lowered again in 2010 when new evidence came out; his final sentence was ten years, for aiding and abetting torture. Slobodan Milošević, Serbia's president, and Goran Hadžić, the local Serb leader, died before their trials ended. Courts in Belgrade later convicted others for Ovčara. *(ICTY finding; established)*
- For the next four years, Vukovar was held by the Serb authorities in this part of Croatia. From January 1996, a United Nations mission ran the region, and on the fifteenth of January 1998 it was returned peacefully to Croatia. *(established)*
- Years later, a JNA veteran, Mile Milošević, talked to a reporter about the war. He asked: "Did it have to be that way?" *(Serbian account)*
- Every year on the eighteenth of November, thousands walk from the hospital to the Memorial Cemetery in Vukovar. In Serbia there is no single view: some still call the battle a liberation, while Serbian human-rights groups and journalists remember the victims. *(established)*
- The water tower, its walls still full of shell holes, was rebuilt as a memorial. It stands over the Danube as a symbol of the town. *(established)*
