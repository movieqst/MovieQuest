**The approach:**
- **One reenactment,** from May to November 1991. The first chapters play on the area map (Vinkovci to the Danube) and the town chapters on the town map.
- **Who did what is named by unit:** "the JNA" (the Yugoslav People's Army, the federal army), "the Territorial Defence", "volunteer groups", "Croatian forces", "the National Guard", "the police". The script never makes "the Serbs" or "the Croats" the actor.
- **The town "fell"** or was "captured". "Liberation" appears only as what one side said.
- **Every number says what it counts and who counted it.** Numbers from different counts are never added together.
- **No graphic detail.** The killings at Ovčara are told plainly in the debrief, as the court found them, with no pictures of victims.
- **Both sides' people are heard:** a Croatian woman in her cellar, a Serb woman of Vukovar, the hospital's own record, a Serbian reporter, and a JNA veteran.

**Tags** (shown after each line on the page):
- **established**: not seriously disputed; supported by the named source.
- **ICTY finding**: found by the judges of the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia. Accusations and witness statements are not findings.
- **Croatian account**: from a Croatian institution, historian, participant or newspaper.
- **Serbian account**: from a Serbian institution, participant, newspaper or the JNA. (A Serbian human-rights group is named as such.)
- **disputed**: sources disagree; the line says who says what, or uses wording true in every version.
- **estimate**: an approximate number, with what it counts.
- **reconstruction**: our own placing on the map of something no source draws exactly.

**Clocks.** Each chapter shows its date in 1991. Where sources give different dates, the clock shows a span ("early November").

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Vukovar, fought in the autumn of 1991 in a town on the
river Danube, in eastern Croatia. On one side were the Yugoslav People's Army, the JNA, with Territorial Defence units and
volunteer groups. On the other were Croatian defenders: the National Guard, the police and local volunteers. The siege lasted
about three months, and when it ended the town lay in ruins. This is a recent war, and people in Croatia and in Serbia still
feel it deeply. We will tell you what is known, and who says what.

## Introduction

### 1. BATTLE OF VUKOVAR
- From late August to the eighteenth of November 1991, the JNA and the forces fighting with it besieged the town of Vukovar. Its defenders held out for about three months before the town fell. *(established)*
- Thousands of people were killed or went missing, soldiers and civilians, and most of the town was destroyed. After the fall, prisoners were taken from the town's hospital and killed. An international court later judged that crime. *(established; ICTY finding)*

### 2. Yugoslavia breaks apart
- Yugoslavia was a country of six republics in south-eastern Europe. Its army, the JNA, belonged to the whole federation. *(established)*
- In 1990 and 1991 the federation came apart. On the twenty-fifth of June 1991, the parliaments of Croatia and Slovenia declared independence. The implementation of Croatia's declaration was suspended for three months. On the eighth of October, Croatia's parliament cut the remaining legal ties with Yugoslavia. *(established)*
- About one person in eight in Croatia was a Serb. Many of them feared what an independent Croatia would mean for them. In areas with many Serbs, local leaders set up their own authorities and barricades, and armed groups formed on both sides. *(established)*
- The JNA said it was keeping the peace and defending Yugoslavia. Croatia's government saw it as an army taking Serbia's side. *(disputed)*
- At Vukovar, the JNA was an attacking force. *(ICTY finding)*

### 3. A town on the Danube
- Vukovar lies where a small river, the Vuka, meets the Danube. Across the Danube is Serbia. *(established)*
- In 1991 the town had about twenty-one thousand Croats and fourteen thousand Serbs, and people of other backgrounds: Hungarians, Rusyns and others. Neighbours, classmates and families crossed these lines. *(established)*
- It had a baroque old town, a hospital, a busy river port, and, just to the north, Borovo Naselje, a factory town built around a major Yugoslav shoe factory. On a hill by the Danube road stood a tall water tower. *(established)*
- The two sides name the battle differently. In Croatia it is the defence of Vukovar. In 1991, the JNA and Serb leaders called its capture a liberation. This page uses neutral words: the battle, the siege, the fall. *(established)*

### 4. The two sides
- The attackers were the JNA, the federal army, with tanks, artillery and aircraft. With it fought the local Serb Territorial Defence and volunteer groups, among them Željko Ražnatović's Serb Volunteer Guard, known as Arkan's Tigers, and a detachment linked to the Serbian politician Vojislav Šešelj. *(established; ICTY finding)*
- The CIA later estimated that more than thirty-six thousand JNA, Territorial Defence and volunteer troops were around Vukovar after reinforcements. That is the CIA's estimate of the reinforced forces around Vukovar, not the number fighting in one street or at one moment. *(estimate)*
- The defenders were the Croatian National Guard and the police, with local volunteers and a smaller force, the Croatian Defence Forces, known as HOS. The 204th Vukovar Brigade became the main military formation in the town's defence. The CIA estimated two thousand to two thousand three hundred defenders. Croatia's veterans ministry gives the brigade a strength of about eighteen hundred. *(estimate; Croatian account)*
- These numbers count different things at different times. They are not a head-to-head count of the two sides. *(established)*

### 5. How this page tells it
- This war is recent. Croatian and Serbian accounts often disagree, and some questions are still in the courts.
- After each line you will see a tag. "Established" means supported by reliable evidence. "ICTY finding" means the judges of the international court found it to be true. "Croatian account" or "Serbian account" tells you whose account it is. "Disputed" means the sources disagree, and "estimate" marks an approximate number.
- The front lines on the map are traced from a map made by the CIA in 2002, from its own research. The map of the land and the town is a painting made for this page, reconstructing the landscape and the town before the destruction. *(reconstruction)*
- The page tells what happened to people on both sides, without graphic detail.

## The reenactment (1991)
Chapters 1 to 6 play on the area map; chapters 7 to 15 move between the area map and the town map. The front lines of 14 September, 30 September and 10 November are the CIA's. Units, arrows and pockets on other days are our reconstruction from the CIA's dated arrows and the histories. *(reconstruction)*

1. **Borovo Selo** · 2 May – 24 August 1991
   On the second of May, Croatian police went into Borovo Selo, a village just north of Vukovar, where armed local Serbs had set up barricades. In the fighting, twelve Croatian policemen were killed. Borovo Selo, the village, is not Borovo Naselje, the factory town. Through the summer, barricades went up across eastern Slavonia. The JNA and local Territorial Defence units took positions around Vukovar, and inside the town, defenders organised under a series of commanders. By August, shells had already damaged the hospital's operating rooms. The Serb National Council, the council of the Serb community in Croatia, also documents killings and disappearances of Serb civilians by Croatian armed groups in Vukovar before the main siege. *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account)*

2. **The siege begins** · 25 August – 13 September 1991
   The twenty-fifth of August is the date usually given for the start of the battle: a large attack from several sides, and shelling almost every day from then on. At the start of September, Mile Dedaković, a former JNA lieutenant colonel known as Jastreb, the Hawk, took command of the town's defence. The hospital moved its patients into the basement. Its own history notes: "Power and water sometimes fail; generators are switched on." People lived in cellars. A Croatian woman, Manda Patko, remembered: "There were days when you couldn't even come out from the basement." A Serb woman of Vukovar, quoted as Kafa, remembered her street's cellar before she left on the seventh of September: "All our neighbors were in one cellar and all stuck together. Croats, Serbs and Ruthenians." A track through the corn fields still linked the town with the outside. *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account for Kafa's recollection; disputed for the date of Dedaković's command)*

3. **Battle for the barracks** · 14 September 1991
   Across Croatia, Croatian forces began to blockade the JNA's barracks. At Vukovar, the JNA attacked from the south, from the direction of Negoslavci, and broke through to its own barracks in the south of the town. The barracks stayed a JNA stronghold inside the town. A JNA brigade, the 1st Guards Mechanised Brigade, held the southern edge of the town until early October. The map shows the CIA's front line for this day. *(established)*

4. **Trpinjska cesta** · 14 – 18 September 1991
   In the north, JNA armoured forces attacked from the direction of Trpinja toward Borovo Naselje, down the long main road, Trpinjska cesta. The JNA used M-84 tanks at Vukovar, a Yugoslav development of the Soviet T-72. The defenders of Borovo Naselje, led by Blago Zadro, fought them with mines and small teams with hand-held anti-tank weapons. Croatian veterans describe renewed attacks by "two large columns of tanks" on the eighteenth of September. In Croatia the road became known as a tank graveyard. *(established; Croatian account)*

5. **The offensive from Šid** · 20 – 25 September 1991
   Around the twentieth of September, the JNA's 1st Guards Mechanised Division advanced from Šid, in Serbia, into eastern Croatia. On the twenty-fifth, the main JNA garrison withdrew from Vinkovci, the next town to the west. In Vukovar, the defenders were organised as the 204th Vukovar Brigade. Not every soldier wanted this war. A Serbian human-rights group records that about two thousand reservists from Kragujevac went home from Šid. On the twenty-third of September, a reservist, Vladimir Živković, drove an armoured vehicle to the Federal Parliament in Belgrade in protest. *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account)*

6. **Two new commands** · 30 September – early October 1991
   At the turn of September and October, the JNA reorganised its forces into two operational groups, one in the south and one in the north. Operational Group South, under Colonel Mile Mrkšić, had its headquarters in a house in Negoslavci. The court found that, from the eighth of October, Mrkšić alone commanded the joint combat operations of the forces in Operational Group South's area. Operational Group North was led by General Mladen Bratić. The core of Operational Group South was the Guards Motorised Brigade, from Belgrade. With the JNA fought the local Territorial Defence and volunteer groups. The front line, which the map shows, now held Vukovar and Borovo Naselje together in one pocket. On the third and fifth of October, the JNA attacked Nuštar, the village on the road to Vinkovci, and was pushed back. *(established; ICTY finding; Croatian account; reconstruction for the map)*

7. **The corn-field road** · October 1991
   On the first of October, the JNA captured Marinci and cut the road west. That blocked the main supply route. The remaining tracks through the corn fields were dangerous, and Bogdanovci held out as a defended pocket on the way. Croatian forces outside tried again and again to break through to the town, but could not open a lasting corridor. Dedaković went out to Vinkovci to lead the relief from outside. In mid-October, Branko Borković, a former JNA captain, took command inside the town. *(established; Croatian account; disputed for the exact date of Borković's command)*

8. **The northern attack** · 16 – 18 October 1991
   On the sixteenth of October, the JNA attacked Borovo Naselje and Trpinjska cesta from several directions at once. Blago Zadro, the commander in Borovo Naselje, was killed. On the seventeenth and eighteenth, there was fighting at Lužac, between Borovo Naselje and the town, including over a temporary floating bridge on the Vuka. *(established)*

9. **The convoy** · 19 October 1991
   For one day, a neutral medical convoy got through. It was organised with Médecins Sans Frontières, Doctors Without Borders. It came in from Bogdanovci along a narrow road through the corn fields. MSF records that it left the hospital at twenty past eleven in the morning on the nineteenth of October, with more than a hundred wounded patients. MSF counted a hundred and nine; the hospital, a hundred and twelve. On the way out, the convoy was forced onto a different route, and a mine injured two MSF nurses. It was a single convoy, not an opened road, and the siege went on. *(established; disputed for the number of patients)*

10. **Bogdanovci holds out** · late October – early November 1991
    West of the town, the village of Bogdanovci guarded the corn-field route. On the twenty-ninth of October, the JNA renewed its attack on it. New Croatian units fought outside the ring, west of the town and toward Nuštar. The CIA later judged that the relief efforts were too weak and badly coordinated. Some people in Croatia later claimed that their own government had given Vukovar up. That claim remains disputed: failed relief attempts alone do not prove an intention to abandon the town. *(established; disputed)*

11. **Lužac and the river** · 2 – 9 November 1991
    In early November, JNA attacks around Lužac and the Danube port threatened the link between Borovo Naselje and the town centre. General Mladen Bratić, commander of Operational Group North, was killed in early November. On the eighth and ninth, the CIA's account describes another JNA attack into Borovo Naselje. A Croatian account records that on the ninth, defenders pulled back to reserve positions at Lužac and Budžak. *(established; disputed for the exact dates; Croatian account)*

12. **The ring closes** · 10 November 1991
    On the tenth of November, Bogdanovci fell, and the remaining route through it was lost. The map shows the CIA's reconstruction of the front on that day, as the defence was being divided into separate pockets. *(established; reconstruction for the map)*

13. **Pockets** · 11 – 17 November 1991
    On the evening of the twelfth of November, Croatian forces outside made one last attack toward Marinci to break through. They had too few troops, and it failed. The CIA dates the loss of Borovo Naselje's grain silo to the thirteenth. By then, the defence was breaking into separate pockets. The CIA describes the attackers' infantry, tanks and engineers working together better in this last phase. At the same time, a Serbian reporter with the attacking side, Jovan Dulović, described confusion among some units. He wrote in his diary on the fourteenth of November: "I don't see who's in command, who obeys who." *(established; Croatian account; Serbian account)*

14. **The fall** · 18 November 1991
    On the eighteenth of November, the defenders of Mitnica, in the south-east of the town, surrendered. They insisted on surrendering to the JNA, not to the Territorial Defence. The court records that the Croatian forces in the area of Vukovar "capitulated on 18 November 1991". The same day, in Zagreb, an agreement provided for the hospital to be evacuated under Red Cross protection, with European monitors given access throughout. In 1991 the JNA and the Serb authorities called the day a liberation. On this page, we say the town fell. The siege had lasted about three months. *(ICTY finding; established)*

15. **After the guns** · 18 – 20 November 1991
    In the days after the surrender, people from the town, civilians and defenders, were gathered and questioned at Velepromet, a warehouse in the south of the town. Some detainees were beaten and abused there. On the nineteenth of November, the JNA's Operational Group South took control of the hospital. The European monitors were kept out, and the Red Cross was given only limited access. On the twentieth, at a bridge, Major Veselin Šljivančanin stopped a Red Cross official, Nicolas Borsinger. The court found that he deliberately delayed the Red Cross. The hospital's director, Dr Vesna Bosanac, was questioned on the nineteenth, held overnight, and returned to the hospital the next morning. She was later imprisoned, and released in a prisoner exchange on the tenth of December. In Borovo Naselje, the historian Davor Marijan dates the end of organised defence to the nineteenth of November; another account gives the twentieth for the final surrender. *(ICTY finding; established; disputed for the last surrender)*

## The outcome
- Vukovar fell on the eighteenth of November 1991, after about three months of siege. *(established; ICTY finding)*
- Much of the town was destroyed. *(established)*
- The brigade's records, cited by the Croatian researchers Ružić and Živić, count four hundred and twenty-two soldiers of the Vukovar brigade dead, four hundred and fifty-seven missing, and seven hundred and seventy-seven wounded, in that historical record. Some books add the dead and the missing together. *(Croatian account)*
- A Croatian study, *Dimensions of the Crime in Vukovar in 1991*, cited by Ružić and Živić, gives about eleven hundred civilians killed in Vukovar. *(Croatian account; estimate)*
- Using incomplete Serb records, Croatian researchers listed three hundred and fifty Serb residents of the former Vukovar municipality: two hundred and three Territorial Defence members, including six listed as missing, and a hundred and forty-seven civilians recorded as dead, in 1991. This is not a count of everyone killed by Croatian forces. *(Croatian account; estimate)*
- Published estimates of the JNA's losses differ widely, and this page does not choose one total. *(disputed)*
- These numbers come from different counts, so they cannot be added together. *(established)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- **Ovčara.** On the twentieth of November, buses carrying prisoners left the hospital at about ten in the morning. Most of the prisoners were men, and many were wounded defenders. They were driven to the JNA barracks, and from there, between one and two in the afternoon, to a hangar on the Ovčara farm, outside Vukovar. Prisoners were beaten at the hangar. That evening, no later than nine o'clock, the JNA guard was withdrawn on Colonel Mrkšić's order. That night, members of the local Territorial Defence and of volunteer units killed the prisoners and buried them in a mass grave. *(ICTY finding)*
- In 1996, two hundred bodies were recovered from the grave. In 2007, the court found that a hundred and ninety-four named victims in this case had been murdered there. They were prisoners, and the law protected them from being killed. *(ICTY finding)*
- After the capture, many non-Serb residents were forced to leave Vukovar. *(ICTY finding)*
- Thousands of prisoners from Croatia, many of them from Vukovar, were held in camps in Serbia, such as Sremska Mitrovica and Stajićevo. The Humanitarian Law Center in Belgrade has documented how badly they were treated. *(established)*
- At the international court in The Hague, Mile Mrkšić was sentenced to twenty years. Miroslav Radić, a company commander, was acquitted. Veselin Šljivančanin's sentence was raised on appeal, when he was also convicted of aiding and abetting murder. In 2010, after new evidence, a review overturned that murder conviction; his final sentence was ten years, for aiding and abetting torture. Slobodan Milošević, Serbia's president, and Goran Hadžić, the local Serb leader, died before their trials ended, so those trials ended without verdicts on their guilt. Courts in Belgrade later convicted others for Ovčara. *(ICTY finding; established)*
- For the next four years, Vukovar was held by the Serb authorities in this part of Croatia. From January 1996, a United Nations mission ran the region. On the fifteenth of January 1998 the mission ended, and the region completed its peaceful return to Croatian administration. *(established)*
- Years later, a JNA veteran, Mile Milošević, talked to a reporter about the war. He asked: "Did it have to be that way?" *(Serbian account)*
- Each November, on the eighteenth, a large remembrance procession walks from the hospital to the Memorial Cemetery in Vukovar. In Serbia there is no single view: some still call the battle a liberation, while Serbian human-rights groups and journalists remember the victims. *(established)*
- The water tower was restored and opened as a memorial, with its shell damage kept. It stands over the Danube as a symbol of the town. *(established)*
