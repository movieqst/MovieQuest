**Owner's rules for this page:**
- No copyrighted material: no film stills, posters, DVD covers, book or newspaper images, and no copied text. Copyrighted
  works (the film, Mark Bowden's book, *Scapegoat*, the National Post feature) are named and cited only.
- No graphic violence. The deaths of Ahmed Arush and Shidane Arone and the deaths in the battle are stated plainly, once,
  without methods, injuries or descriptions of the dead. No images of the dead or wounded, and none of the photographs
  taken at Beledweyne on 16 March 1993.

**The approach:**
- **Seven parts:** war and famine; the intervention; the Somalia Affair (Canada); UNOSOM II and the hunt for Aidid; the
  battle; the aftermath; the film and the history. Chapter numbers run in page order (1–31).
- **Every number says who counted it.** Somali death tolls differ five- to tenfold depending on who counted, so the script
  gives ranges and names the counters. Numbers from different counts are never added together.
- **Every side is a side.** The United Nations, the United States, the Somali National Alliance and the other factions are
  all actors. Where the United States and the United Nations saw law enforcement, the SNA saw a foreign force taking sides,
  and the script gives both.
- **Courts, the inquiry and the press are kept apart.** In the Somalia Affair, courts martial decided individual guilt; the
  national inquiry made findings about leadership and a cover-up but convicted no one; later claims are given as claims.
- **Living people only as courts and reliable sources state.** Clayton Matchee was never tried and is never called guilty.
  Kyle Brown's own account is given as his, next to the court's verdict.
- **The film is a film.** Its differences from history are given with who says so.
- **Spoken forms:** numbers and dates as the narrator says them. Initials are written out at first use.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Mogadishu, fought in the capital of Somalia on the third and fourth of October 1993. It began as a raid by American special forces, and became the longest street battle American soldiers had fought since the Vietnam War. But the battle was the end of a longer story: a civil war, a famine, and an international mission that set out to feed people and ended up at war. Part of that story happened to Canadians, in a town called Beledweyne. And many people know this battle from a film. We will tell you what is known, who says what, and what is still not known. Some chapters describe deaths and the killing of a prisoner. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### 1. MOGADISHU 1993
- On the third of October 1993, American soldiers raided a building in central Mogadishu, looking for leaders of General Mohamed Farrah Aidid's movement. *(established)*
- Two American helicopters were shot down. About ninety soldiers were trapped overnight in the city, until a rescue convoy of American, Malaysian and Pakistani troops reached them the next morning. *(established)*
- Eighteen American soldiers and one Malaysian soldier were killed. No one knows how many Somalis died. Estimates range from about a hundred and thirty to more than a thousand, and many were civilians. *(established; estimate; disputed)*
- Within days, President Bill Clinton set a date for American forces to leave Somalia. *(established)*
- Six months earlier, in another Somali town, Canadian soldiers had killed two Somali men. What followed in Canada became known as the Somalia Affair. *(established)*

### 2. Somalia
*Map: Somalia · 1991*
- Somalia lies in the Horn of Africa, along the Indian Ocean and the Gulf of Aden. Mogadishu, its capital, is a port on the Indian Ocean. *(established)*
- Most Somalis share a language and a religion, Islam. Somali society is organised into clans: large families tracing their descent through the father's line. *(established)*
- Somalia also has minority communities, among them the farming peoples of the south, with their own histories. *(established)*
- Clans are not armies. But in the war that followed 1991, armed movements drew their fighters and their support largely from particular clans. *(scholarship)*

### 3. The fall of Siad Barre
*Map: Somalia · January 1991*
- General Mohamed Siad Barre ruled Somalia from 1969. In his last years his army fought rebel movements in the north and the south. Our page Somalia 1988 tells the story of the war in the north. *(established)*
- In late January 1991, rebels of the United Somali Congress took Mogadishu, and Barre fled the capital. Sources give the twenty-sixth or the twenty-seventh of January. *(established; disputed)*
- Barre's forces regrouped in the south-west and tried twice to retake the capital. He died in exile in Nigeria in 1995. *(established)*
- Somalia was left without a national government. *(established)*

### 4. Who is who
- The United Somali Congress drew mostly on the Hawiye clan-family. It split almost at once into two factions. *(established)*
- Ali Mahdi Mohamed, of the Abgal clan, declared himself interim president on the twenty-ninth of January 1991. *(established)*
- General Mohamed Farrah Aidid, of the Habr Gidir clan, refused to accept him. His faction later formed the Somali National Alliance, the SNA. *(established)*
- Later in this story come the United Nations missions, called UNOSOM; a coalition led by the United States, called UNITAF; and an American special-operations force, Task Force Ranger. *(established)*

### 5. Two stories, one mission
*Map: Somalia · 1992 to 1993*
- This page follows two stories. Most of it is about Mogadishu and the battle of October 1993. *(established)*
- Part 3 is about Canadian soldiers at Beledweyne, a town on the Shabelle River in central Somalia, in March 1993. *(established)*
- The two stories happened in different places, under different missions, about seven months apart. They are linked by the intervention itself: an international effort to feed Somalia that turned to force. *(established)*

### 6. How this page tells it
- Many numbers in this story are disputed. We give each one with the name of whoever counted it. *(established)*
- Courts, a national inquiry, governments, human-rights groups and journalists all appear. We tell you which one says what. *(established)*
- The film *Black Hawk Down* has shaped how many people remember this battle. Part 7 compares the film with the history. *(established)*

## Part 1: War and famine, 1991 to 1992

### 1. A divided city
*Map: Mogadishu · November 1991 to March 1992*
- From November 1991, the forces of Ali Mahdi and General Aidid fought for Mogadishu. The city was split between a Mahdi north and an Aidid south. *(established)*
- The human-rights groups Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights estimated about fourteen thousand people killed and twenty-seven thousand wounded in Mogadishu, from November 1991 to the end of February 1992. Most of them were civilians. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights)*
- Human Rights Watch reported that the fighting died down in March and April 1992. At the end of March, both leaders signed agreements with the United Nations on monitoring a ceasefire. *(report: Human Rights Watch; government: United Nations)*

### 2. The famine
*Map: Somalia · 1991 to 1992*
- War destroyed the farms of the south. Armed groups looted and fought over food, and the harvest and herds failed. *(report: Human Rights Watch)*
- The famine was worst between the Juba and Shabelle rivers, around Baidoa and Bardera. *(established)*
- In 1992, United Nations officials estimated that perhaps three hundred thousand people had died since November 1991, and that at least one and a half million were at immediate risk. *(estimate; government: United Nations)*
- In Baidoa, surveys by the United States Centers for Disease Control counted about seventeen hundred deaths a week in September 1992. By mid-November, as more food arrived, the count had fallen to about three hundred a week. *(report: CDC)*

### 3. A small United Nations mission
*Map: Mogadishu · April to November 1992*
- On the twenty-fourth of April 1992, the United Nations Security Council created the United Nations Operation in Somalia, UNOSOM. It began with fifty unarmed observers to watch the ceasefire. *(established)*
- In September 1992, a battalion of about five hundred Pakistani soldiers arrived to protect Mogadishu's port and airport and the food deliveries. *(established)*
- The mission depended on the consent of the factions, in a country with no government to give it. It could not stop the looting of aid. *(established)*
- The United States flew in food from August 1992, in an airlift called Operation Provide Relief. *(established)*

## Part 2: The intervention

### 4. "All necessary means"
*Map: Somalia · December 1992*
- On the third of December 1992, the Security Council voted fifteen to none for Resolution 794. It allowed member states to use "all necessary means" to make Somalia safe for relief work. *(established)*
- The International Committee of the Red Cross describes it as the first time the Security Council had authorised force to deliver humanitarian aid. *(report: Red Cross)*
- The force was the Unified Task Force, UNITAF, which the Americans called Operation Restore Hope. It was approved by the United Nations, but led by the United States. *(established)*
- On the ninth of December 1992, American Marines came ashore at Mogadishu. *(established)*

### 5. The coalition
*Map: Somalia · December 1992 to May 1993*
- At its largest, UNITAF had about thirty-seven thousand troops, most of them American, from more than twenty countries. Counts range from thirty-two thousand to more than thirty-eight thousand. *(estimate; disputed)*
- Southern Somalia was divided into relief sectors, each held by a lead nation: Americans in Mogadishu and Bardera, Australians at Baidoa, Italians at Gialalassi, French at Oddur, and Canadians at Beledweyne. *(established)*
- The soldiers guarded convoys, ports and food stores, and seized weapons. *(established)*
- Death rates were already falling before UNITAF landed, as relief arrived. The Refugee Policy Group later estimated that international aid saved about a hundred thousand lives. Not everyone accepted that figure. *(report: CDC; estimate; disputed)*

### 6. Canada at Beledweyne
*Map: Beledweyne · December 1992 to June 1993*
- Canada's part was called Operation Deliverance. Its core was the Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group: about nine hundred soldiers, most of them paratroopers, with armoured cars of the Royal Canadian Dragoons and a mortar company of the Royal Canadian Regiment. *(government: Canada)*
- Helicopters of 427 Squadron flew with them, and the supply ship HMCS Preserver waited offshore. In all, about fourteen hundred Canadians served. *(government: Canada; estimate)*
- Their sector was centred on Beledweyne, which the Canadians called Belet Huen. *(established)*
- They escorted convoys, repaired roads and bridges, cleared mines and helped reopen hospitals. *(press: The Canadian Encyclopedia)*
- Canada's national inquiry later found that most of these soldiers did their best, in a mission for which they had been badly prepared. *(inquiry)*

### 7. A camp in the dark
*Map: Beledweyne · early 1993*
- The Canadian camp at Beledweyne held food, fuel and equipment, in a region of deep poverty. People got into the camp at night, and some took things. *(established)*
- Officers of the Airborne Regiment gave orders about how to deal with intruders. Those orders later came before courts martial and the national inquiry. *(court: acquittal; inquiry)*
- The Airborne Regiment had arrived with problems of discipline and leadership already known to the army. The inquiry later found that its leaders had failed to deal with them before it was sent. *(inquiry)*

## Part 3: The Somalia Affair

### 8. The fourth of March
*Map: Beledweyne · 4 March 1993*
- On the night of the fourth of March 1993, soldiers of the Airborne's reconnaissance platoon shot two Somali men near the Canadian compound. *(established)*
- Ahmed Arush was killed. Abdi Hunde Bei Sabrie was wounded. *(established)*
- A Canadian army surgeon, Major Barry Armstrong, raised concerns about how Arush had died. A pathology report in 1994 disputed his findings. *(press; disputed)*
- The first army investigation found the shooting within the rules. The officer in command, Captain Michel Rainville, was later tried and acquitted. *(court: acquittal)*
- Canada's national inquiry found that "the chain of command covered up its undeniable responsibility" for the fourth of March. *(inquiry)*

### 9. The sixteenth of March
*Map: Beledweyne · 16 March 1993*
- On the night of the sixteenth of March 1993, Canadian soldiers detained a Somali teenager, Shidane Abukar Arone, aged sixteen, in their camp. *(established)*
- That night, he was tortured and killed in Canadian custody. *(established; court: conviction)*
- Soldiers took photographs that night. They later became evidence. We do not show them. *(established)*
- The inquiry tied the handling of the fourth of March to what happened twelve days later. *(inquiry)*

### 10. Before the courts
*Map: Beledweyne · 1993 to 2008*
- Master Corporal Clayton Matchee was charged with second-degree murder and torture. Days after his arrest, he tried to take his own life in custody, and suffered a permanent brain injury. *(established)*
- He was found unfit to stand trial. In 2008 the charges were withdrawn, because, a prosecutor said, he would never be fit. He was never tried, and no court decided his guilt. *(court: unfit to stand trial; court: charges withdrawn)*
- In 2017 his wife told CBC News that an anti-malaria drug, mefloquine, given to soldiers in Somalia, had changed him. That is her account. No court and not the inquiry made a finding on it. *(witness; press: CBC)*
- Private Kyle Brown was convicted of manslaughter and torture in March 1994. He was sentenced to five years and dismissed from the army with disgrace. He was released early. *(court: conviction)*
- Brown later wrote a book with a journalist, called *Scapegoat*. In it he maintains his innocence, and argues that the army punished junior soldiers and protected their leaders. That is his account. The court's verdict stands. *(witness)*

### 11. Officers on trial
- Sergeant Mark Boland, in charge of the guard that night, pleaded guilty to negligent performance of duty. He was jailed and reduced to the rank of private. *(court: conviction)*
- Major Anthony Seward, the company commander, was acquitted of causing bodily harm, and convicted of negligent performance of duty. On appeal, his punishment was raised to three months in prison and dismissal. *(court: conviction)*
- The regiment's commander, Lieutenant Colonel Carol Mathieu, was charged over his orders about intruders. He was acquitted, retried, and acquitted again. *(court: acquittal)*
- The heaviest sentence fell on a private. Many Canadians asked why so few leaders were held to account. *(established; press)*

### 12. A regiment disbanded
*Map: Beledweyne · 1995*
- In January 1995, two videos became public: one recorded in Somalia, and one of a hazing ritual inside the regiment in Canada in 1992, with racist content. *(established)*
- On the twenty-third of January 1995, Defence Minister David Collenette ordered the Canadian Airborne Regiment disbanded. It was formally disbanded in March 1995. *(government: Canada)*
- The regiment had served Canada since 1968. Many of its soldiers had served honourably, in Somalia and elsewhere. *(established)*

### 13. The inquiry
- In March 1995, the government appointed the Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia, chaired by Justice Gilles Létourneau. *(established)*
- Its hearings uncovered altered and missing documents. The commission found that senior officers had failed in their leadership, and that some had misled it and covered up. *(inquiry)*
- It found that General Jean Boyle, later Chief of the Defence Staff, had been a party to releasing altered documents. *(inquiry)*
- In January 1997, the government ended the hearings and set a deadline of June 1997. The government cited time and cost. The commissioners wrote that this prevented them from following the events in Somalia, including the sixteenth of March, up the chain of command. *(government: Canada; inquiry)*
- Its report, *Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair*, was released in July 1997. *(established)*

### 14. The victims
- Ahmed Arush and Shidane Arone were Somalis who came near a foreign army's camp, in a time of hunger and war. *(established)*
- The research for this page found no published statement from their families. Their own voices are missing from most of the record. *(established)*
- In Canada, the Somalia Affair changed how the military trains, commands and investigates itself, and it is still taught as a lesson in leadership. *(scholarship)*

## Part 4: UNOSOM II and the hunt for Aidid

### 15. A bigger mandate
*Map: Mogadishu · March to May 1993*
- On the twenty-sixth of March 1993, the Security Council created a new mission, UNOSOM II. It was commanded by the United Nations and allowed to use force. Its mandate included disarming the factions and helping rebuild Somalia's institutions. *(established)*
- On the fourth of May 1993, UNITAF handed over to UNOSOM II. *(established)*
- Its force commander was Turkish Lieutenant General Çevik Bir. Retired American Admiral Jonathan Howe led the mission for the Secretary-General. *(established)*
- About eleven hundred American soldiers stayed as a Quick Reaction Force, under American, not United Nations, command. *(government: United States)*

### 16. The fifth of June
*Map: Mogadishu · 5 June 1993*
- On the fifth of June 1993, Pakistani peacekeepers inspected SNA weapon stores, including one at Radio Mogadishu, which Aidid's movement used. *(established)*
- They were attacked in several places in the city. Twenty-two to twenty-four Pakistani soldiers were killed, depending on the count, and about fifty were wounded. *(estimate; government: United States)*
- An investigator for the United Nations found "clear and convincing" evidence that Aidid had authorised the attack. Aidid denied it. *(government: United Nations; sna)*
- How many Somalis died that day is not recorded in the sources reviewed here. *(established)*

### 17. Resolution 837
*Map: Mogadishu · June 1993*
- The next day, the Security Council condemned the attack and called for the arrest and trial of those responsible. *(established)*
- From the twelfth of June, United Nations and American forces attacked SNA sites in Mogadishu. *(established)*
- On the thirteenth of June, Pakistani United Nations troops reportedly shot dead about twenty Somali demonstrators, according to Amnesty International. *(report: Amnesty)*
- Admiral Howe ordered Aidid's arrest and offered a reward of twenty-five thousand dollars. *(established)*
- For the United Nations and the United States, this was enforcing the law after an attack on peacekeepers. For Aidid's supporters, it was a foreign force taking sides in a civil war. *(established)*

### 18. The twelfth of July
*Map: Mogadishu · 12 July 1993*
- On the twelfth of July 1993, American helicopters attacked a house in Mogadishu, the Abdi House, where, by the American and United Nations account, SNA leaders were meeting. *(established; government: United States)*
- Other accounts describe a meeting of Habr Gidir elders, some of them discussing peace proposals. *(press; disputed)*
- The death toll depends on who counted. On the day, a United Nations spokesman said at least thirteen. The Red Cross reportedly counted fifty-four. Amnesty International said more than fifty, and Somali sources seventy-three. *(estimate; government: United Nations; report: Red Cross; report: Amnesty; sna)*
- Afterwards, an angry crowd killed four journalists who came to the scene: Dan Eldon, Hos Maina, Anthony Macharia and Hansi Krauss. *(established)*
- Many historians see this raid as the point after which Aidid's movement treated the Americans as enemies. *(scholarship)*

### 19. Task Force Ranger
*Map: Mogadishu · August to September 1993*
- On the eighth of August 1993, a mine killed four American military police. *(established)*
- The United States sent Task Force Ranger, about four hundred and forty men under Major General William Garrison. It answered to American, not United Nations, command. *(established; estimate)*
- It was made of Army Rangers, the Delta Force, helicopter crews of the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, and Air Force rescue specialists. *(established)*
- Its first raid, on the thirtieth of August, detained United Nations workers by mistake. In six raids before October it arrested SNA members, but never found Aidid. *(established)*
- On the twenty-fifth of September, Aidid's fighters shot down an American Black Hawk helicopter, and three American soldiers were killed. *(established)*
- Human Rights Watch estimated that, by the end of October 1993, United States and United Nations forces had killed at least five to six hundred Somalis, civilians and fighters. *(estimate; report: Human Rights Watch)*

## Part 5: The battle, 3 and 4 October 1993

### 20. The raid
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, about 3:30 pm*
- On Sunday the third of October 1993, Task Force Ranger received word that two of Aidid's senior advisers were meeting in a building near the Olympic Hotel, close to the Bakara Market. *(government: United States)*
- At about half past three in the afternoon, the helicopters took off. Soldiers of the Delta Force landed at the building, and Rangers slid down ropes to guard the streets around it. A convoy of trucks and Humvees drove in to take the prisoners away. *(established)*
- Within minutes they had twenty-four prisoners. Aidid was not there. *(established)*
- Then the force came under fire from across the neighbourhood, from SNA fighters and armed residents. *(established)*

### 21. Super Six-One
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, about 4:20 pm*
- At about twenty past four, a Black Hawk called Super Six-One was shot down by a rocket-propelled grenade, a few streets from the target. Both pilots, Cliff Wolcott and Donovan Briley, were killed. *(established)*
- Most of the raiding force moved on foot to the crash site, to protect the crew. *(established)*
- The convoy with the prisoners tried to reach them, lost its way in narrow streets under fire, and had to turn back. *(established)*

### 22. Super Six-Four
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, about 4:40 pm*
- About twenty minutes later, a second Black Hawk, Super Six-Four, flown by Michael Durant, came down about a kilometre and a half away. *(established; disputed)*
- Two Delta Force snipers, Master Sergeant Gary Gordon and Sergeant First Class Randy Shughart, asked to be put down to protect the crew. *(established)*
- Both were killed. Durant, the only survivor, was taken prisoner. *(established)*
- Gordon and Shughart were awarded the Medal of Honor after their deaths, the first since the Vietnam War. *(government: United States)*

### 23. The night
*Map: Battle · 3 to 4 October 1993, night*
- About ninety American soldiers spent the night around the first crash site, in houses and courtyards, under fire. *(established)*
- Helicopters flew all night to support them. *(established)*
- Around them, the people of the neighbourhood were caught in the fighting. Many of the Somali dead and wounded were civilians. *(report: Red Cross; established)*

### 24. The rescue convoy
*Map: Battle · 4 October 1993, about 2 am*
- A rescue force gathered at Mogadishu's port: American soldiers of the 10th Mountain Division, with Malaysian armoured carriers and four Pakistani tanks, serving with the United Nations. *(established)*
- A former Malaysian commander later counted a hundred and thirteen Malaysian soldiers in thirty-two armoured carriers. *(witness)*
- The convoy left late in the evening, fought through the city, and reached the trapped soldiers at about two in the morning. *(established)*
- A Malaysian soldier, Corporal Mat Aznan Awang, was killed in the rescue. *(established)*

### 25. The Mogadishu Mile
*Map: Battle · 4 October 1993, about 6 am*
- Shortly before six in the morning, the convoy pulled out. There was not room in the vehicles for everyone, so some soldiers had to move on foot for part of the way, under fire. This became known as the Mogadishu Mile. *(established)*
- By about half past six, everyone had reached the stadium held by the Pakistani battalion, or the airport. *(established)*
- In the words of 10th Mountain Division veterans, published by the United States Army: "We didn't leave anybody behind." *(witness; government: United States)*

### 26. Who died
*Map: Battle · 3 and 4 October 1993*
- Eighteen American soldiers were killed, and about seventy-three to eighty-three wounded, depending on the count. Two days later, a mortar strike killed one more. *(established; disputed)*
- One Malaysian soldier was killed. Malaysian and Pakistani soldiers were wounded. *(established)*
- Somali deaths depend on who counted. In 1993 Aidid's movement gave three hundred and fifteen killed and over eight hundred wounded. In 1998, its leaders spoke of a hundred and thirty-three of their own fighters killed. *(estimate; sna)*
- The Red Cross reportedly counted about two hundred civilians killed. The journalist Mark Bowden wrote of more than seven hundred killed. American estimates went higher. No one knows the true number. *(estimate; report: Red Cross; press: Mark Bowden; government: United States)*

## Part 6: Aftermath

### 27. The pilot and the deadline
*Map: Mogadishu · October 1993 to March 1994*
- Television pictures from Mogadishu, including a video of the captured pilot, shocked many Americans. *(established)*
- On the seventh of October 1993, President Clinton sent more troops, but announced that American forces would leave Somalia no later than the thirty-first of March 1994. *(government: United States)*
- Michael Durant was released on the fourteenth of October, after eleven days, together with a Nigerian peacekeeper. The American envoy said there was no exchange of prisoners. *(established; government: United States)*
- Defence Secretary Les Aspin had refused a request for tanks and armoured vehicles shortly before the raid. He resigned in December 1993. *(established)*

### 28. Leaving
*Map: Somalia · 1994 to 1996*
- Almost all American forces had left Somalia by the end of March 1994. *(government: United States)*
- UNOSOM II stayed on, without the Americans, for another year. Its last troops left in March 1995. *(established)*
- General Aidid was wounded in fighting between factions in Mogadishu, and died in August 1996. *(established)*
- Somalia remained without an effective national government for many years. *(established)*

### 29. A long shadow
- When genocide began in Rwanda in April 1994, the United States and the United Nations did not send troops to stop it. *(established)*
- Some former officials and historians say the memory of Mogadishu weighed on that decision. *(scholarship)*
- Others point out that a stricter American policy on peacekeeping was already being written before the battle. *(scholarship)*
- Both views are held by serious historians. *(established)*

## Part 7: The film and the history

### 30. Mogadishu in Morocco
*Map: Rabat · March to June 2001*
- In 2001, the director Ridley Scott made the film *Black Hawk Down*, from a book by the journalist Mark Bowden. *(established)*
- It was filmed in Morocco from about March to June 2001, mostly in Rabat and the neighbouring city of Salé. *(press)*
- Streets in Salé's Sidi Moussa district were painted and signed as Mogadishu, and the first crash site was built in another Salé district, Hay Arrahma. The base scenes were filmed at Kénitra, about thirty-five kilometres to the north. *(press)*
- The United States Army lent helicopters and crews from the same regiment that flew in Mogadishu. *(government: United States)*
- On this map, the film's places are approximate. The real places are on the Battle map. *(established)*

### 31. What the film changes
- The film follows the battle closely, from the American side. The Army called it authentic, and some veterans praised it, while noting changes made "for the sake of filmmaking". *(government: United States; witness)*
- It leaves out most of the background: the famine relief, the United Nations mission, and the raid of the twelfth of July. *(press)*
- It barely shows the Malaysian and Pakistani soldiers of the rescue convoy. In 2002, Malaysia's defence minister publicly regretted that. *(film; government: Malaysia)*
- Somalis appear mostly as a crowd. Somali viewers and critics objected to that portrayal. *(film; press)*
- Some characters are combined or renamed. One was renamed because the real soldier was later convicted of a crime. *(press)*
- In the film, soldiers run all the way to the stadium. In fact they ran to meet vehicles, which carried them on. *(film; press)*
- The film ends soon after the battle. It does not show Durant's eleven days in captivity, or what followed. *(film; established)*
- A film can help us imagine what it was like for the people it shows. History asks also about the people it does not show. *(established)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- After Barre's fall in 1991, war between the factions and the famine it caused killed hundreds of thousands of Somalis. *(estimate; government: United Nations)*
- The world sent first a small United Nations mission, then a large coalition led by the United States, to protect food deliveries. *(established)*
- After an attack on Pakistani peacekeepers in June 1993, the United Nations mission and the United States went to war with General Aidid's movement. The battle of the third and fourth of October was the deadliest fight of that war. *(established)*
- American soldiers were trapped overnight, and were rescued by a convoy of American, Malaysian and Pakistani troops. *(established)*
- Afterwards the United States withdrew, and the United Nations followed in 1995. *(established)*

### The numbers, side by side (most are estimates)
- Mogadishu's war of 1991 to 1992: about fourteen thousand killed, by Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights. *(estimate)*
- The famine: perhaps three hundred thousand dead by late 1992, by United Nations officials. *(estimate)*
- The twelfth of July: thirteen to seventy-three killed, depending on who counted. *(estimate; disputed)*
- The battle: eighteen Americans and one Malaysian killed. Somali deaths from about a hundred and thirty to more than a thousand, depending on who counted. *(established; estimate; disputed)*
- These figures count different things, by different people. They cannot be added together. *(established)*

### The Somalia Affair
- Canadian soldiers killed Ahmed Arush on the fourth of March 1993, and Shidane Arone in their custody on the sixteenth of March 1993. *(established)*
- Courts martial convicted Kyle Brown, Mark Boland and Anthony Seward. Clayton Matchee was never tried. *(court: conviction; court: unfit to stand trial)*
