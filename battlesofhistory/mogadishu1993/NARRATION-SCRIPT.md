## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Mogadishu, fought in the capital of Somalia on the third and fourth of October 1993. It began as a raid by American special forces, and became an overnight street battle. But the battle was the end of a longer story: a civil war, a famine, and an international mission that set out to feed people and ended up at war. Part of that story happened to Canadians, in a town called Beledweyne. And many people know this battle from a film. We will tell you what is known, who says what, and what is still not known. Some chapters describe deaths, and the killing of a prisoner. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### I1. MOGADISHU 1993
- On the third of October 1993, American soldiers raided a building in central Mogadishu, looking for leaders of General Mohamed Farrah Aidid's movement. *(established)*
- Two American helicopters were shot down. American soldiers remained around the first crash site overnight, until a rescue convoy of American, Malaysian and Pakistani troops reached them the next morning. *(established)*
- Eighteen American soldiers and one Malaysian soldier were killed in the battle. The Somali dead included civilians and fighters. Estimates differ greatly, and no reliable total is known. *(established; disputed)*
- Within days, President Bill Clinton set a deadline for withdrawing American combat forces from Somalia. *(established)*
- Earlier that year, in another Somali town, Canadian soldiers had killed two Somali men. What followed in Canada became known as the Somalia Affair. *(established)*

### I2. Somalia
*Map: Somalia · 1991*
- Somalia lies in the Horn of Africa, along the Indian Ocean and the Gulf of Aden. Mogadishu, its capital, is a port on the Indian Ocean. *(established)*
- Most Somalis share a language and a religion, Islam. Somali society is organised into clans: large families tracing their descent through the father's line. *(established)*
- Somalia also has minority communities, among them the farming peoples of the south, with their own histories. *(established)*
- Clans are not armies. But in the war that followed 1991, armed movements drew their fighters and their support largely from particular clans. *(established)*

### I3. The fall of Siad Barre
*Map: Somalia · January 1991*
- General Mohamed Siad Barre ruled Somalia from 1969. In his last years his army fought rebel movements in the north and the south. Our page Somalia 1988 tells the story of the war in the north. *(established)*
- In late January 1991, rebels of the United Somali Congress took Mogadishu, and Barre fled the capital. *(established)*
- His forces regrouped in the south-west and tried to retake the capital, but he never returned to power. *(established)*
- Somalia was left without a national government. *(established)*

### I4. Who is who
- The United Somali Congress drew mostly on the Hawiye clan-family. It split almost at once into two factions. *(established)*
- Ali Mahdi Mohamed declared himself interim president. *(established)*
- General Mohamed Farrah Aidid refused to accept him. Aidid's faction later formed the Somali National Alliance, the SNA. *(established)*
- Later in this story come the United Nations missions, called UNOSOM; a coalition led by the United States, called UNITAF; and an American special-operations force, Task Force Ranger. *(established)*

### I5. Two stories, one mission
*Map: Somalia · 1992 to 1993*
- This page follows two stories. Most of it is about Mogadishu and the battle of October 1993. *(page)*
- Part 3 is about Canadian soldiers at Beledweyne, a town on the Shabelle River in central Somalia, in March 1993. *(page)*
- The two stories happened in different places, under different missions, about seven months apart. They are linked by the intervention itself: an international effort to feed Somalia that turned to force. *(established)*

### I6. How this page tells it
- Many numbers in this story are disputed. The tables on screen give each one with the name of whoever counted it, and what they counted. *(page)*
- Courts, a national inquiry, governments, human-rights groups and journalists all appear. We tell you which one says what. *(page)*
- The film *Black Hawk Down* has shaped how many people remember this battle. Part 7 compares the film with the history. *(page)*

## Part 1: War and famine, 1991 to 1992

### 1. A divided city
*Map: Mogadishu · November 1991 to March 1992*
- From November 1991, the forces of Ali Mahdi and General Aidid fought for Mogadishu. Ali Mahdi's forces controlled the north of the city, and Aidid's the south. *(established)*
- The human-rights groups Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights estimated about fourteen thousand people killed and twenty-seven thousand wounded in Mogadishu, from November 1991 to the end of February 1992. Many of them were civilians. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights)*
- Through the fighting, Somali doctors and nurses kept caring for the wounded and the sick. *(report: Human Rights Watch)*
- At the end of March 1992, both leaders signed agreements with the United Nations on monitoring a ceasefire. *(government: United Nations)*

### 2. The famine
*Map: Somalia · 1991 to 1992*
- War, looting and drought disrupted farming and food supplies. Hunger and disease spread. *(report: Human Rights Watch; report: CDC)*
- The famine was worst between the Juba and Shabelle rivers, around Baidoa and Bardera. *(established)*
- In 1992, United Nations officials estimated that perhaps three hundred thousand people had died since November 1991, and that at least one and a half million were at immediate risk. *(estimate; government: United Nations)*
- A report by the United States Centers for Disease Control, using Red Cross and Red Crescent records, described a sharp fall in weekly deaths in Baidoa between September and November 1992, before the troops landed. *(report: CDC)*

### 3. A small United Nations mission
*Map: Mogadishu · April to November 1992*
- On the twenty-fourth of April 1992, the United Nations Security Council created the United Nations Operation in Somalia, UNOSOM, with fifty unarmed observers to watch the ceasefire. *(established)*
- In September 1992, a battalion of about five hundred Pakistani soldiers began arriving, to protect Mogadishu's port and airport and the food deliveries. *(established)*
- The mission depended on the consent of the factions, in a country with no government to give it. It could not stop the looting of aid. *(established)*
- The United States flew in food from August 1992, in an airlift called Operation Provide Relief. *(established)*

## Part 2: The intervention

### 4. "All necessary means"
*Map: Somalia · December 1992*
- On the third of December 1992, the Security Council voted fifteen to none for Resolution 794. It allowed member states to use "all necessary means" to make Somalia safe for relief work. *(established)*
- The force was the Unified Task Force, UNITAF, which the Americans called Operation Restore Hope. It was authorised by the United Nations, but commanded by the United States. *(established)*
- On the ninth of December 1992, American Marines came ashore at Mogadishu. *(established)*

### 5. The coalition
*Map: Somalia · December 1992 to May 1993*
- Human Rights Watch described UNITAF as about thirty-seven thousand troops, including twenty-four thousand Americans, from more than twenty countries. *(estimate; report: Human Rights Watch)*
- Southern Somalia was divided into relief sectors. Their lead contingents included Americans in Mogadishu and Bardera, Australians at Baidoa, Italians at Gialalassi, French at Oddur, and Canadians at Beledweyne. *(established)*
- The soldiers guarded convoys, ports and food stores, and seized weapons. *(established)*
- Relief was already reducing deaths before UNITAF arrived. *(report: CDC)*

### 6. Canada at Beledweyne
*Map: Beledweyne · December 1992 to June 1993*
- Canada's part was called Operation Deliverance. Its core was the Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group: about nine hundred soldiers, most of them paratroopers, with armoured cars of the Royal Canadian Dragoons and a mortar company of the Royal Canadian Regiment. *(government: Canada)*
- Helicopters of 427 Squadron flew with them, and the supply ship HMCS Preserver supported the mission offshore. In all, about fourteen hundred Canadians served. *(government: Canada)*
- Their sector was centred on Beledweyne, which the Canadians called Belet Huen. *(established)*
- They escorted convoys, repaired roads and bridges, cleared mines and helped reopen hospitals. *(press: The Canadian Encyclopedia)*
- In May 1993 they passed to the new United Nations mission, and most of them went home in June. *(government: Canada)*
- Canada's national inquiry later found that most of these soldiers did their best, in a mission for which they had been badly prepared. *(inquiry)*

### 7. A camp in the dark
*Map: Beledweyne · early 1993*
- The Canadian camp at Beledweyne held food, fuel and equipment, in a region of deep poverty. People entered the camp at night, and some took things. *(court: appeal)*
- Officers of the Airborne Regiment gave orders about how to deal with intruders. Those orders later came before courts martial and the national inquiry. *(court: acquittal; inquiry)*
- The inquiry found that problems of discipline and leadership in the regiment were known before it was sent, and were not dealt with. *(inquiry)*

## Part 3: The Somalia Affair

### 8. The fourth of March
*Map: Beledweyne · 4 March 1993*
- On the night of the fourth of March 1993, soldiers of the Airborne's reconnaissance platoon shot two Somali men near the Canadian compound. *(established)*
- Ahmed Arush was killed. Abdi Hunde Bei Sabrie was wounded. *(established)*
- A Canadian army surgeon, Major Barry Armstrong, raised concerns about how Arush had died. *(inquiry)*
- The officer in command, Captain Michel Rainville, was tried in 1994 and acquitted. *(court: acquittal)*
- The inquiry found serious failures in the response to the shooting, and examined the cover-up that followed. *(inquiry)*

### 9. The sixteenth of March
*Map: Beledweyne · 16 March 1993*
- On the sixteenth of March 1993, Canadian soldiers captured sixteen-year-old Shidane Abukar Arone and brought him into Canadian custody. *(established)*
- That night, he was tortured and killed in Canadian custody. *(court: conviction)*
- Soldiers took photographs that night. They later became evidence. This page does not show them. *(established; page)*
- The inquiry tied the handling of the fourth of March to what happened twelve days later. *(inquiry)*

### 10. Before the courts
*Map: Beledweyne · 1993 to 2008*
- Master Corporal Clayton Matchee was charged with second-degree murder and torture. After an attempt to take his own life in custody, he suffered a permanent brain injury. *(established)*
- In 1994 he was found unfit to stand trial. In 2008 the charges were withdrawn, because he would never be fit. He was never tried, and no court decided his guilt. *(court: unfit to stand trial; court: charges withdrawn)*
- His wife later said that an anti-malaria drug, mefloquine, given to soldiers in Somalia, had changed his behaviour. No court tested her claim. The inquiry discussed the drug, but could not reach a final conclusion about its role in misconduct. *(witness; inquiry)*
- Private Kyle Brown was convicted of manslaughter and torture in March 1994. He was sentenced to five years in prison and dismissed from the army with disgrace. His appeal was dismissed in January 1995. *(court: conviction; court: appeal)*

### 11. Officers on trial
*Map: Beledweyne · 1994 to 1996*
- Sergeant Mark Boland, in charge of the guard that night, pleaded guilty to negligent performance of duty. On appeal, his sentence was increased to a year in prison. *(court: conviction; court: appeal)*
- Major Anthony Seward, the company commander, was acquitted of causing bodily harm, and convicted of negligent performance of duty. On appeal, his punishment was raised to three months in prison and dismissal. *(court: conviction; court: appeal)*
- The regiment's commander, Lieutenant Colonel Carol Mathieu, was charged over his orders about intruders. He was acquitted. An appeal court ordered a new trial, and he was acquitted again. *(court: acquittal; court: appeal)*
- The courts decided the guilt of individuals. The inquiry looked at the leadership, and was critical of senior officers. *(inquiry)*

### 12. A regiment disbanded
*Map: Beledweyne · 1995*
- In January 1995, two videos became public: one recorded in Somalia, and one of a hazing ritual inside the regiment in Canada in 1992, with racist content. *(established)*
- The government decided to disband the Canadian Airborne Regiment. It closed in March 1995. *(government: Canada)*
- The regiment had served Canada since 1968. Many of its soldiers had served honourably, in Somalia and elsewhere. *(established)*

### 13. The inquiry
*Map: Beledweyne · 1995 to 1997*
- On the twentieth of March 1995, the government appointed the Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia, chaired by Justice Gilles Létourneau. *(government: Canada)*
- The inquiry found that senior officers had failed in their leadership, and examined altered and missing documents. *(inquiry)*
- The inquiry found that General Jean Boyle, later Chief of the Defence Staff, had been a party to the decision to release altered documents. That is an inquiry finding, not a criminal verdict. *(inquiry)*
- In January 1997, the government set a deadline of June 1997, and the hearings were cut short. The government cited time and cost. The commissioners wrote that this left their work on Somalia itself unfinished. *(government: Canada; inquiry)*
- The commission handed in its report at the end of June 1997. It was published in July, as *Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair*. *(established)*

### 14. The victims
*Map: Beledweyne · 1993 to 1999*
- Ahmed Arush and Shidane Arone were Somalis who came near a foreign army's camp, in a time of hunger and war. *(established)*
- Shidane Arone's family later sought damages from the Canadian government in an Ontario court. *(established)*
- In Canada, the affair prompted demands for changes in military leadership and military justice. *(inquiry)*

## Part 4: UNOSOM II and the hunt for Aidid

### 15. A bigger mandate
*Map: Mogadishu · March to May 1993*
- On the twenty-sixth of March 1993, the Security Council created a new mission, UNOSOM II. It was commanded by the United Nations and allowed to use force. Its mandate included disarming the factions and helping rebuild Somalia's institutions. *(established)*
- On the fourth of May 1993, UNITAF handed over to UNOSOM II. *(established)*
- Its force commander was Turkish Lieutenant General Çevik Bir. Retired American Admiral Jonathan Howe led the mission for the Secretary-General. *(established)*
- About eleven hundred American soldiers formed a Quick Reaction Force: a reserve ready to move fast in an emergency. It supported UNOSOM II, while remaining under an American chain of command. *(government: United States)*

### 16. The fifth of June
*Map: Mogadishu · 5 June 1993*
- On the fifth of June 1993, Pakistani peacekeepers inspected SNA weapon stores, including one at Radio Mogadishu, which Aidid's movement used. *(established)*
- They were attacked in several places in the city. The United Nations investigator Tom Farer reported twenty-four Pakistani soldiers killed and fifty-six wounded. *(government: United Nations)*
- Farer's investigation concluded that there was "clear and convincing" evidence that Aidid had authorised the attack. It was an investigation, not a trial. Aidid denied it. *(government: United Nations; sna)*
- A later United Nations commission, in 1994, discussed the evidence again. Responsibility remained contested. *(government: United Nations; disputed)*

### 17. Resolution 837
*Map: Mogadishu · June 1993*
- On the sixth of June, the Security Council condemned the attack and authorised action against those responsible, including their arrest for trial. *(established)*
- From the twelfth of June, United Nations and American forces attacked SNA sites in Mogadishu. *(established)*
- On the thirteenth of June, Pakistani United Nations troops reportedly shot dead about twenty Somali demonstrators, according to Amnesty International. *(report: Amnesty)*
- Admiral Howe ordered Aidid's arrest and offered a reward of twenty-five thousand dollars. *(report: Human Rights Watch)*
- United Nations leaders presented the campaign as enforcing the mandate and arresting those responsible. Aidid's supporters described it as foreign intervention against their side. *(government: United Nations; sna)*

### 18. The twelfth of July
*Map: Mogadishu · 12 July 1993*
- On the twelfth of July 1993, American helicopters attacked a house in Mogadishu, the Abdi House, where, by the American and United Nations account, SNA leaders were meeting. *(established; government: United States)*
- Other accounts describe a meeting of clan elders, some of them discussing peace proposals. *(press; disputed)*
- The United Nations first said at least thirteen people were killed. Human Rights Watch reported that the Red Cross counted at least fifty-four dead. The SNA said seventy-three. *(estimate; government: United Nations; report: Human Rights Watch; sna)*
- Afterwards, an angry crowd killed four journalists who came to the scene: Dan Eldon, Hos Maina, Anthony Macharia and Hansi Krauss. *(established)*
- After the raid, Aidid's movement attacked American forces directly. *(established)*

### 19. Task Force Ranger
*Map: Mogadishu · August to September 1993*
- On the eighth of August 1993, a roadside bomb killed four American military police. *(established)*
- The United States sent a special-operations task force under Major General William Garrison. It was ordered out on the twenty-second of August, and its main parts were in Mogadishu by the twenty-eighth. *(government: United States)*
- It was made of Army Rangers, the Delta Force, helicopter crews of the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, Navy SEALs, and Air Force rescue specialists. *(government: United States)*
- Its first raid detained United Nations workers by mistake. In six raids before October it arrested SNA members, but never found Aidid. *(government: United States)*
- On the twenty-fifth of September, Aidid's fighters shot down an American Black Hawk helicopter, and three American soldiers were killed. *(established)*

## Part 5: The battle, 3 and 4 October 1993

### 20. The raid
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, about 3:30 pm*
- On Sunday the third of October 1993, Task Force Ranger received word that two of Aidid's senior advisers were meeting in a building near the Olympic Hotel, close to the Bakara Market. *(government: United States)*
- At about half past three in the afternoon, the helicopters took off. Soldiers of the Delta Force landed at the building, and Rangers slid down ropes to guard the streets around it. A convoy of trucks and Humvees drove in to take the prisoners away. *(established)*
- They took twenty-four prisoners. Aidid was not there. *(established)*
- Then the force came under fire from across the neighbourhood, from SNA fighters and other armed men. *(established)*

### 21. Super Six-One
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, late afternoon*
- Later that afternoon, a Black Hawk called Super Six-One was shot down by a rocket-propelled grenade, a few streets from the target. Both pilots were killed. *(established)*
- Most of the raiding force moved on foot to the crash site, to protect the crew. *(established)*
- The convoy with the prisoners tried to reach them, lost its way in narrow streets under fire, and had to turn back. *(established)*

### 22. Super Six-Four
*Map: Battle · 3 October 1993, late afternoon*
- Soon afterwards, a second Black Hawk, Super Six-Four, flown by Michael Durant, came down less than a mile to the south. *(established)*
- Two Delta Force snipers, Master Sergeant Gary Gordon and Sergeant First Class Randy Shughart, volunteered to be put down to protect the crew. *(government: United States)*
- Both were killed. Durant, the only survivor, was taken prisoner. *(established)*
- Gordon and Shughart received the Medal of Honor after their deaths, for their actions in Mogadishu. *(government: United States)*

### 23. The night
*Map: Battle · 3 to 4 October 1993, night*
- American soldiers spent the night around the first crash site, in houses and courtyards, under fire. *(established)*
- Helicopters flew all night to support them. *(established)*
- Around them, the people of the neighbourhood were caught in the fighting. The Somali dead and wounded included both fighters and civilians. *(established)*

### 24. The rescue convoy
*Map: Battle · 4 October 1993, about 2 am*
- A rescue force gathered at Mogadishu's New Port: American soldiers of the 10th Mountain Division, with Malaysian armoured carriers and Pakistani tanks, serving with the United Nations. *(established)*
- The Malaysian commander, Brigadier-General Abdul Latif Ahmad, later said a hundred and thirteen Malaysian soldiers took part, in thirty-two armoured carriers. *(witness; press: Agence France-Presse)*
- The convoy left late that evening, fought through the city, and reached the trapped soldiers at about two in the morning. *(established)*
- A Malaysian soldier, Mat Aznan Awang, was killed in the rescue. *(press: Agence France-Presse)*

### 25. The Mogadishu Mile
*Map: Battle · 4 October 1993, about 6 am*
- Shortly before six in the morning, the convoy pulled out. There was not room in the vehicles for everyone, so some soldiers had to move on foot, under fire, to meet vehicles further on. This became known as the Mogadishu Mile. *(established)*
- The main rescue convoy reached the stadium held by the Pakistani battalion at about half past six. *(established)*

### 26. Who died
*Map: Battle · 3 and 4 October 1993*
- Eighteen American soldiers were killed in the battle, and more than seventy were wounded. Two days later, a mortar strike killed one more. *(established)*
- One Malaysian soldier was killed. Malaysian and Pakistani soldiers were wounded. *(established)*
- The Somali dead included both fighters and civilians. An SNA officer, Captain Haad, later said a hundred and thirty-three of its militiamen were killed. That counts only the militia. Human Rights Watch reported more than two hundred Somalis killed. No reliable total is known. *(sna; report: Human Rights Watch; disputed)*
- By the end of October, Africa Watch estimated that United States and United Nations forces had killed at least five to six hundred Somalis in the whole confrontation, including this battle. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch)*

## Part 6: Aftermath

### 27. The pilot and the deadline
*Map: Mogadishu · October 1993 to March 1994*
- Television reports of the American deaths and of the captured pilot shocked many Americans. *(established)*
- On the seventh of October 1993, President Clinton sent more troops, but set the end of March 1994 as the deadline for withdrawing American combat forces, with a small support force allowed to remain. *(government: United States)*
- Michael Durant was released on the fourteenth of October, after eleven days, together with a Nigerian peacekeeper. President Clinton said no deal had been made. *(established; government: United States)*
- Defence Secretary Les Aspin had refused a request for tanks and armoured vehicles before the raid. He resigned in December 1993. *(established)*

### 28. Leaving
*Map: Somalia · 1994 to 1995*
- The American combat forces left Somalia by the end of March 1994. A small number of American troops stayed until September. *(government: United States)*
- UNOSOM II continued for another year. American troops returned briefly to help the final United Nations withdrawal, in March 1995. *(government: United States)*
- Somalia remained without an effective national government for many years. *(established)*

### 29. A long shadow
*Map: Somalia · 1994*
- In April 1994, genocide began in Rwanda. United Nations peacekeepers were already in Rwanda. After the genocide began, their force was cut back, and enough reinforcements did not arrive in time to stop it. *(established)*
- The United States decided not to send its own troops. *(established)*
- Some former officials and historians say the memory of Mogadishu weighed on these decisions. Others point out that a stricter American policy on peacekeeping was already being written before the battle. *(scholarship)*
- Somalia influenced policy, but it was not the only reason for the failure to act. *(scholarship)*

## Part 7: The film and the history

### 30. Mogadishu in Morocco
*Map: Rabat · March to June 2001*
- In 2001, the director Ridley Scott made the film *Black Hawk Down*, from a book by the journalist Mark Bowden. *(established)*
- Filming began on the eighth of March 2001, at Kénitra, and continued until June, mostly in Rabat and the neighbouring city of Salé. *(established)*
- Streets in Salé were painted and signed to stand in for Mogadishu. *(established)*
- The United States Army lent helicopters and soldiers from the units that fought in Mogadishu. *(government: United States)*
- On this map, the film's places are approximate. The real places are on the Battle map. *(page)*

### 31. What the film changes
*Map: Rabat · 2001 to 2002*
- The film follows the battle from the American side. At its premiere in 2002, the Army's vice chief of staff, General Jack Keane, called it authentic, and the Ranger veteran Matt Eversmann praised it, while noting changes made "for the sake of filmmaking". *(witness; press: American Forces Press Service)*
- The film briefly introduces the famine and the intervention, but gives little of the political history that led to the raid. *(film)*
- It gives little part to the Malaysian and Pakistani soldiers of the rescue convoy. In 2002, Malaysia's defence minister, Najib Razak, publicly regretted that. *(government: Malaysia; press: Agence France-Presse)*
- Somalis appear mostly as a crowd. Some Somali viewers and critics objected to that portrayal. *(film; press)*
- Some characters are combined or renamed. *(established)*
- The historical Mogadishu Mile ended at a meeting with vehicles, which carried the soldiers on. *(established)*
- The film ends soon after the battle. It does not show Durant's eleven days in captivity, or what followed. *(film)*
- A film can help us imagine what it was like for the people it shows. History asks also about the people it does not show. *(page)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- After Barre's fall in 1991, war between the factions, looting and drought caused a famine. United Nations officials estimated that perhaps three hundred thousand people died. *(estimate; government: United Nations)*
- The world sent first a small United Nations mission, then a large coalition led by the United States, to protect food deliveries. *(established)*
- After the attack on Pakistani peacekeepers in June 1993, United Nations and American forces fought General Aidid's movement. The October battle became a turning point in the intervention. *(established)*
- American soldiers were trapped overnight, and were rescued by a convoy of American, Malaysian and Pakistani troops. *(established)*
- Afterwards the American combat forces withdrew, and the United Nations followed in 1995. *(established)*

### The numbers (see the tables on screen)
- Eighteen American soldiers and one Malaysian soldier were killed in the battle. *(established)*
- The Somali dead included civilians and fighters. Estimates differ greatly, and no reliable total is known. *(disputed)*
- Each count on this page counts something different: all deaths, or fighters only, or a longer period. They cannot be added together. *(page)*

### The Somalia Affair
- Canadian soldiers killed Ahmed Arush on the fourth of March 1993, and Shidane Arone in their custody on the sixteenth of March 1993. *(established)*
- Courts martial convicted Kyle Brown, Mark Boland and Anthony Seward. Clayton Matchee was never tried. *(court: conviction; court: unfit to stand trial)*
- The national inquiry found failures of leadership and examined a cover-up. The government cut its work short. *(inquiry; government: Canada)*
- The Canadian Airborne Regiment was disbanded in 1995. *(established)*

### Still unknown
- How many Somalis died in the battle, and in the months of fighting before it. *(disputed)*
- Who was responsible for the attack of the fifth of June remained contested. *(disputed)*
