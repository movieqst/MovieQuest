## 1. Deployment: the Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group at Belet Huen under UNITAF

### Takeaway
In December 1992 Canada sent about 1,400 troops, mostly from the Canadian Airborne Regiment, as its part of the US-led UNITAF (Canada's name for the mission was Operation Deliverance). They were based in the Belet Huen humanitarian relief sector in south-central Somalia, where they secured the area and helped deliver aid. They served until about May or June 1993. Sources differ on the exact start and end dates.

### Cited Findings
- About 1,400 Canadian troops, mostly from the Canadian Airborne Regiment, were sent to the Belet Huen Humanitarian Relief Sector in south-central Somalia. A battalion-sized group arrived in Belet Huen in December 1992. [REF] — [Canadian Military Journal Vol. 17 No. 4](http://www.journal.forces.gc.ca/Vol17/no4/page35-eng.asp); [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Canadian Peacekeepers in Somalia"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canadian-peacekeepers-in-somalia)
- Start date: Wikipedia's "Operation Deliverance" article gives 3 December 1992 (tertiary). The Canadian Military Journal gives 19 December 1992 as the date Canada's role in securing the sector was set. — [Operation Deliverance (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Deliverance); [Canadian Military Journal](http://www.journal.forces.gc.ca/Vol17/no4/page35-eng.asp)
- End date: Wikipedia gives the operation as December 1992 to May 1993 (tertiary). UNITAF handed over to UN command (UNOSOM II) on 4 May 1993. One Canadian veteran's account describes service from November 1992 to June 1993. — [Operation Deliverance (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Deliverance); [Shared Histories, "Peacemakers"](https://sharedhistories.ca/realizing-the-pledge/peacekeeping/somalia/)
- Tasks: bringing order to the sector and distributing relief supplies. This included rebuilding roads and bridges, clearing land mines, reopening hospitals and guarding food-aid convoys. [REF] — [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Canadian Peacekeepers in Somalia"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canadian-peacekeepers-in-somalia); [Shared Histories](https://sharedhistories.ca/realizing-the-pledge/peacekeeping/somalia/)
- Donna Winslow, "Between Dream and Reality: the Canadian Mission to Somalia", is a scholarly account of the deployment. It came up in search results but was not read. [REF] — [Winslow (PDF)](https://www.bmlv.gv.at/pdf_pool/publikationen/01_11pop_04_wins.pdf)

### Inferences
- A safe wording for the page is "about 1,400 Canadian troops, mostly from the Canadian Airborne Regiment, served at Belet Huen from December 1992 until spring 1993 as part of the US-led UNITAF mission." That avoids picking between the conflicting start and end dates.
- The aid work (roads, bridges, mine clearing, hospitals, convoy escort) is the documented basis for saying that most soldiers served honourably.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Inquiry report or a DND page to confirm the exact arrival date, departure date or troop count. Belet Huen is also spelled "Beledweyne".

## 2. 4 March 1993: the shooting near the Canadian compound

### Takeaway
On the night of 4 March 1993, soldiers of the Airborne's reconnaissance platoon, led by Captain Michel Rainville, shot two Somali men near the Canadian compound at Belet Huen. Ahmed Arush (also spelled Achmed Aruush) was killed and Abdi Hunde Bei Sabrie was wounded. A Canadian military surgeon, Major Barry Armstrong, raised concerns about how Arush died, and his findings were disputed. The Somalia Inquiry later found that the chain of command had covered up its responsibility for the incident. Rainville was acquitted.

### Cited Findings
- The two Somali men were Ahmed Arush, who was killed, and Abdi Hunde Bei Sabrie, who was wounded. They were shot near the Canadian compound at Belet Huen. [REF/tertiary] — [Michel Rainville (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rainville); [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)
- Captain Michel Rainville led the reconnaissance platoon that carried out the operation. Per Wikipedia (tertiary) he was acquitted of criminal charges over it and was released from military service. [COURT, tertiary report] — [Michel Rainville (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rainville)
- Major Barry Armstrong, a Canadian Forces surgeon, examined Arush's body and raised concerns about how he died, becoming a whistle-blower. A later pathology report, by Dr. James Ferris (1994), disputed Armstrong's findings. [PRESS/REF] — [The Globe and Mail, "Military doctor Barry Armstrong blew the whistle on soldiers' crimes in Somalia"](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-military-doctor-barry-armstrong-blew-the-whistle-on-soldiers-crimes-in/); [CMAJ, 27 Jan 1998, "MD at centre of Somalia controversy finds peace in Northern Ontario"](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/cmaj/158/2/244.full.pdf); [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)
- Commission finding (quoted from the report): "From an initial damage-control approach, through subsequent distortion and suppression of relevant or incriminating information, and through inaction when positive action was required, the chain of command covered up its undeniable responsibility for the March 4 incident." The report also says it "unearthed the origins of cover-up" in the 4 March incident. [COMMISSION] — [Report of the Somalia Commission of Inquiry (copy)](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf); [Somalia Mission Inquiry Findings (Scribd copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/27679447/Somalia-Inquiry-Report-1997)
- Commission/reference point: the first investigation was ordered inside the regiment, so the commanding officer was in effect investigating his own unit's operational decisions. A summary investigation found the shooting was within the rules of engagement and cleared the Reconnaissance Platoon of criminal responsibility. One source links the internal investigation to pressure from Colonel Serge Labbé (the Canadian Joint Force Somalia commander). Sources describe the investigation's objectivity as "questionable". [COMMISSION/REF] — [Somalia Inquiry report (copy)](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf); [Queen's CIDP, "Crisis to Catalyst" (OP 70)](https://www.queensu.ca/cidp/sites/cidpwww/files/uploaded_files/OP%2070%20online.pdf)
- Per a source summarized in search results, no one was convicted in relation to the death of Ahmed Arush. [REF] — [Queen's CIDP, "Crisis to Catalyst"](https://www.queensu.ca/cidp/sites/cidpwww/files/uploaded_files/OP%2070%20online.pdf)
- The Inquiry report ties the handling of 4 March to the death of Shidane Arone 12 days later. [COMMISSION] — [Somalia Inquiry report (copy)](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf)
- Search results also mention a 2001 National Post headline about Rainville and a conviction in a "raid" case. That case does not concern Somalia and should **not** be connected to the 4 March incident. [PRESS, unverified] — [Michel Rainville (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rainville)

### Inferences
- For students, the safest wording is: "One Somali man, Ahmed Arush, was killed and another was wounded. A Canadian army doctor raised concerns about how he died. The national inquiry later found that commanders had covered up their responsibility." No detail of the wounds is needed.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the date or wording of the charge against Rainville, his acquittal date, or the CMAC record. I also could not confirm whether the two men were said to be trying to enter the compound (to steal, according to the military's account). Search snippets mention "food and water" and a "trap" operation, but I could not check that wording.
- I could not read the Ferris/Armstrong dispute in full. The page should say only that the medical evidence was disputed and that the Inquiry did not accept the chain of command's account.

## 3. 16 March 1993: the death of Shidane Abukar Arone in Canadian custody

### Takeaway
Shidane Abukar Arone, a 16-year-old Somali, died in Canadian custody at the Belet Huen camp on the night of 16 March 1993. Master Corporal Clayton Matchee was charged with second-degree murder and torture, but never stood trial (see section 4). Private Kyle Brown was convicted of manslaughter and torture. Others in the chain of command were tried for lesser offences (see section 5).

### Cited Findings
- Shidane Arone, a Somali teenager, was killed at the Belet Huen camp in March 1993. This is the event the deployment is best known for. [REF] — [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)
- Matchee "had been charged with second-degree murder and torture in connection with the 1993 death of Somali national Shidane Abukar Arone, 16." [PRESS] — [CBC News, "Charges withdrawn for former Canadian soldier accused of killing Somali teen"](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411)
- Brown was convicted of manslaughter and torture. [COURT, as reported] — [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair); [CBC Archives clip on Brown's sentencing](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/1826241220)
- After the regiment returned, nine of its members were tried in military court over offences connected to Arone's death (per Wikipedia, tertiary). — [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)
- The Inquiry was shut down before it had examined the events in Somalia in detail, including Arone's death (see section 7). [REF] — [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)

### Inferences
- A suitable sentence for ages 12+ is: "Shidane Abukar Arone, a 16-year-old Somali, was beaten to death while held prisoner by Canadian soldiers. Two soldiers were charged; one was convicted of manslaughter and torture." Leave out methods, injuries and the photographs.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm from a primary source why Arone was detained (reports say he was caught inside the compound on 16 March). The exact time of death was also not confirmed.

## 4. Master Corporal Clayton Matchee

### Takeaway
Matchee was arrested after Arone's death and tried to take his own life while in custody in March 1993. The attempt left him with a permanent brain injury. A court martial found him unfit to stand trial (reported date 25 April 1994), and he was found unfit again in 2004. The Department of National Defence withdrew the murder and torture charges in 2008, citing his permanent injury. His wife has since publicly blamed the antimalarial drug mefloquine; that is her claim, not a finding by any court or the Inquiry.

### Cited Findings
- Matchee was charged with second-degree murder and torture in Arone's death. [PRESS] — [CBC News (2008)](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411)
- His suicide attempt in custody failed but caused serious brain damage, and he was found unfit to stand trial. [PRESS/REF] — [CBC News (2008)](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411); [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Somalia Inquiry's Damning Report"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report)
- A court martial found him unfit to stand trial on 25 April 1994, citing a mental disorder (date as given in search summaries; check before use). [COURT, as reported] — [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)
- In 2004 a judge again found him unfit, and the military still did not drop the charges then. [PRESS] — [CBC News, "Military won't drop charges against Matchee"](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/military-won-t-drop-charges-against-matchee-1.512515)
- In 2008 the murder and torture charges were withdrawn, as the Department of National Defence announced, "15 years" after the death. A prosecutor said Matchee had a permanent brain injury and "will never be fit to stand trial". The same reporting says he had been released from a Saskatchewan psychiatric hospital and that officials judged he did not "pose a significant threat to the community". [PRESS] — [CBC News (2008)](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411); [CTV News, "Charges dropped against soldier in Somali death"](https://www.ctvnews.ca/mobile/charges-dropped-against-soldier-in-somali-death-1.325044); [The Globe and Mail, "Brain-damaged ex-soldier freed of murder charge"](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/news/national/brain-damaged-ex-soldier-freed-of-murder-charge/article18204357/)
- In 2017 his wife, Marj Matchee, told CBC that mefloquine had driven him "to madness" and asked for the Somalia Inquiry to be reopened. CBC reported that he needs round-the-clock care and spends most of his time in a veterans' hospital or with his parents. [CLAIM, reported by PRESS] — [CBC News, "Man at centre of Somalia affair driven 'to madness' by antimalarial drug, wife says"](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatoon/clayton-matchee-s-wife-wants-to-reopen-somalia-inquiry-1.4258889)
- Anti-mefloquine veterans' groups have made the same argument, noting that the Inquiry closed before planned testimony on mefloquine was heard. [CLAIM] — [MBC Radio (2016)](https://www.mbcradio.com/2016/09/anti-mefloquine-veterans-highlight-use-of-drug-in-clayton-matchees-deployment)

### Inferences
- Because Matchee was never tried, the page must not call him guilty. Accurate wording is "charged with murder and torture; never tried because a brain injury left him unfit; charges withdrawn in 2008."
- The mefloquine argument should be presented only as what his family and some veterans have said. No court or the Inquiry made any finding on it.
- The page should name the self-harm without describing it. Something like "he was badly injured in a suicide attempt while in custody" is enough.

### Gaps
- **Date of the suicide attempt:** none of the sources I reached gave it. It is widely given elsewhere as 19 March 1993, but I could not verify that here, so it should not be used unless checked.
- I could not confirm the exact day in 2008 that the charges were withdrawn (CBC says it was a Monday).

## 5. Private Kyle Brown and the other courts martial

### Takeaway
Kyle Brown was convicted of manslaughter and torture in March 1994. He was sentenced to five years and dismissed with disgrace, and his appeal failed. He was released early, but sources disagree on how long he served (about one to three years). Courts martial for others in the chain of command produced mixed results. Sgt. Mark Boland pleaded guilty to negligent performance of duty. Maj. Anthony Seward was first only reprimanded, but on appeal got three months' imprisonment and dismissal. Lt.-Col. Carol Mathieu was acquitted twice. Capt. Rainville was acquitted (over 4 March). No senior officer was convicted of a serious offence.

### Cited Findings
**Kyle Brown**
- He was convicted of manslaughter and torture and sentenced to five years' imprisonment, plus dismissal from the Canadian Forces. [COURT, as reported] — [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)
- CBC Archives (clip dated 18 March 1994): "Private Kyle Brown receives dismissal with disgrace and is sentenced to five years in prison." [PRESS] — [CBC Archives](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/1826241220)
- A Global News photo caption places a court session at CFB Petawawa on 17 March 1994. Grokipedia (tertiary, AI-generated) gives 16 March 1994 as the date of conviction. — [Global News, "History of the 90s: the Somalia affair"](https://globalnews.ca/news/6510691/history-of-the-90s-somalia-affair/amp); [Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Somalia_affair)
- One older Wikipedia-derived page says four years, not five. This is an outlier; the CBC Archives clip supports five. — [en-academic (tertiary)](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/8034067)
- His appeal to the Court Martial Appeal Court was dismissed (Grokipedia and Wikipedia, tertiary, say 1995). [COURT, tertiary report] — [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)
- **Early release (sources conflict):** The Canadian Encyclopedia ("Somalia Affair") says he was released "after less than two years". The Fandom wiki and Wikipedia (tertiary) say parole after one year. A 1995 New York Times piece cited by en-academic says he had served about one third of his sentence. A different Canadian Encyclopedia article ("Somalia Inquiry's Damning Report", 1997) says he "served three years". — [TCE "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair); [TCE "Somalia Inquiry's Damning Report"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report); [Fandom (tertiary)](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Somalia_Affair)
- Later account: Brown wrote *Scapegoat* with journalist Peter Worthington. In it he argues that senior officers worked to keep the convictions to the lower ranks, and he maintains his innocence. [CLAIM, reported by REF] — [TCE "Somalia Inquiry's Damning Report"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report)

**Sergeant Mark Boland**
- He pleaded guilty to negligent performance of duty and not guilty to torture (he was acquitted of torture). He was sentenced to 90 days' detention and reduced to private. Reports describe him as found "wilfully blind". [COURT, tertiary report] — [Mark Adam Boland (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Adam_Boland)

**Major Anthony Seward** (commander of 2 Commando)
- He was acquitted of unlawfully causing bodily harm. In 1994 a court martial reprimanded him for negligent performance of duty, over telling his men they could abuse prisoners. On appeal, the Court Martial Appeal Court found the sentence too lenient and replaced it with three months' imprisonment and dismissal from the Canadian Forces. [COURT, as reported] — [Mark Adam Boland / Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair); [Tampa Bay Times, 28 May 1996, "Canadian gets jail term in Somalia death"](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1996/05/28/canadian-gets-jail-term-in-somalia-death/)

**Lieutenant-Colonel Carol Mathieu** (commanding officer, Canadian Airborne Regiment)
- He was charged with negligent performance of duty (over his orders on dealing with intruders/looters) and acquitted in June 1994. The defence argued he had ordered tighter security, not that looters be shot "at all costs". The prosecution appealed, the Court Martial Appeal Court ordered a new trial, and he was acquitted again. [COURT, as reported] — [UPI, 24 June 1994, "Canada officer cleared in Somali death"](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1994/06/24/Canada-officer-cleared-in-Somali-death/1690772430400/); [Somalia affair (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair)

**Captain Michel Rainville**
- He was acquitted over the 4 March incident and released from the Forces (see section 2). [COURT, tertiary report] — [Michel Rainville (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rainville)

### Inferences
- The result is that a junior soldier (Brown) got the heaviest sentence, and the officers were acquitted or received light sentences. This pattern is what Brown's "scapegoat" argument and the Inquiry's leadership findings both point to, from different directions. Stating it plainly is fair to both the victims and the rank and file.
- The Seward appeal is CMAC case R. v. Seward (1996). Boland's appeal is reported elsewhere as R. v. Boland (1995), where his sentence may have been increased. I could not verify either case on CanLII.

### Gaps
- I could not reach CanLII or cmac-cacm.ca, so R. v. Brown, R. v. Seward and R. v. Boland were not read directly. Whether Boland's 90 days was increased on appeal (often reported as raised to one year) is **unverified**.
- I could not confirm the date of Brown's parole or release.
- I could not identify all nine accused or the outcome of each case (others named elsewhere include Pte. David Brocklebank, acquitted, and Sgt. Perry Gresty and Cpl. Matt McKay, among others). These names are from memory and **not verified**; do not use them.

## 6. Photographs, videos and the disbandment of the Canadian Airborne Regiment

### Takeaway
Photographs taken during Arone's detention became public and turned his death into a national scandal (do not describe them). In January 1995, videos became public: one shot in Somalia in 1993 and a 1992 initiation (hazing) video from CFB Petawawa that contained racist content. On 23 January 1995, Defence Minister David Collenette ordered the regiment disbanded. One scholarly source gives 5 March 1995 for the formal disbandment, and Wikipedia gives 1 September 1995 for the regiment's end date.

### Cited Findings
- Two videos became public in January 1995. One was shot in early 1993 while the regiment was in Somalia. The other was a 1992 home video of a hazing ritual at CFB Petawawa, which included racist content and was made public the week after the first. [PRESS] — [UPI, 23 Jan 1995, "Disgraced airborne regiment disbanded"](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1995/01/23/Disgraced-airborne-regiment-disbanded/5605790837200/)
- On 23 January 1995, Defence Minister David Collenette ordered the regiment disbanded and its members returned to their home units. UPI called this the first time a Canadian regiment had been disbanded in disgrace. [PRESS] — [UPI, 23 Jan 1995](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1995/01/23/Disgraced-airborne-regiment-disbanded/5605790837200/); [1995 in Canada (Wikipedia, tertiary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_in_Canada)
- An article in *Canadian Military History* (Vol. 22, Iss. 4) gives 5 March 1995 as the date of disbandment. Wikipedia's article on the regiment gives 1 September 1995 as the end of its active service. These may refer to different formal steps (a disbandment parade versus the official end of the unit). [REF] — [Canadian Military History 22(4)](https://scholars.wlu.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1704&context=cmh); [Canadian Airborne Regiment (Wikipedia, tertiary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Airborne_Regiment)
- Maclean's covered the period (e.g., "Canada Notes", 20 Feb 1995; "Dubious Conduct", 16 Oct 1995). [PRESS] — [Maclean's archive](https://archive.macleans.ca/article/1995/2/20/canada-notes)

### Inferences
- The page can say: "Photographs taken by soldiers and, later, videos showing racist hazing rituals became public. In January 1995 the government decided to disband the regiment." No description is needed.
- A careful wording for the date would be "ordered disbanded on 23 January 1995; formally disbanded in March 1995" (for March, check the 5 March date against a DND or Library and Archives Canada source).

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the 1995 videos were first aired on CBC or CTV, or the exact date each aired. A search snippet says CBC Newsworld received a 1995 videotape leak.
- I did not find the date the Arone photographs first became public; an early press report, not the courts martial, is the likely source.

## 7. The Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia (Létourneau Commission)

### Takeaway
The government set up the Commission on 20 March 1995, after pressure on Defence Minister Collenette. It was chaired by Federal Court Justice Gilles Létourneau, with commissioners Peter Desbarats and Robert Rutherford. The government extended its deadline several times. Then, on 10 January 1997, Defence Minister Doug Young decided to end the hearings and set a final report date of 30 June 1997, rejecting the Commission's request to sit until at least the end of 1997. The Commission therefore could not look into the events in Somalia or senior decision-making in detail. Its report, *Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair*, was released on 2 July 1997. It blamed failures of leadership by senior officers and found a cover-up, including the tampering of documents that reached up to Chief of the Defence Staff Gen. Jean Boyle. It made 160 recommendations (one source says 157).

### Cited Findings
- The Commission was appointed on 20 March 1995. [OFFICIAL RECORD] — [Senate, Special Committee on the Canadian Airborne Regiment in Somalia, transcript](https://sencanada.ca/en/content/sen/committee/352/soma/02ev-e); [TCE "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)
- Chaired by Federal Court judge Gilles Létourneau, it held hearings from late 1995 until autumn 1996. [REF] — [TCE "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)
- The government extended the mandate to 28 June 1996, then to 31 March 1997, and finally to 30 June 1997. [OFFICIAL RECORD] — [House of Commons debates, 14 Feb 1997 (openparliament.ca)](https://openparliament.ca/debates/1997/2/14/?page=3)
- The Commission wrote that "The Minister's decision of January 10, 1997, eliminated any possibility" of taking its inquiry up the chain of command to its logical conclusion. The government rejected the Commission's request to extend to at least 31 December 1997. [COMMISSION] — [Somalia Inquiry report (copy)](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf)
- Why it was cut short: TCE ("Damning Report") says Defence Minister Doug Young decided to end the hearings after the commissioners said they could not hear critical witnesses within the deadline. The government cited cost and time; the inquiry had cost about $25 million. [REF] — [TCE "Somalia Inquiry's Damning Report"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report); [TCE "Somalia Affair"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)
- Young was widely criticized for refusing the extension. [PRESS] — [Maclean's, 14 July 1997, "The faces of Somalia"](https://archive.macleans.ca/article/1997/7/14/the-faces-of-somalia)
- Commissioner Peter Desbarats published *Somalia Cover-Up: A Commissioner's Journal* (1997). He called the shutdown unprecedented and an attack on the independence of public inquiries. [CLAIM by a commissioner] — [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Somalia_Cover_up.html?id=S1J4QgAACAAJ); see also [Alberta Law Review, "The Independence of Public Inquiries" (1997)](https://www.canlii.org/w/canlii/1997CanLIIDocs150.pdf)
- Report title and release: *Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair* (Report of the Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia; executive summary cat. no. CP32-66/1997). It was released on 2 July 1997 by Defence Minister Art Eggleton. - Main findings as summarized in search results:
  - Most soldiers did their best. The mission was badly prepared and "drifted" into disaster, which damaged a proud legacy. Systems broke down and organizational discipline collapsed.
