## 1. The fall of Barre, the USC split, the Mogadishu fighting and the March 1992 ceasefire

### Takeaway
On 26 or 27 January 1991, the United Somali Congress (USC) drove Siad Barre out of Mogadishu. Sources disagree on which day. Within days the USC split over who should lead: Ali Mahdi Mohamed (Abgal clan) declared himself interim president on 29 January 1991, and General Mohamed Farrah Aidid (Habr Gidir clan) objected. From November 1991 the two factions fought for the city. Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights estimated about 14,000 killed and 27,000 wounded by the end of February 1992. A UN-brokered ceasefire followed in March 1992.

### Cited Findings
- **Date Barre left: sources disagree.** The Inter-NGO Committee for Somalia (UK) 1991 report and The New Humanitarian chronology both give **27 January 1991**, the day Barre fled Mogadishu as USC forces took power. Other accounts say the USC stormed the presidential palace and Barre left on **26 January 1991**. — [INCS-UK 1991 report, Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/docid/3ae6a8074.html); [The New Humanitarian, Somalia chronology](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/54227/somalia-chronology-key-events-1990); 26 Jan given in [Tufts "Atrocity Endings": Somalia, fall of Siad Barre](https://sites.tufts.edu/atrocityendings/?p=50). History UK's "this day in history" entry files it under 27 January. — [History.co.uk](https://www.history.co.uk/this-day-in-history/27-january/somali-dictator-flees). **Flag: 26 vs 27 January.** A safe wording is "in the last days of January 1991 (26-27 January)". I could not reach a Britannica page to settle it.
- After leaving the capital, Barre twice failed to retake Mogadishu with forces from his own clan. He went first to his home region of Gedo, then to Kenya and Nigeria, and died in Lagos in 1995. — search-snippet summary of [BlackPast, Mohamed Siad Barre](https://new.blackpast.org/?p=40386) and [Guardian obituary repost](https://somaliarchive77.substack.com/p/siad-barre-obituary-architect-of) (verify wording). His forces regrouped as the Somali National Front (SNF) and advanced on Mogadishu again in March 1991. — [Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP), SNF - USC/SNA](https://ucdp.uu.se/nonstate/5438)
- The USC was "a largely Hawiye-based clan organization". Soon after Barre left it split openly into an **Abgal** faction led by **Ali Mahdi** and a **Habr Gedir** faction led by **General Aidid**. — [UCDP](https://ucdp.uu.se/nonstate/5438); [The New Humanitarian](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/54227/somalia-chronology-key-events-1990)
- **Ali Mahdi declared himself interim president on 29 January 1991**, and Aidid objected. — [The New Humanitarian chronology](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/54227/somalia-chronology-key-events-1990). Reuters archive footage title: "USC rebels hold Mogadishu / Ali Mahdi Mohamed becomes interim president". — [Reuters/Screenocean](https://reuters.screenocean.com/record/1030462)
- The rivalry was older. In June 1990, Ali Mahdi and the USC's Rome-based faction rejected Aidid's election as USC chairman. — search-snippet summary drawing on [Wikipedia, United Somali Congress](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/United_Somali_Congress) (encyclopedia mirror; verify)
- **Casualties in Mogadishu, November 1991 - February 1992.** Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights estimated **about 14,000 killed and 27,000 injured** in Mogadishu between November 1991 and the end of February 1992, most of them civilians. Rakiya Omaar, Africa Watch's executive director, gave the same figures ("at least 14,000 ... killed and 27,000 wounded") in *Current History* in May 1992. — [Africa Watch / PHR, "No Mercy in Mogadishu" (1992), via CJA](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/No%20Mercy%20for%20Mogadishu.pdf); [Human Rights Watch World Report 1993, Somalia](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm). **Flag:** a Wikipedia draft runs the period to March 1992 ("over 14,000 dead, according to Human Rights Watch"). The count covers Mogadishu only, killed plus wounded, and was published in 1992. — [Wikipedia draft, Somali Civil War (1991-1992)](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Draft:Somali_Civil_War_(1991%E2%80%931992))
- How the fighting went: Aidid's forces overran most of the city but could not dislodge Ali Mahdi's forces from their strongholds in the north. HRW says a lull followed the failure of a major Aidid attempt to overrun the Mahdi enclave at the end of February 1992, and that "in March and April, the fighting in Mogadishu began to subside somewhat, although chronic insecurity remained." — [HRW World Report 1993, Somalia](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm)
- **Ceasefire.** UN Security Council Resolution 733 (23 January 1992) and Resolution 746 (17 March 1992) came first. Resolution 746 backed a UN technical team for ceasefire monitoring. **Interim President Ali Mahdi signed Letters of Agreement on the mechanisms for monitoring the ceasefire on 28 March 1992, and General Aidid signed on 27 March 1992.** — [UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html) (snippet; also mirrored at [mineaction.org](https://mineaction.org/sites/default/files/past/unosom1backgr2.html)). **Gap/flag:** I found no source snippet confirming the commonly cited **3 March 1992** signing date of the ceasefire agreement itself, which was negotiated in New York in February 1992 and signed in Mogadishu. The 27 and 28 March dates are for the monitoring agreements, not the ceasefire.

### Inferences
- For students: "Barre fled the capital in late January 1991; the victors then fell out among themselves." This is accurate whichever January date is chosen.
- Aidid and Ali Mahdi were both from the Hawiye clan family but from different sub-clans (Habr Gidir and Abgal). That helps explain why the fight in Mogadishu was so bitter and why the city ended up divided north and south.

### Gaps
- An authoritative single date for Barre's departure. Britannica and the US Army Center of Military History (CMH) pages could not be reached.
- Primary confirmation of the 3 March 1992 ceasefire date.
- Neutral figures for fighting outside Mogadishu in 1991-92 (Kismayo, Bardera, SNF offensives).

## 2. The 1991-1992 famine: where, how many died, who estimated, and why

### Takeaway
The famine was worst in the farming and inter-river areas of southern Somalia. Baidoa became its best-known centre, and the Baidoa-Bardera area is often called the "triangle of death". Death estimates range from about 200,000 to 300,000 or more, depending on who counted and over which period. The main causes were war, looting of food and aid, and the destruction of farming, on top of drought.

### Cited Findings
- **UN estimate (1992):** "It was estimated that perhaps **300,000 people died since November 1991**, and at least **1.5 million lives were at immediate risk**." — [UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html) (snippet). This counts famine and related deaths from November 1991 onward. It is a UN Secretariat figure from 1992; the exact date of the statement is not in the snippet.
- **Range in reference works:** "an estimated **200,000-300,000** deaths", with a note that the toll "is more difficult to estimate than with most famines." — [Wikipedia, 1992 famine in Somalia](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia) (encyclopedia; secondary)
- **Refugee Policy Group (November 1994):** estimated that **about 100,000 lives were saved** by international aid, and credited the US airlift (August to December 1992) with saving **about 40,000** of them. This counts lives saved, not deaths. Pakistani Brigadier-General Imtiaz Shaheen, head of the first UN troop contingent, disputed the estimate. — [Wikipedia, 1992 famine in Somalia](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia) (secondary report of the RPG study; verify against the RPG paper). **Flag:** I could not confirm the RPG death toll that is often quoted (about 220,000 or 240,000). The only RPG figure I found is the lives-saved estimate.
- **Baidoa (CDC survey, 1992).** Baidoa's estimated population fell from **37,000 in early August 1992 to about 21,000 by 20 November 1992**. Among displaced people in Baidoa, the average crude death rate was **16.8 per 10,000 per day** (95% CI 14.6-19.1) over the preceding 232 days. Weekly deaths in the town fell from about **1,700 in September to about 300 by mid-November 1992**. — [US CDC, MMWR (1992), "Population-based mortality assessment - Baidoa and Afgoi, Somalia"](https://www.cdc.gov/MMWR/preview/mmwrhtml/00018099.htm). For comparison, 1 death per 10,000 per day is the usual emergency threshold (my note; not from the snippet).
- A peer-reviewed study of displaced and resident populations in central Somalia during the 1992 famine is listed by the Burnet Institute (Moore et al., published in *The Lancet*, 1993). — [Burnet Institute summary](https://burnet.edu.au/knowledge-and-media/research-articles/mortality-rates-in-displaced-and-resident-populations-of-central-somalia-during-1992-famine). I could not see its figures.
- **HRW on causes:** Human Rights Watch's 1993 World Report on Somalia describes famine driven by the war, with food looted and fought over. — [HRW World Report 1993, Somalia](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm). I could not read the exact wording; only the casualty and fighting passages came through.

### Inferences
- When the page gives a death toll, it should name the source and the scope, for example: "UN officials estimated in 1992 that about 300,000 people had died since November 1991; later estimates range from about 200,000 to 300,000."
- The CDC figures for Baidoa show the death rate falling sharply between September and November 1992. Relief was reaching the area before UNITAF arrived in December, which matters when crediting UNITAF alone.

### Gaps
- An ICRC statement or figure. No ICRC famine-death estimate showed up in the searches. The ICRC was the largest relief food distributor in 1992, but I have no citable snippet for that.
- The origin of the phrase "triangle of death" (Baidoa-Bardera-Hoddur area) in a citable source.
- Bardera-specific mortality figures.
- How much the drought contributed compared with the war, in a citable source.

## 3. UNOSOM I (Resolution 751) and its limits

### Takeaway
Security Council Resolution 751 (24 April 1992) set up UNOSOM I, beginning with 50 unarmed military observers to watch the Mogadishu ceasefire. In August 1992 Resolution 775 enlarged it, but in practice only a single Pakistani battalion of about 500 arrived, in September 1992. It depended on faction consent and could not secure food deliveries.

### Cited Findings
- **Resolution 751, adopted 24 April 1992:** it established the UN Operation in Somalia (UNOSOM) and asked the Secretary-General to deploy **50 observers immediately to monitor the ceasefire in Mogadishu**. — [UN Digital Library, S/RES/751 (1992)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/141599/files/S_RES_751%281992%29-EN.pdf); [Security Council Report](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/un-documents/document/somalia-s-res-751.php); [UN Peacekeeping UNOSOM I page](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html)
- Canada's DND summary of UNOSOM I gives the mandate as monitoring the ceasefire in Mogadishu and providing protection and security for UN personnel, equipment and humanitarian assistance. — [Canada DND, Op CORDON / UNOSOM I](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/cordon.html)
- **Resolution 775 (28 August 1992)** expanded UNOSOM I's mandate and strength to protect convoys and distribution centres across Somalia. — [UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I mandate](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/unosom1mandate.html). Authorized strength later rose to **4,219 troops and 50 military observers**. — snippet from the same UN mandate page (verify). **Flag:** one search snippet tied the 3,500 security personnel to Resolution 746. That is almost certainly a mistake in the secondary page, since 746 dealt only with a technical team. The 3,500 figure belongs to the August 1992 expansion (Resolution 775). Verify on the UN page.
- **Pakistani battalion:** sources give different arrival dates. One says 500 Pakistani troops arrived in Mogadishu on **14 September 1992**. A dated news chronology says the final part of the **500-man Pakistani infantry battalion arrived on 28 September 1992**. — [Valka.cz overview (secondary)](https://en.valka.cz/topic/view/47470); [Deseret News chronology, 9 Dec 1992](https://www.deseret.com/1992/12/9/19020446/somalia-chronology/). The battalion's job was to secure Mogadishu airport for relief flights. — same sources.
- **Consent problem:** the UN envoy Mohamed Sahnoun's August 1992 arrangement with Aidid reportedly allowed 500 UN troops, on condition that any more needed the agreement of Aidid's faction. — search-snippet summary of secondary pages ([Wikipedia, UNOSOM I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Operation_in_Somalia_I)); verify.
- **Limits in practice:** the Pakistani troops were threatened and isolated by the warring parties at Mogadishu airport, and a Pakistani peacekeeper was shot when his vehicle was hijacked. — snippet from secondary sources in the same search ([Valka.cz](https://en.valka.cz/topic/view/47470)); verify. **Flag:** I found no reliable source saying they were "confined" to the airport, although that word appears in popular accounts.
- **US Operation Provide Relief** airlifted food from August to December 1992 (dates as given in the RPG lives-saved estimate above). — [Wikipedia, 1992 famine in Somalia](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia)

### Inferences
- UNOSOM I was a traditional consent-based peacekeeping mission in a country with no government to give consent. Its troops were too few and too dependent on faction approval to stop the looting of aid. That gap is what led to the Chapter VII decision in December 1992.

### Gaps
- Full text of the UN Blue Book (*The United Nations and Somalia 1992-1996*) was not reachable.
- The exact number of Pakistani troops deployed by November 1992, from a primary source.

## 4. UNITAF / Operation Restore Hope: Resolution 794 to the handover of 4 May 1993

### Takeaway
On 3 December 1992 the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 794. Acting under Chapter VII, it authorized member states to use "all necessary means" to create a secure environment for humanitarian relief. US forces landed in Mogadishu on 9 December 1992, under US Marine Lieutenant General Robert B. Johnston. At its peak UNITAF had roughly 32,000-38,000 troops from 21-24 nations; sources disagree on both figures. Southern Somalia was divided into humanitarian relief sectors (HRS), usually given as nine. UNOSOM II took over military command on 4 May 1993.

### Cited Findings
- **Resolution 794, adopted 3 December 1992, at the Council's 3145th meeting, by 15 votes to 0.** "Acting under Chapter VII", it authorized the Secretary-General and cooperating member states "to use all necessary means to establish as soon as possible a secure environment for humanitarian relief operations in Somalia." — [UN resolution text, Security Council Report PDF](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/atf/cf/{65BFCF9B-6D27-4E9C-8CD3-CF6E4FF96FF9}/Chap%20VII%20SRES%20794.pdf); [Refworld copy](https://www.refworld.org/node/80089); [University of Minnesota Human Rights Library copy](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/peace/docs/scres794.html)
- It is described as the first Security Council resolution to authorize force under Chapter VII to deliver humanitarian aid. — [ICRC Casebook, "UN forces in Somalia"](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/un-un-forces-somalia) (snippet; secondary commentary)
- **Landing and dates:** UNITAF existed "from 9 December 1992 through 4 May 1993". — [Marine Corps Gazette, "Operation Restore Hope in Somalia"](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/operation-restore-hope-in-somalia/)
- **Command:** US and allied forces were designated the Unified Task Force (UNITAF) and placed under **Lieutenant General Robert B. Johnston, USMC**, commander of I Marine Expeditionary Force. — [Marine Corps Gazette](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/operation-restore-hope-in-somalia/); [USMC History Division, *Restoring Hope: In Somalia with the Unified Task Force 1992-1993*](https://www.usmcu.edu/Portals/218/Restoring%20Hope.pdf)
- **Size and nations: sources disagree.**
  - "More than **38,000 troops from 21 nations** (including **28,000 Americans**)". — [Marine Corps Gazette](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/operation-restore-hope-in-somalia/)
  - "A coalition of **23 nations**, ultimately approximately **32,000 troops**". — [US Air Force History, Haulman, *The USAF in Somalia 1992-1995*](https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/Airmen-at-War/Haulman-USAFSomalia1992-1995.pdf)
  - "UNITAF had deployed approximately **37,000 troops** in southern and central Somalia". — [ICRC Casebook](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/un-un-forces-somalia)
  - **Flag:** the figure of 24 nations, often seen online, did not appear in these sources. Suggested wording: "about 37,000 troops (sources range from 32,000 to over 38,000), roughly two-thirds or more of them American, from over 20 countries".
- **Humanitarian relief sectors (HRS).** UNITAF divided southern Somalia, the area worst hit by drought and famine, into **nine humanitarian relief sectors** that served both for relief distribution and as military areas of responsibility. — [US Army CMH / AUSA edition, *The United States Army in Somalia 1992-1994*](https://www.ausa.org/sites/default/files/SR-2002-The-US-Army-in-Somalia.pdf); [Joint History Office, *The Effort to Save Somalia*](https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/History/Monographs/Somalia.pdf) (snippets). **Flag:** an Italian military history volume refers to **eight** HRS. Some early plans had eight, with a ninth added later. — [Italian MoD, *Le Operazioni Interforze e Multinazionali* ACTA Tomo II, p.81](https://musei.difesa.it/allegati/Atti%202013%20Le%20Operazioni%20Interforze%20e%20Multinazionali%20nella%20Storia%20Militare%20-%20Torino%20tomo%20II/files/basic-html/page81.html). UNITAF Fragmentary Order 9 (19 December 1992) set up nine main supply routes linking the sectors. — [USMC, *Restoring Hope*](https://www.usmcu.edu/Portals/218/Restoring%20Hope.pdf) (snippet)
- **Who held which sector (partial, from snippets):**
  - US Marines: **Bardera (Baardheere)** and parts of **Mogadishu**. US Army: security, often with allies, in **Baidoa, Baledogle (Balli Doogle), Merca (Marka) and Kismayo**. — [US Army in Somalia 1992-1994 (AUSA/CMH)](https://www.ausa.org/sites/default/files/SR-2002-The-US-Army-in-Somalia.pdf)
  - **Australia:** 1st Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment ran the **Baidoa** HRS (about 17,000 square kilometres), January to May 1993. — [Australian War Memorial, UNITAF 1992-1993](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84821)
  - **Canada: Belet Huen (Belet Uen / Beledweyne). Italy: Gialalassi. France: Oddur.** — [CSIS study on Somalia (2009)](https://csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/legacy_files/files/media/csis/pubs/090130_somalia_study.pdf) (snippet; spelling "Belen Uen" in source)
  - Italian forces are also described as setting up the **Merca** sector. — [USMC, *Restoring Hope*](https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/Restoring%20Hope%20In%20Somalia%20with%20the%20Unified%20Task%20Force%201992-1993%20PCN%2019000413500_5.pdf) (snippet)
  - **Belgium: Kismayo** is widely reported, but **I could not confirm it** in this session. The snippet mentioned Belgium only as a contributor. Flag for verification.
- **Resolution 814 (26 March 1993):** acting under Chapter VII, it expanded UNOSOM's size and mandate (UNOSOM II) and asked for a "prompt, smooth and phased transition from UNITAF to UNOSOM II". — [UN Digital Library, S/RES/814 (1993)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/164678/files/S_RES_814%281993%29-EN.pdf); [UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM II background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom2backgr2.html)
- **Handover: sources disagree on the date.** The UN says the Secretary-General proposed **1 May 1993** for transferring budgetary and administrative control, and that **military command transferred on 4 May 1993**. — [UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM II background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom2backgr2.html). HRW's 1994 report says UNITAF "was replaced, on May 1" by UNOSOM II. — [HRW World Report 1994, Somalia (Refworld)](https://www.refworld.org/node/22046). Use **4 May 1993** for the military handover. UNOSOM II's force commander was Turkish Lieutenant General Çevik Bir. — [UN Peacekeeping UNOSOM II pages](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom2backgr2.html) (snippet; verify)

### Inferences
- UNITAF was a US-led coalition authorized by the UN, not a UN-commanded force. Johnston reported through US channels. That difference sets up the change in May 1993, when command passed to a UN force with a much wider and more ambitious mandate under Resolution 814.

### Gaps
- A full, authoritative table of all nine HRS and their lead nations. The US Army CMH book (history.army.mil) was not reachable. The likely full list is Mogadishu, Baledogle, Baidoa, Bardera, Kismayo, Merca, Oddur, Gialalassi and Belet Uen; only parts of it are confirmed above.
- The full list of contributing nations.
- An official peak-strength date.

## 5. Canada's part: Operation Deliverance

### Takeaway
Operation Deliverance was Canada's contribution to UNITAF. Its main element was the Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group (CARBG, about 900, mostly paratroopers), based in the Belet Huen sector of west-central Somalia. It was supported by HMCS Preserver offshore, Hercules transports and a helicopter flight. Total Canadian strength is commonly given as about 1,400. When UNITAF ended on 4 May 1993, the Canadian mission came under UN command.

### Cited Findings
- **Units (Canada DND official history):** the Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group, an airlift control element with Hercules aircraft, and 93 Rotary Wing Aviation Flight. The **CARBG numbered about 900**, most of them from the Canadian Airborne Regiment. The **Royal Canadian Dragoons supplied about 130** soldiers ("A" and Recce Squadrons), and **1st Battalion, Royal Canadian Regiment** supplied a mortar company. **93 RWAF had 89 personnel** from 427 Tactical Helicopter Squadron, Petawawa. — [Canada DND, Op DELIVERANCE](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/deliverance.html) (snippet; the page could not be fetched)
- **Base:** the CARBG operated "out of Belet Huen in west-central Somalia", delivering humanitarian aid and disarming local militias. — [Canada DND, Op DELIVERANCE](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/deliverance.html)
- **Total strength:** "about **1,400 Canadian troops**, a helicopter unit and the supply ship HMCS Preserver"; mission start given as **3 December 1992**. — [Wikipedia, Operation Deliverance](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Operation_Deliverance) (encyclopedia; secondary). **Flag:** the DND snippet itemizes only about 900 plus about 89 (plus aircrew). The 1,400 total probably includes support elements and the ship's company, but this is unverified. The "3 December" start matches the date of Resolution 794, not the troops' arrival. An academic article says three Airborne commandos and support units deployed in **January 1993**. — [Journal of Military and Strategic Studies](https://jmss.org/article/download/78424/57254) (snippet). Suggested wording: "Canada committed about 1,400 personnel in December 1992; most of the battle group arrived in Somalia in late December 1992 and January 1993" (verify the arrival dates).
- **HMCS Preserver:** a supply ship that took part from **December 1992**, stationed offshore. Canadian helicopters ferried cargo and personnel between the ship and shore, and carried out many medical evacuations. — [Royal Canadian Navy, HMCS Preserver history](https://www.canada.ca/en/navy/services/history/ships-histories/preserver.html); [Veterans Affairs Canada, Peacekeeping in Somalia](https://veterans.gc.ca/en/remembrance/military-history/peacekeeping/somalia). I found no number for the ship's crew on this deployment.
- **Change to UN command:** Operation Deliverance was Canada's part of the US-led Operation Restore Hope, and "on 4 May 1993 it came under UN command" (UNOSOM II). — search snippet drawing on [Wikipedia, Somalia affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair) and [Canada DND, Op DELIVERANCE (UNOSOM II)](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/deliverance.html). The DND page is titled "United Nations Operations in Somalia II (UNOSOM II) - DELIVERANCE", which matches this.
- **Official record:** the Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia published its report in 1997 as *Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair*. Volume 3 covers the planning of Operation Deliverance and the change of mission when the battle group arrived in Belet Huen. — [publications.gc.ca, Somalia Inquiry vol.](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2015/bcp-pco/CP32-64-3-1997-eng.pdf); [Walter Dorn mirror of Vol. 3](https://www.walterdorn.net/pdf/Somalia-Inquiry_Vol-3_ReducedSize_CP32-65-1997-3-eng.pdf). Note for the writer: the inquiry dealt with serious misconduct by some soldiers in 1993, the "Somalia affair" ([Canadian Encyclopedia](https://prod-front.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)). That falls in the March 1993 period, so it is inside this timeframe, but it may be out of scope for this page.

### Inferences
- Canada's original UNOSOM I commitment (Op CORDON, peacekeeping) was redirected into UNITAF's Chapter VII mission. The Somalia Inquiry's discussion of the "change in mission" supports this, but the snippet does not give details.

### Gaps
- An official total for Canadian personnel, and exact arrival dates in Belet Huen. The DND page and Somalia Inquiry PDFs could not be opened.
- The size of HMCS Preserver's crew and the dates it was on station.
- The date the CARBG left Somalia (probably June 1993, which is outside this strand's period).
