# A famine rescue turns into urban war

The Battle of Mogadishu on 3-4 October 1993 was the end point of a chain that began with a relief mission. When Siad Barre's government fell in January 1991, the victors split, Mogadishu became a battlefield, and war-driven famine killed a number the UN put at **about 300,000 by late 1992** ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html)). A small, consent-based UN force (UNOSOM I) could not protect food deliveries, so in December 1992 the Security Council authorised a US-led coalition, UNITAF, to use force to secure them ([S/RES/794](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/peace/docs/scres794.html)). Canada's part, Operation Deliverance at Belet Huen, produced the Somalia Affair: two Somali deaths at the hands of Canadian soldiers in March 1993, courts martial, a cover-up that a national inquiry later documented, and the disbandment of the Canadian Airborne Regiment in 1995 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)). It happened about 300 km and six months away from the battle, under a different mission. After UNITAF handed over to the larger, more ambitious UNOSOM II in May 1993, an attack on Pakistani peacekeepers on 5 June turned the UN into a party hunting General Aidid's Somali National Alliance (SNA). That hunt brought US Task Force Ranger to Mogadishu and, on 3 October, a raid that left **18 Americans, one Malaysian and an unknown number of Somalis (estimates run from about 133 to over 1,000) dead** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Battle-of-Mogadishu)). The battle led to a US withdrawal by March 1994 and the end of UNOSOM II in March 1995. The 2001 film *Black Hawk Down*, shot in Rabat and Salé in Morocco, keeps the battle's sequence but shows it almost entirely from the American side. Every fact in this report comes from search-result snippets rather than full pages, and needs checking before publication (see the last two sections).

## Barre's fall split Mogadishu and starved the south (1991-92)

Barre's regime collapsed in **the last days of January 1991**. Sources disagree on whether he fled Mogadishu on 26 or 27 January: the Inter-NGO Committee for Somalia and *The New Humanitarian* give 27 January, while a Tufts University study gives 26 January ([Refworld, INCS-UK report](https://www.refworld.org/docid/3ae6a8074.html); [The New Humanitarian chronology](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/54227/somalia-chronology-key-events-1990); [Tufts, Atrocity Endings](https://sites.tufts.edu/atrocityendings/?p=50)). The page can safely say "late January 1991". The winning movement, the United Somali Congress (USC), was largely Hawiye, and it broke apart almost at once. **Ali Mahdi Mohamed** of the Abgal sub-clan declared himself interim president on 29 January 1991, and **General Mohamed Farrah Aidid** of the Habr Gidir sub-clan rejected him ([UCDP](https://ucdp.uu.se/nonstate/5438); [The New Humanitarian](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/54227/somalia-chronology-key-events-1990)). Both men came from the same clan family, which helps explain why Mogadishu ended up divided between a Mahdi north and an Aidid south. Barre's own forces regrouped as the Somali National Front and twice tried to retake the capital before he went into exile. He died in Lagos in 1995 ([UCDP](https://ucdp.uu.se/nonstate/5438); [BlackPast](https://new.blackpast.org/?p=40386)).

From November 1991 the two USC factions fought for the city. **Africa Watch and Physicians for Human Rights estimated about 14,000 killed and 27,000 wounded in Mogadishu between November 1991 and the end of February 1992**, most of them civilians ([Africa Watch/PHR, *No Mercy in Mogadishu*](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/No%20Mercy%20for%20Mogadishu.pdf); [HRW World Report 1993](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm)). The figure covers Mogadishu only and was made during the war. Human Rights Watch reported that the fighting began to subside in March and April 1992, after a failed Aidid assault on the Mahdi enclave at the end of February ([HRW World Report 1993](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm)). The UN record shows Aidid signing the agreements on monitoring a ceasefire on **27 March 1992** and Ali Mahdi on **28 March 1992** ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html)). The commonly quoted date of 3 March 1992 for the ceasefire itself was not found in any snippet.

The famine was worst in the farming and inter-river lands of the south, with Baidoa its best-known centre. Death tolls depend on who counted and for which period. **UN officials estimated in 1992 that "perhaps 300,000 people died since November 1991" and that at least 1.5 million lives were at immediate risk** ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom1backgr2.html)). Reference works give 200,000-300,000 ([Wikipedia, 1992 famine in Somalia](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia), secondary). The most solid local data come from a **US Centers for Disease Control survey in Baidoa**: displaced people there had a crude death rate of **16.8 per 10,000 per day** over 232 days, and weekly deaths in the town fell from about **1,700 in September to about 300 by mid-November 1992** ([CDC, MMWR 1992](https://www.cdc.gov/MMWR/preview/mmwrhtml/00018099.htm)). This matters for fairness: death rates were falling before UNITAF landed, so relief already under way deserves part of the credit. The Refugee Policy Group later estimated that international aid saved about 100,000 lives. That is a lives-saved figure, not a death toll, and a Pakistani UN commander disputed it ([Wikipedia, 1992 famine](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia), secondary report). Human Rights Watch described the famine as driven by war, with food looted and fought over ([HRW World Report 1993](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/WR93/Afw-08.htm)), but no snippet allowed a sourced statement on how much drought added.

## Five hundred peacekeepers gave way to a 37,000-strong coalition

The UN's first answer was small. **Resolution 751 (24 April 1992)** created the UN Operation in Somalia (UNOSOM) and sent **50 unarmed observers** to watch the Mogadishu ceasefire ([S/RES/751](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/141599/files/S_RES_751%281992%29-EN.pdf)). Resolution 775 (28 August 1992) enlarged the mandate to protecting convoys and distribution centres ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM I mandate](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/unosom1mandate.html)). In practice, though, only **one Pakistani battalion of about 500** arrived, in September 1992, to secure Mogadishu airport; sources give 14 or 28 September ([Deseret News chronology](https://www.deseret.com/1992/12/9/19020446/somalia-chronology/); [Valka.cz](https://en.valka.cz/topic/view/47470), secondary). The force depended on the factions' consent in a country with no government to give it, and it could not stop the looting of aid. The US ran a separate food airlift, Operation Provide Relief, from August 1992 ([Wikipedia, 1992 famine](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/1992_famine_in_Somalia), secondary).

The turning point was **Resolution 794 of 3 December 1992, adopted 15-0**. Acting under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, it authorised member states "to use all necessary means to establish as soon as possible a secure environment for humanitarian relief operations in Somalia" ([S/RES/794, UMN Human Rights Library](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/peace/docs/scres794.html)). The ICRC Casebook describes it as the first Security Council authorisation of force to deliver humanitarian aid ([ICRC Casebook](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/un-un-forces-somalia)). The result, the **Unified Task Force (UNITAF, "Operation Restore Hope")**, was US-led and UN-authorised but not UN-commanded. It existed from **9 December 1992 to 4 May 1993** under US Marine Lieutenant General Robert B. Johnston ([Marine Corps Gazette](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/operation-restore-hope-in-somalia/); [USMC, *Restoring Hope*](https://www.usmcu.edu/Portals/218/Restoring%20Hope.pdf)). Its size depends on the source:

| Who counted | Peak troops | Nations |
|---|---|---|
| *Marine Corps Gazette* | "more than 38,000" (28,000 US) | 21 |
| US Air Force History Office (Haulman) | about 32,000 | 23 |
| ICRC Casebook | about 37,000 | — |

Sources: [Marine Corps Gazette](https://www.mca-marines.org/gazette/operation-restore-hope-in-somalia/); [Haulman, *The USAF in Somalia*](https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/Airmen-at-War/Haulman-USAFSomalia1992-1995.pdf); [ICRC Casebook](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/un-un-forces-somalia). Suggested page wording: "about 37,000 troops (counts range from 32,000 to over 38,000), most of them American, from over 20 countries."

UNITAF divided the south into **nine humanitarian relief sectors**, each with lead nations. The US Army and Joint Staff histories give nine; an Italian defence ministry volume gives eight ([AUSA edition of the CMH history](https://www.ausa.org/sites/default/files/SR-2002-The-US-Army-in-Somalia.pdf); [Joint History Office](https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/History/Monographs/Somalia.pdf); [Italian MoD](https://musei.difesa.it/allegati/Atti%202013%20Le%20Operazioni%20Interforze%20e%20Multinazionali%20nella%20Storia%20Militare%20-%20Torino%20tomo%20II/files/basic-html/page81.html)). The snippets confirm US Marines at Bardera and in Mogadishu, Australians at Baidoa, Canada at Belet Huen, Italy at Gialalassi and France at Oddur ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84821); [CSIS 2009](https://csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/legacy_files/files/media/csis/pubs/090130_somalia_study.pdf)). Belgium at Kismayo is widely stated but was not confirmed.

**Canada's Operation Deliverance** was its contribution to UNITAF. According to the Department of National Defence, the **Canadian Airborne Regiment Battle Group numbered about 900**, mostly paratroopers, with about 130 Royal Canadian Dragoons, a mortar company from 1st Battalion, The Royal Canadian Regiment, and an 89-strong helicopter flight from 427 Squadron. It operated "out of Belet Huen in west-central Somalia", delivering aid and disarming militias ([DND, Op DELIVERANCE](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/deliverance.html)). The supply ship HMCS Preserver stayed offshore from December 1992 ([Royal Canadian Navy](https://www.canada.ca/en/navy/services/history/ships-histories/preserver.html)). The total usually quoted is **about 1,400 Canadians** ([Canadian Military Journal](http://www.journal.forces.gc.ca/Vol17/no4/page35-eng.asp); [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canadian-peacekeepers-in-somalia)). The DND itemisation does not add up to that, and the gap is probably support and ship's crew; this is unconfirmed. The start date is also unsettled. Wikipedia's 3 December 1992 is the date of Resolution 794, the *Canadian Military Journal* gives 19 December for the sector assignment, and an academic article has the commandos deploying in January 1993 ([CMJ](http://www.journal.forces.gc.ca/Vol17/no4/page35-eng.asp); [JMSS](https://jmss.org/article/download/78424/57254)). The soldiers rebuilt roads and bridges, cleared mines, reopened hospitals and escorted convoys ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canadian-peacekeepers-in-somalia)). The Somalia Inquiry itself found that most soldiers did their best in a mission that was badly prepared ([Inquiry Executive Summary](https://walterdorn.org/pdf/Somalia-Inquiry_Exec-Summary_ReducedSize_CP32-66-1997-eng.pdf)). That balance should frame the next section.

## Belet Huen, March 1993: two deaths that ended a regiment

### 4 March: a shooting the inquiry called a cover-up

On the night of **4 March 1993**, soldiers of the Airborne's reconnaissance platoon under **Captain Michel Rainville** shot two Somali men near the Canadian compound at Belet Huen. **Ahmed Arush** (also spelled Achmed Aruush) was killed and **Abdi Hunde Bei Sabrie** was wounded ([Wikipedia, Michel Rainville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rainville), tertiary; [Wikipedia, Somalia affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalia_affair), tertiary). A Canadian Forces surgeon, **Major Barry Armstrong**, raised concerns about how Arush died and became a whistle-blower. A 1994 pathology report by Dr James Ferris disputed his findings ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-military-doctor-barry-armstrong-blew-the-whistle-on-soldiers-crimes-in/); [CMAJ, 1998](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/cmaj/158/2/244.full.pdf)). The page should say only that the medical evidence was disputed. The first inquiry was a unit-level summary investigation, which found the shooting within the rules of engagement ([Queen's CIDP, *Crisis to Catalyst*](https://www.queensu.ca/cidp/sites/cidpwww/files/uploaded_files/OP%2070%20online.pdf)). The national inquiry later concluded, in words worth quoting briefly, that "the chain of command covered up its undeniable responsibility for the March 4 incident" ([Somalia Inquiry report, copy](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf)). Rainville was acquitted, and according to one source nobody was convicted over Arush's death ([Queen's CIDP](https://www.queensu.ca/cidp/sites/cidpwww/files/uploaded_files/OP%2070%20online.pdf)). A later, unrelated case involving Rainville appears in search results and must not be linked to Somalia. The inquiry tied the handling of 4 March directly to what happened twelve days later ([Inquiry report, copy](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf)).

### 16 March: the death of Shidane Arone

On the night of **16 March 1993**, **Shidane Abukar Arone, aged 16**, died in Canadian custody at the Belet Huen camp after being beaten and tortured ([CBC News, 2008](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411); [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)). Under the owner's rules the page states this once and goes no further: no method, no injuries, and none of the photographs soldiers took that night. Why he was detained (reportedly caught inside the compound) was not confirmed from a primary source. The researchers found **no published statement from the Arone or Arush families** in the material they could reach. That is a real gap for a page meant to be fair to the victims, and it is worth a search of the Canadian press archives from 1993-97.

### The courts martial: heaviest sentences at the bottom

Master Corporal **Clayton Matchee** was charged with second-degree murder and torture. While in custody he attempted suicide, which left him with a permanent brain injury. He was found unfit to stand trial (reported as 25 April 1994; check), found unfit again in 2004, and in 2008 the Department of National Defence withdrew the charges because, a prosecutor said, he "will never be fit to stand trial" ([CBC News, 2008](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/charges-withdrawn-for-former-canadian-soldier-accused-of-killing-somali-teen-1.708411); [CBC, 2004](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/military-won-t-drop-charges-against-matchee-1.512515); [CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/mobile/charges-dropped-against-soldier-in-somali-death-1.325044)). Because he was never tried, the page must not describe him as guilty. In 2017 his wife told CBC that the antimalarial drug mefloquine had driven him "to madness" ([CBC, 2017](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatoon/clayton-matchee-s-wife-wants-to-reopen-somalia-inquiry-1.4258889)). That is her claim; no court and not the inquiry made any finding on it. The date of the suicide attempt (often given as 19 March 1993) was not confirmed.

### Disbandment and the cut-short inquiry

Photographs from 16 March, and in January 1995 two videos (one shot in Somalia in 1993, and a 1992 hazing video from CFB Petawawa with racist content), turned the affair into a national scandal. On **23 January 1995** Defence Minister David Collenette ordered the Canadian Airborne Regiment disbanded. UPI called it the first Canadian regiment disbanded in disgrace ([UPI, 23 January 1995](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1995/01/23/Disgraced-airborne-regiment-disbanded/5605790837200/)). The formal end is dated **5 March 1995** in *Canadian Military History* and 1 September 1995 on Wikipedia, which may be two different formal steps ([Canadian Military History 22(4)](https://scholars.wlu.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1704&context=cmh)). Suggested wording: "ordered disbanded on 23 January 1995; formally disbanded in March 1995".

The **Commission of Inquiry into the Deployment of Canadian Forces to Somalia**, chaired by Federal Court Justice **Gilles Létourneau** with Peter Desbarats and Robert Rutherford, was appointed on **20 March 1995** ([Senate committee transcript](https://sencanada.ca/en/content/sen/committee/352/soma/02ev-e)). Its deadline was extended to 28 June 1996, then 31 March 1997, then 30 June 1997 ([House of Commons debates, 14 February 1997](https://openparliament.ca/debates/1997/2/14/?page=3)). On **10 January 1997** Defence Minister Doug Young ended the hearings and refused the commission's request to sit until at least the end of 1997. The commission wrote that this decision "eliminated any possibility" of following its inquiry up the chain of command, so the events in Somalia themselves, including Arone's death, were never examined in full ([Inquiry report, copy](https://nkitson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/somalia-inquiry-report1.pdf); [TCE](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-affair)). The government cited time and cost (about $25 million). Critics, including Commissioner Desbarats in *Somalia Cover-Up: A Commissioner's Journal*, called the shutdown an attack on the independence of public inquiries ([TCE, Damning Report](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Somalia_Cover_up.html?id=S1J4QgAACAAJ)). Both views should be given, each attributed.

The report, ***Dishonoured Legacy: The Lessons of the Somalia Affair***, was released on **2 July 1997** ([Government of Canada Publications](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.831108/publication.html)). As summarised in the snippets, it blamed a failure of leadership by senior officers. It criticised senior leaders for altering documents, stonewalling and misleading testimony, and found that General Jean Boyle, later Chief of the Defence Staff, "was a party to the decision to informally release altered documents" under an Access to Information request ([Gale/press summary](https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?asid=0cd36777&id=GALE%7CA310555592&it=r&p=AONE&sid=sitemap&u=googlescholar&v=2.1); [TCE](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/somalia-inquirys-damning-report)). The number of recommendations is given as 160 or 157. The page must keep three things apart: the **courts martial** decided individual guilt, the **commission** made findings about leadership and the cover-up but convicted no one, and the **press** reported events and later claims.

## From peacekeeping to manhunt, May to October 1993

**Resolution 814 (26 March 1993)**, again under Chapter VII, created **UNOSOM II**. It was a much larger UN-commanded force with the right to use force, and a mandate that included disarming the factions and rebuilding Somali institutions ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM II](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom2backgr2.html); [ICRC Casebook](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/un-un-forces-somalia)). How far that disarmament could be enforced is disputed; one legal analysis says it was not meant to be done by force ([UvA chapter](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/33357788/law_9780198784357_chapter_40.pdf)). UNITAF handed over on **4 May 1993** ([Clinton letter to Congress, 10 June 1993](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1993/06/1993-06-10-president-letter-to-congress-on-somalia.html)). The command was split. Turkish **Lieutenant General Çevik Bir** commanded the UN force, retired US Admiral **Jonathan Howe** was the Secretary-General's Special Representative, and US General **Thomas Montgomery** was both Bir's deputy and commander of US forces ([PBS Frontline, Montgomery interview](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/ambush/interviews/montgomery.html)). About **1,100 US troops** stayed as a Quick Reaction Force under US Central Command, not the UN ([Clinton letter](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1993/06/1993-06-10-president-letter-to-congress-on-somalia.html)). That double chain of command explains much of the later friction.

On **5 June 1993**, Pakistani peacekeepers were attacked after inspecting SNA weapons sites, including the one at Aidid's Radio Mogadishu. Casualty figures differ by source:

| Who counted | Pakistani killed | Wounded |
|---|---|---|
| President Clinton, letter to Congress (June 1993) | 23 | — |
| US State Department, 1993 human-rights report | 24 | about 50 |
| Canadian Immigration and Refugee Board chronology (1994) | at least 22 | over 50 |

Sources: [American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/letter-congressional-leaders-somalia); [State Dept via Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/usdos/1994/en/25187); [IRB via Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1994/en/93832). Somali casualties that day are undocumented. An independent expert's investigation, attached to the Secretary-General's report of 24 August 1993, found "clear and convincing" evidence that Aidid authorised the attack ([UN SC Repertoire 1993-95](https://www.un.org/fr/sc/repertoire/93-95/CHAPTER%208/AFRICA/item%2006-%20Somalia.pdf)). The expert is usually named as Tom Farer, but only secondary sources say so. Aidid denied responsibility ([State Dept via Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/usdos/1994/en/25187)). MERIP judged his responsibility "more than likely" but noted that UN reprisals began before any investigation ([MERIP, November 1993](https://www.merip.org/1993/11/from-peacekeeping-to-peace-enforcement/)). A claim that a separate inquiry found the attack spontaneous rests on a blog and should not be used.

**Resolution 837 (6 June 1993)** condemned the attack and called for the arrest and prosecution of those responsible. Secondary sources say it named the SNA but not Aidid personally ([IRB chronology](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1994/en/93832)). Its exact wording ("all necessary measures") was not read from the UN text. Strikes on SNA sites in Mogadishu began on 12 June ([UvA chapter](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/33357788/law_9780198784357_chapter_40.pdf)). On 13 June, Pakistani UN troops reportedly shot dead about 20 Somali demonstrators, according to Amnesty International ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/nws11/064/1993/en/)). Admiral Howe issued an arrest warrant for Aidid with a **$25,000 reward**, dated 17 June by Wikipedia and 19 June by one college page; *Time* confirms the amount ([Time](https://time.com/archive/6724029/somalia-anatomy-of-a-disaster/)). From the US and UN point of view this was enforcing the law after an attack on peacekeepers. From the SNA's, it was the UN taking sides in a civil war. The page should give both.

The **12 July 1993 raid on the "Abdi House"** is the event many accounts treat as the point of no return. US Quick Reaction Force helicopters fired missiles and cannon into a house where, by the US and UN account, SNA leaders were meeting. Other accounts describe a gathering of Habr Gidir elders, some discussing peace proposals ([Daily Beast](https://www.thedailybeast.com/did-the-us-cover-up-a-civilian-massacre-before-black-hawk-down/); [1-22 Infantry unit history](http://1-22infantry.org/history/somaliapagefour.htm)). The US Army's own overview calls it a "unilateral attack" that "implicated the coalition partners by association" ([US Forces Somalia After Action Report](https://api.army.mil/e2/c/downloads/314322.pdf)). The death counts show how much depends on who counted:

| Who counted | Somali killed |
|---|---|
| UNOSOM spokesman Col. David Haynes, on the day | "at least 13" (confirmed; "will be greater") |
| ICRC (as reported second-hand) | 54 killed, 161 wounded |
| Amnesty International | "more than 50" |
| SNA / Somali sources (as reported second-hand) | 73 |

Sources: [UPI, 12 July 1993](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1993/07/12/US-forces-attack-Somali-warlords-forces/6457742449600/); [Wikipedia, Bloody Monday raid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Monday_raid) (secondary for ICRC and SNA figures); [Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/nws110631994en.pdf). Afterwards an angry crowd killed four journalists who had come to the scene: **Dan Eldon, Hos Maina, Anthony Macharia (Reuters) and Hansi Krauss (AP)** ([The Baron, Reuters memorial](https://www.thebaron.info/people/memorial-book/anthony-macharia-hos-maina-and-dan-eldon)).

The conflict then escalated against US forces. On **8 August** a remotely detonated mine killed four US military police ([The Lantern, AP](https://osupublicationarchives.osu.edu/?a=d&d=LTN19930809-01.2.11)). **Task Force Ranger**, under Major General William Garrison and reporting to US Central Command rather than the UN, deployed on 22 August and arrived on 23 August ([DoD, Task Force Ranger report](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/International_Security_Affairs/07-A-2365_Task_Force_Ranger_Report_Operations_in_Somalia_1993.pdf)). It was built from B Company, 3rd Battalion, 75th Ranger Regiment, a Delta Force squadron, the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment and Air Force special tactics teams, usually given as about 440 men ([Wikipedia, Operation Gothic Serpent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Gothic_Serpent), secondary). Its first raid, on 30 August, detained UN employees by mistake ([ACSC paper, NSArchive](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB63/doc10.pdf)). In six missions before 3 October it met little resistance and arrested many SNA members, among them the financier **Osman Ali Atto** on 21 September ([CPT Kurt Smith paper](https://mcoecbamcoepwprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/library/DonovanPapers/other/STUP5/S-Z/SmithKurt%20CPT.pdf)). On **25 September** the SNA shot down a Quick Reaction Force Black Hawk, killing three US soldiers ([Washington Post, 26 September 1993](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1993/09/26/3-gis-killed-as-somalis-down-helicopter/70f8e493-6e8f-44b3-b760-7a2e00e0df17/)). That was a warning, a week early, of what the SNA could do. Over the same months **Human Rights Watch estimated that by the end of October 1993 at least 500-600 Somalis, civilians and fighters, had been killed by US or UNOSOM forces** ([HRW World Report 1994](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1994/WR94/Africa-08.htm)). For one incident, on 9 September, estimates run from "no firm figure" (*Washington Post*) to about 200 (Keesing's, via the IRB) ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1993/09/10/us-helicopters-fire-on-somalis/bf79ed24-8b12-400b-a0a2-43b160067a54/); [IRB chronology](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1994/en/93832)). Relations among contingents were strained too. The UN sought the recall of Italy's commander, General Bruno Loi, in July 1993 after he refused orders not cleared by Rome ([Washington Post, 15 July 1993](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1993/07/15/un-removes-italian-general-impeding-somalia-operation/82a80400-c6f1-4e5e-9f24-f64910959964/); [CSMonitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1993/0722/22031.html)).

## Fifteen hours in Mogadishu, 3-4 October 1993

On the afternoon of **Sunday 3 October**, Task Force Ranger launched a raid on a building near the **Olympic Hotel, close to the Bakara Market**, acting on intelligence that two of Aidid's senior advisers, named as Omar Salad Elmi and Abdi Hassan Awale "Qeybdiid", were meeting there ([DoD, TFR report](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/International_Security_Affairs/07-A-2365_Task_Force_Ranger_Report_Operations_in_Somalia_1993.pdf)). Helicopters took off at about **15:32** according to veterans' and military-history sites ([ArmyRanger.com](https://www.armyranger.com/task-force-ranger-somalia/); [SOFREP](https://sofrep.com/specialoperations/day-october-3-1993-battle-mogadishu-black-hawk)). The "about 15:40" in the page plan has no source. The raid itself worked within minutes: the force **took 24 prisoners, but not Aidid, who was not there** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Battle-of-Mogadishu)). The battle began when the force tried to leave.

At about **16:20**, the Black Hawk **Super 61** (pilots CW3 Cliff Wolcott and CW3 Donovan Briley) was brought down by a rocket-propelled grenade in the city. At about **16:40**, **Super 64** (pilot Michael Durant) was brought down roughly 1.5 km to the southwest ([ASOM Foundation](https://www.asomf.org/the-battle-of-mogadishu/); [ArmyRanger.com](https://www.armyranger.com/task-force-ranger-somalia/)). An Army history article puts the gap at about twelve minutes, not twenty ([DVIDS](https://dvidshub.net/news/431268/operation-gothic-serpent-task-force-ranger-and-search-missing-part-2)). Most of the assault force moved to the first crash site. Two Delta snipers, **Master Sergeant Gary Gordon and Sergeant First Class Randy Shughart**, volunteered to be put down at the second to protect its crew. Both were killed, and Durant, the only survivor of Super 64, was captured. Both snipers were awarded the **Medal of Honor** after their deaths, presented by President Clinton in May 1994, the first since the Vietnam War ([US Army, Medal of Honor citations](https://www.army.mil/medalofhonor/citations27.html); [PBS, American Valor](https://www.pbs.org/weta/americanvalor/stories/gordon.html)). The official citation and Mark Bowden's book differ on the order of events at the second site ([Wikipedia, Gary Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Gordon)), so the page should not give a sequence. The assault force spent the night cut off around the first crash site. The researchers found no citable detail of that night or of the ground convoy that tried and failed to reach it earlier in the evening (the "lost convoy"), and both need the CMH volume.

The rescue came from the UN's own contingents. A convoy assembled at Mogadishu's New Port from **two companies of the US 10th Mountain Division, the rest of Task Force Ranger, Malaysian Condor armoured personnel carriers and four Pakistani M48 tanks** ([Military.com op-ed](https://mst.military.com/daily-news/opinions/32-years-ago-un-troops-came-rescue-mogadishu-during-black-hawk-down.html)). A former Malaysian commander later counted **113 Malaysian troops in 32 Condors** ([Malaysiakini](https://www.malaysiakini.com/news/6400)). The convoy left at about 23:23, according to a Wikipedia mirror timeline, and reached the trapped soldiers at about **02:00 on 4 October** ([Defense Media Network](https://defensemedianetwork.com/stories/task-force-ranger-20th-anniversary-the-battle-of-mogadishu-oct-3-1993/3)). The withdrawal began at **05:42** ([F.E. Warren AFB](https://www.warren.af.mil/News/Features/Article/2383181/90th-missile-wing-honors-troops-of-mogadishu-mile)). The vehicles were full, so some soldiers moved on foot for part of the way, the "Mogadishu Mile". By about 06:30 everyone had reached the **Pakistani-held stadium** or the airport ([SOFREP](https://sofrep.com/specialoperations/day-october-3-1993-battle-mogadishu-black-hawk)). An Air Force account says the soldiers were meant to move alongside the convoy but it pulled ahead ([F.E. Warren AFB](https://www.warren.af.mil/News/Features/Article/2383181/90th-missile-wing-honors-troops-of-mogadishu-mile)), and an Army War College chair argues the run was shorter than popular memory has it ([Wikipedia, Mogadishu Mile](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Mogadishu_Mile), secondary). In 10th Mountain veterans' own words, published by the Army: "We didn't leave anybody behind" ([army.mil](https://www.army.mil/article/113311/we_didnt_leave_anybody_behind_10th_mountain_division_veterans_reflect_on_mogadishu_rescue_missi)).

**Michael Durant** was held for eleven days and released on **14 October 1993**, together with a Nigerian peacekeeper captured in September, after talks led by US envoy Robert Oakley ([Deseret News, 14 October 1993](https://www.deseret.com/1993/10/14/19070928/warlord-s-forces-release-american-pilot-nigerian/); [NPR, 2014](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2014/06/11/321018014/is-bergdahl-exchange-really-unprecedented)). Whether the two were linked is not established, and the page should say so. Durant's rank is given as both CW3 and CW4.

## A deadline, a resignation and a long shadow (October 1993-1995)

On **7 October 1993**, President Clinton told the nation he was sending about **1,700 more troops** and naval forces offshore, but that all US troops would be out of Somalia "no later than March the 31st", apart from a few hundred support personnel ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-nation-somalia)). The *Roanoke Times* counted the total reinforcement at 5,300 ([Roanoke Times, 8 October 1993](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/VA-news/ROA-Times/issues/1993/rt9310/931008/10080150.htm)). Defense Secretary **Les Aspin** had turned down General Montgomery's request for tanks and armoured vehicles shortly before the raid ([TIME](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,979399-7,00.html)). He resigned on **15 December 1993**, citing personal reasons, while much of Washington believed he had been pushed out ([Christian Science Monitor, 17 December 1993](https://csmonitor.com/1993/1217/17022.html)). Clinton later wrote that almost all US forces left on **31 March 1994** and the last few on 15 September 1994 ([Public Papers](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PPP-1993-book2/html/PPP-1993-book2-doc-pg1739.htm); exact letter not isolated). A secondary source has the last Marines leaving on 24 March, and "25 March" was not confirmed anywhere.

UNOSOM II continued without the Americans until the Security Council let it lapse. The last personnel left under the cover of **Operation United Shield (9 January-3 March 1995)**, ahead of a mandate that ran to 31 March 1995 ([UN Peacekeeping, UNOSOM II](https://peacekeeping.un.org/mission/past/unosom2.htm); [DND](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/services/military-history/history-heritage/past-operations/africa/deliverance.doc)). Aidid was wounded in factional fighting in Mogadishu in late July 1996 and died on 1 or 2 August 1996 ([Deseret News, 2 August 1996](https://www.deseret.com/1996/8/2/19257825/death-of-warlord-aidid-sends-somali-mourners-pouring-into-the-streets/)). The cause is also disputed. Somalia had no effective national government for years afterwards.

The battle's longest effect, its influence on US policy during the **Rwandan genocide** that began in April 1994, is genuinely contested, and the page should present both views. A former official interviewed by PBS spoke of Somalia's "searing effect" on later peacekeeping decisions, and the Miller Center says that by spring 1994 US foreign policy was "substantially influenced" by Mogadishu ([PBS Frontline, Sheehan interview](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/ghosts/interviews/sheehan.html); [Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/node/103771)). Against this, a Wilson Center study of Clinton Library drafts finds that the restrictive peacekeeping directive (PDD-25, signed May 1994) was already taking shape before the battle and barely changed afterwards ([Wilson Center](https://wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/pdd-25-and-the-genocide-rwanda-why-not-task-for-the-united-states)).

## *Black Hawk Down*: filmed in Salé, seen from the American side

Ridley Scott's film (2001; the AFI Catalog dates it 2002 by its wide release) was adapted from Mark Bowden's 1999 book and **shot in Morocco from about March to late June 2001**. The first day was reportedly 8 March at Kénitra ([The Cinemaholic](https://thecinemaholic.com/where-was-black-hawk-down-filmed); [AFI Catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/53922), not read). Secondary sources agree on the main places. **Most of the film was shot in Rabat and Salé**, with Salé's Sidi Moussa district painted and signposted as downtown Mogadishu and Avenue Nasser standing in for Hawlwadig Road. The base scenes were shot at **Kénitra** and Mehdya, and the first crash site was built in **Hay Arrahma, Salé** ([The Cinemaholic](https://thecinemaholic.com/where-was-black-hawk-down-filmed); [IMDb](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0265086/reference)). Rue des Consuls (the Bakara Market), the Mehdiya firing range, the warehouse used for the operations centre and the second crash site appear only in the project's unchecked `FILM-LOCATIONS.md`. The comparison map should stay at district level, labelled "approximate", as the project already plans. **US military support** is counted differently. The Army Special Operations history office describes about 150 service members with four MH-60 Black Hawks and four Little Birds of the 160th SOAR ([ARSOF History](https://arsof-history.org/articles/23sept_based_on_an_actual_event_page_1.html)), while a June 2001 Reuters report, known only from a forum repost, said four Black Hawks and about 35 Rangers.

The film's realism was praised mainly from the US military side. At the January 2002 premiere General Jack Keane called it "authentic" and Ranger veteran Matt Eversmann said the producer "accomplished his mission", while noting things "done for the sake of filmmaking" ([DVIDS](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/527437/army-declares-black-hawk-down-authentic)). The criticism came mainly from Somali, Malaysian and Pakistani perspectives. A Somali former fighter quoted by *Dawn* said the film played down the role of the Malaysian and Pakistani rescuers ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/16457/)). Malaysian veterans later answered with their own film, *Malbatt: Misi Bakara* (2023) ([The Star](https://thestar.com.my/lifestyle/entertainment/2023/08/25/malaysia039s-contribution-in-1993-mission-in-mogadishu-made-into-movie-039malbatt-misi-bakara039)). Three popular claims have no reliable source and should be dropped: that Pervez Musharraf criticised the film, that veterans rate it "75-80% accurate", and that the Pentagon forced scenes to be cut. Neither Bowden's nor Durant's view of the film was found. On Stebbins, the owner's existing wording ("renamed because the real soldier was later convicted of a crime") is the right level of detail for students, and the conviction date should be checked against a court-martial record or contemporary press before use.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what the page is about. The battle was not a stand-alone firefight. It came at the end of a slide in which each step widened the use of force: consent-based peacekeeping, then force to feed people, then force to disarm, then force to arrest one faction's leader. Each step looked reasonable to those who took it, and each was seen by the SNA as taking sides. The Canadian story runs alongside without touching the battle. It shows a different failure of the same intervention, one of discipline and accountability rather than of mandate, and its legacy was in Canadian institutions rather than in Somalia. Students will grasp both strands better if the page links them through the mission's changing purpose rather than through the setting alone.

The research also shows that the numbers *are* the history here. For almost every Somali death toll in 1991-93 (the Mogadishu war, the famine, 12 July, 3-4 October), the gap between the lowest and highest count is a factor of five to ten, and that gap tracks who was counting. Tables of "who counted what" are the most honest and most teachable device available to the page. The largest open questions are whether the Canadian courts-martial records and *Dishonoured Legacy* confirm the dates and outcomes given here, whether the CMH volume settles the battle's timings and US casualty figures, what the ICRC actually said about Somali casualties, and what Somali voices, including the Arone and Arush families, have said for the record. None of these could be reached this time, and they decide what the page can state as fact.
