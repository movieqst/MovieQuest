# UKRAINE AND RUSSIA: ESCALATION, 1991–2014: research report (v2, corrected)
**Battles of History · 18 November 2026**

This is the dossier (`RESEARCH-DOSSIER.md`, v1, written from general knowledge) **corrected after ChatGPT's source check**
(`reviews/CHATGPT-SOURCE-CHECK.md`). The check's corrections are applied and take precedence. Its sources are cited in square

**What this page is.** A **background** page, not a battle. It is the first card of the group "Russia–Ukraine War: Crimea and
Donbas, 2014–2015". It explains how independent Ukraine and the Russian Federation went from the agreements of 1990–1991 to
the armed seizure of the Crimean parliament on 27 February 2014, so that the five battle pages that follow make sense. It has
no fighting until its last chapter.

## Evidence labels
- **ESTABLISHED:** documented in treaty texts, official records or the standard scholarship.
- **ACCOUNT:** a claim by one side, one witness or one report; attributed.
- **ESTIMATE:** a number with real uncertainty, with what it counts.
- **DISPUTED:** credible sources or the two sides differ; both stated.
- **RECONSTRUCTION:** a choice made for the page.
- **UKRAINIAN ACCOUNT / RUSSIAN ACCOUNT:** a claim found only in one government's statements.
- **COURT:** a judicial finding (Ukrainian courts; the European Court of Human Rights), with what it did and did not decide.

## 1. The starting point (to 1991)
- **1954.** A decree of the Presidium of the USSR Supreme Soviet of 19 February 1954 transferred the Crimean oblast from the
  Russian SFSR to the Ukrainian SSR, within one state. A USSR law followed on 26 April 1954. The reasons given were economic and
  geographic ties. [ESTABLISHED] The later claim that the transfer was unconstitutional is a RUSSIAN ACCOUNT, made from 1992.
- **The Crimean Tatars,** deported en masse by Stalin in May 1944, began returning from the late 1980s. [ESTABLISHED]
- **Census figures** (2001) measure declared nationality, not language or political allegiance. "Crimea" means the
  autonomous republic, counted separately from Sevastopol. Exact percentages are to be taken from the census tables before
  use. [ESTABLISHED; figures still to cite]
- **16 July 1990:** the Ukrainian SSR's Declaration of State Sovereignty, including an intention to become a neutral,
  non-nuclear state. [ESTABLISHED]
- **19 November 1990:** a bilateral treaty between the Russian SFSR and the Ukrainian SSR, recognising each other's territorial
  integrity within the existing borders of the USSR. It is a precursor to the December 1991 commitments. [ESTABLISHED;
  added by the source check]
- **24 August 1991,** after the failed coup in Moscow: the Verkhovna Rada's Act of Declaration of Independence. [ESTABLISHED]
- **1 December 1991, the independence referendum:** **90.32% yes, on a turnout of 84.18%**. Every region voted yes.
  - Crimea: **54.19%**
  - Sevastopol: **57.07%**
  - Donetsk: **83.90%**
  - Luhansk: **83.86%**
  - Leonid Kravchuk was elected president the same day.
  - [ESTABLISHED; results table, Seventeen Moments in Soviet History. The page's introductory "92.3%" conflicts with its own
    table and is not used.]
- **8 December 1991, the Belavezha agreement** (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus) dissolved the USSR and founded the CIS. Its
  **Article 5** commits the parties to respect each other's territorial integrity and the inviolability of existing borders.
  The **Alma-Ata Declaration** of 21 December reaffirmed it. Gorbachev resigned on 25 December. [ESTABLISHED]

## 2. The nuclear weapons and the Budapest Memorandum (1991–1996)
- **The arsenal.** Ukraine had on its territory **about 1,900 strategic warheads, 176 ICBMs and about 45 strategic bombers**
  (Steven Pifer). The bombers' weapons were part of the arsenal. Tactical nuclear weapons were moved to Russia by mid-1992.
  The strategic weapons were stationed in Ukraine; **launch authorization remained tied to Moscow**. The tactical and the
  strategic transfers are kept distinct. [ESTIMATE; Pifer, Brookings]
- **23 May 1992, the Lisbon Protocol** to START I: Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan undertook to join the NPT as non-nuclear
  states. [ESTABLISHED]
- **14 January 1994, the Trilateral Statement** (United States, Russia, Ukraine) in Moscow: compensation (reactor fuel for the
  uranium) and security assurances. [ESTABLISHED]
- **5 December 1994, Budapest,** at the CSCE summit: the **Memorandum on Security Assurances**, signed by **Kuchma, Yeltsin,
  Major and Clinton** (UN Treaty Series 3007, I-52241). Ukraine acceded to the NPT the same day. [ESTABLISHED]
  - Its first undertaking: "to respect the independence and sovereignty and the existing borders of Ukraine".
  - Its second: "to refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of
    Ukraine". The clause also addresses the use of weapons, with its UN Charter exceptions; the full clauses are shown on
    screen.
  - It also includes consultation, and a narrowly framed undertaking to seek UN Security Council action if Ukraine became the
    victim of aggression or the threat of aggression with nuclear weapons.
  - **The page calls these "security assurances".** Whether the memorandum is legally binding is a separate question from
    whether it promised military defence, which it did not. [ESTABLISHED; the legal status DISPUTED]
- **The last trains** carrying nuclear warheads **arrived in Russia on 1 June 1996** (not "left on"). [ESTABLISHED; Brookings]
- **Two views.** The Ukrainian account from 2014 is that Russia broke the memorandum. The Russian account is that the 2014
  government was not the state the assurances were given to. [DISPUTED]

## 3. Crimea, Sevastopol and the Black Sea Fleet (1991–1999)
- **20 January 1991:** a referendum in Crimea on **re-establishing the Crimean ASSR as a subject of the USSR and a participant
  in a proposed Union Treaty**. In February 1991 Ukraine's legislature restored the autonomy within the Ukrainian SSR.
  [ESTABLISHED]
- **May 1992:**
  - the Crimean parliament's act of independence and its constitution;
  - Kyiv's rejection;
  - the cancellation of the planned referendum.
  - These are told separately. The act did not become an effective secession. The precise legislative sequence (5 and 6 May)
    is still to verify. [ESTABLISHED in outline; Sasse, *The Crimea Question*]
- **21 May 1992:** the Russian Supreme Soviet (the parliament, not Yeltsin's government) declared the 1954 transfer invalid.
  [ESTABLISHED]
- **9 July 1993:** the Russian parliament declared Sevastopol a Russian city. **20 July 1993:** a **statement by the President
  of the UN Security Council on behalf of the Council** (S/26118) said the decree was incompatible with the relevant
  bilateral commitments and with the purposes and principles of the UN Charter, and was without effect. Yeltsin's government
  distanced itself. [ESTABLISHED]
- **1994–1995:** Yuri Meshkov elected president of Crimea in January 1994. In March 1995 the Rada abolished the Crimean
  presidency and the 1992 constitution. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The Black Sea Fleet** was disputed from January 1992. There were interim agreements at Dagomys (June 1992), Yalta (August
  1992) and Massandra (September 1993). The number of ships and craft is **not given** (no source). [ESTABLISHED]
- **28 May 1997, the fleet agreements:**
  - Russia's share was **81.7%** and Ukraine's **18.3%**, the final allocation after transfers.
  - Russia leased bases at Sevastopol for 20 years, to 2017.
  - The rent was **US$97.75 million a year**, credited against Ukrainian state debt to Russia (not "gas debt").
  - [ESTABLISHED; SIPRI 1998; the settlement agreement]
- **31 May 1997, the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Partnership ("the Big Treaty"):**
  - **Article 2:** respect for territorial integrity and the inviolability of existing borders.
  - Ratified by Ukraine **14 January 1998**.
  - Ratified by Russia's Duma **25 December 1998** and Federation Council **17 February 1999**; federal law **2 March 1999**.
  - In force **1 April 1999**.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **28 January 2003:** the treaty on the Russian–Ukrainian state border (the land border). [ESTABLISHED]

## 4. The Kuchma years (1994–2005)
- **Kuchma (president 1994–2005):** closer economic ties with Russia alongside a declared "European choice". Ukraine joined
  NATO's Partnership for Peace (1994) and signed a Charter on a Distinctive Partnership with NATO (July 1997). [ESTABLISHED]
- **2000–2001, the Gongadze affair:** the murdered journalist Georgiy Gongadze, the "Melnychenko tapes", and the "Ukraine
  without Kuchma" protests. [ESTABLISHED; Kuchma's responsibility DISPUTED]
- **September–October 2003, Tuzla:** Russia's Krasnodar region built a dam from the Taman peninsula toward the Ukrainian
  island of Tuzla in the Kerch Strait. Ukraine sent border guards. The building stopped after Kuchma and Putin met.
  [ESTABLISHED]
- **December 2003:** the treaty on cooperation in the use of the Sea of Azov and the Kerch Strait, the diplomatic sequel to
  Tuzla. [ESTABLISHED; added by the source check]
- **19 September 2003:** the Single Economic Space agreement with Russia, Belarus and Kazakhstan; never fully implemented.
  [ESTABLISHED]

## 5. The Orange Revolution (2004)
- **The election.** Viktor Yushchenko (opposition) faced Viktor Yanukovych (prime minister, the candidate favoured by Kuchma
  and Moscow). Putin visited Ukraine during the campaign. [ESTABLISHED]
- **September 2004:** Yushchenko was poisoned with TCDD dioxin; the perpetrator has never been established. [ESTABLISHED;
  perpetrator UNKNOWN]
- **The runoff and the protests:**
  - **21 November** was the runoff voting day. The Central Election Commission **announced the result on 24 November**.
  - Observers (OSCE/ODIHR) documented serious abuses.
  - Hundreds of thousands protested on the Maidan for weeks. [ESTABLISHED; crowd sizes ESTIMATE]
- **The ruling and the re-run:**
  - **3 December:** the Supreme Court invalidated the results and ordered a repeat runoff.
  - **26 December:** **Yushchenko 52.0%, Yanukovych 44.2%** (51.99 and 44.20).
  - Yushchenko was inaugurated on 23 January 2005.
  - [ESTABLISHED; ODIHR 2004]
- **The 2004 constitutional compromise** (December 2004) moved powers from the president to parliament. The February 2014
  agreement sought to restore it. [ESTABLISHED; added by the source check]
- **Two framings:** the Russian account is a Western-sponsored "colour revolution"; the Ukrainian and Western account is a
  civic uprising against a stolen election. ODIHR's evidence of abuses and the Supreme Court's ruling are directly documented.
  Any claim of foreign orchestration is attributed to a named source. [DISPUTED framing]

## 6. The gas disputes (2006, 2009)
- Ukraine carried most of Russia's gas exports to Europe. The share varied by year; **year-specific figures** only.
  [ESTIMATE]
- **January 2006:**
  - a price dispute;
  - supply to Ukraine was cut on 1 January;
  - settled through the intermediary RosUkrEnergo.
  - The "$95" figure was the intermediary's **blended price for supply to Ukraine**, not Gazprom's price.
  - The detailed 2006 price chronology is still to verify. [ESTABLISHED in outline]
- **January 2009:**
  - Russia stopped supply for Ukrainian use on **1 January**;
  - transit to Europe fell sharply on **6 January** and **stopped on 7 January**;
  - it resumed on **20 January**, after **two ten-year contracts** (supply and transit) agreed by Tymoshenko and Putin.
  - The Oxford Institute for Energy Studies counts **16 EU members plus Moldova** in its cutoff description. "Eighteen
    countries" is not used.
  - [ESTABLISHED; Oxford Institute for Energy Studies, 2009]
- **October 2011:** Tymoshenko was sentenced to seven years for abuse of office over the 2009 contract. The EU called the case
  selective justice. [ESTABLISHED; the charge's merit DISPUTED]

## 7. NATO, Bucharest and Georgia (2007–2009)

- **January 2008:** Ukraine's leaders asked NATO for a Membership Action Plan. [ESTABLISHED]
- **3 April 2008, Bucharest:**

  - Paragraph 23 of the declaration: "We agreed today that these countries will become members of NATO."

## 8. Yanukovych and the Kharkiv agreements (2010–2013)
- **7 February 2010:** **Yanukovych 49.0%, Tymoshenko 45.5%** (48.95 and 45.47). ODIHR said the election met most
  international commitments. [ESTABLISHED]
- **21 April 2010, Kharkiv:**
  - the fleet lease was extended by **25 additional years from 2017**, with automatic five-year renewals unless notice was
    given;
  - in return, a gas discount of **30%, capped at US$100 per 1,000 m³**;
  - ratified on 27 April 2010 amid a brawl in the Rada.
  - [ESTABLISHED; the agreements as quoted by the ECHR; Radio Svoboda]
- **1 July 2010:** a law declaring Ukraine non-aligned. [ESTABLISHED]
- **2012:** the **language law** (on the principles of state language policy), which allowed regional official status for
  minority languages, Russian in many regions. [ESTABLISHED; added: needed before its attempted repeal in 2014]
- **The EU Association Agreement:**
  - Yanukovych's government negotiated it; it was initialled in 2012.
  - The EU required progress on **selective justice, elections and reforms**. Tymoshenko's imprisonment was the most prominent
    selective-justice case. [ESTABLISHED]
- **August 2013:** Russian customs blocked Ukrainian imports for about a week, as Russia pressed for the Customs Union.
  [ESTABLISHED]

## 9. Euromaidan (November 2013 – February 2014)
- **21 November 2013:** the government suspended preparations to sign the Association Agreement, a week before the Vilnius
  summit. Protests began on the Maidan that night. [ESTABLISHED]
- **30 November:** riot police (Berkut) violently cleared the students. **1 December:** a very large crowd in central Kyiv.
  [ESTABLISHED; crowd ESTIMATE]
- **17 December 2013:**
  - Russia announced purchases of **up to US$15 billion** of Ukrainian bonds and bought an initial **US$3 billion** issue.
  - The announced gas price was **US$268.50 per 1,000 m³**.
  - [ESTABLISHED; High Court, *Law Debenture v Ukraine*]
- **January 2014:**
  - 16 January: the laws restricting protest;
  - 19 January: clashes on Hrushevskoho Street;
  - 22 January: the first protesters killed by gunfire;
  - 28 January: the laws were repealed and Prime Minister Azarov resigned.
  - The early death chronology is still to verify in detail. [ESTABLISHED in outline]
- **18–20 February 2014:**
  - OHCHR counts **108 protesters and other individuals and 13 law-enforcement personnel** killed in its Maidan account.
  - "The Heavenly Hundred" is a commemorative name, not a count.
  - On 20 February, **dozens of protesters were shot** on Instytutska Street. [ESTABLISHED; OHCHR]
- **Who fired:**
  - In October 2023 a Kyiv court gave convictions and acquittals of former Berkut officers in the main Instytutska case. Its
    latest appeal status is not verified. [COURT]
  - The ECHR (*Shmorgunov and others*) established extensive state abuses and failures to investigate. It did not determine who
    fired every shot. [COURT]
  - The strongest supported account assigns responsibility for the documented shootings to security personnel, while leaving
    some incidents unresolved. Claims of a coordinated opposition "false flag" need independent evidence. Unresolved shots and
    individual acquittals do not establish that claim. [DISPUTED for unresolved incidents]
- **21 February, the agreement** (UN S/2014/121):
  - Yanukovych and the opposition leaders agreed to restore the **2004 constitutional arrangements** and hold early elections.
  - It was witnessed by the foreign ministers of Germany and Poland and, for France, **Éric Fournier** (not Foreign Minister
    Fabius).
  - Russia's envoy did not sign.
  - That night Yanukovych left Kyiv. [ESTABLISHED]
- **22 February:**
  - **328 deputies voted in favour** of a resolution declaring that Yanukovych had **withdrawn from performing his duties**,
    and calling a presidential election for **25 May**.
  - This was **not an Article 111 impeachment**. [ESTABLISHED; Rada resolution 757-VII; PACE chronology]
- **23 February:**
  - the Rada assigned the president's duties to its speaker, Oleksandr Turchynov;
  - it voted to repeal the 2012 language law;
  - the repeal was **never signed into law**. [ESTABLISHED]
- **Two positions** [DISPUTED: both stated]:
  - The Ukrainian position: the Rada said Yanukovych had withdrawn from exercising his duties, and called a new election.
  - The Russian position: an unconstitutional coup (UN Security Council debate).
  - The constitutional dispute turns on the absence of the prescribed impeachment process, against parliament's response to a
    president who had stopped exercising his functions.
- **Putin's later account.** In the trailer of the documentary "Crimea: the Way Home", aired **8 March 2015**, Putin described
  the night of 22–23 February 2014: "We have to start work on returning Crimea to Russia" ("мы вынуждены начать работу по
  возврату Крыма в состав России"). It establishes what he publicly claimed; no exact operational order is inferred from it.
  [ACCOUNT; Interfax]
- **27 February 2014:** armed men without insignia seized the Crimean parliament in Simferopol. The ECHR found Russian
  effective control of Crimea from that date, for the purposes of the Convention. **This page ends here; the Crimea 2014 page
  begins.** [ESTABLISHED; COURT]
- **Positions at the United Nations, 2014:**
  - Ukraine told the Security Council that Russian military action violated its sovereignty, and sought withdrawal.
  - Russia asserted grounds for intervention, including Yanukovych's request. Putin later acknowledged Russian military support
    for the Crimean forces.
  - [UKRAINIAN ACCOUNT; RUSSIAN ACCOUNT; UN S/PV.7125]

## 10. Causes: how historians read them (for the debrief)
- **A security-dilemma reading** stresses NATO enlargement and Moscow's perception of a strategic loss (for example Richard
  Sakwa, *Frontline Ukraine*).
- **A sovereignty-centred reading** stresses independent Ukrainian choices, coercion, and Russian claims over Ukraine (for
  example Serhii Plokhy, *The Russo-Ukrainian War*).
- **The page keeps** causal interpretation separate from responsibility for specific acts. [scholarship: both stated]
