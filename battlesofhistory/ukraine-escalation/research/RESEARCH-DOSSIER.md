## What this page is
A **Background** page, not a battle: the first card of the group "Russia–Ukraine War: Crimea and Donbas, 2014–2015". It explains how independent Ukraine and the Russian Federation went from the handshake of 1991 to the armed seizure of Crimea in February 2014, so that the five battle pages that follow (Crimea 2014, Sloviansk, Donetsk airport, Ilovaisk, Debaltseve) make sense. It has no fighting until its last chapter. Its map is a map of states, treaties, pipelines, fleets and crowds.

## Evidence labels
- ESTABLISHED: documented in treaty texts, official records or the standard scholarship, and not seriously contested.
- ACCOUNT: a claim by one side, one witness or one report; attribute it.
- ESTIMATE: a number with real uncertainty.
- DISPUTED: credible sources or the two sides differ; present both.
- RECONSTRUCTION: a choice made for the page (what to show, where, in what order).

Two further labels fit this subject and should be used on the page in place of the battle pages' "British account / German account": **UKRAINIAN ACCOUNT** and **RUSSIAN ACCOUNT**, for claims that come only from one government's statements.

## 1. The starting point: one country, two republics (to 1991)
- Crimea was transferred from the Russian SFSR to the Ukrainian SSR by a decree of the Presidium of the USSR Supreme Soviet dated 19 February 1954, within one state; the reasons given were economic and geographic ties. [ESTABLISHED] The later Russian claim that the transfer was unconstitutional is a RUSSIAN ACCOUNT from 1992 onward.
- The Crimean Tatars, deported en masse by Stalin in May 1944, began returning from the late 1980s; by the 2001 Ukrainian census Crimea was about 58% Russian, 24% Ukrainian and 12% Crimean Tatar by nationality. [ESTABLISHED; census figures to verify to the decimal]
- Ukraine's 2001 census: about 77.8% Ukrainian and 17.3% Russian by nationality; Russian was the everyday language of much of the east and south and of Crimea. [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- 16 July 1990: the Ukrainian SSR's Declaration of State Sovereignty, which included an intention to be a neutral, non-nuclear state. [ESTABLISHED]
- 24 August 1991, after the failed Moscow coup: the Verkhovna Rada's Act of Declaration of Independence. [ESTABLISHED]
- 1 December 1991: referendum confirming independence, about 92% yes on a turnout of about 84%; every region voted yes, including Crimea (about 54%) and Sevastopol (about 57%), the lowest figures. Leonid Kravchuk elected president the same day. [ESTABLISHED; verify the regional figures]
- 8 December 1991: Belavezha Accords (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus) dissolving the USSR and founding the CIS; 21 December Alma-Ata Protocol; 25 December Gorbachev resigned. [ESTABLISHED]
- The Russian Federation recognised Ukraine's independence and borders in December 1991. [ESTABLISHED; cite the exact instrument]

## 2. The nuclear weapons and the Budapest Memorandum (1991–1996)
- Ukraine inherited the third-largest nuclear arsenal in the world: roughly 1,900 strategic warheads on 176 ICBMs (130 SS-19 and 46 SS-24) and about 44 heavy bombers, plus tactical warheads (removed to Russia by mid-1992). Operational control of the strategic weapons remained with Moscow's command system. [ESTIMATE; figures vary by source, verify]
- 23 May 1992: Lisbon Protocol to START I, Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan undertaking to join the NPT as non-nuclear states. [ESTABLISHED]
- 14 January 1994: Trilateral Statement (US, Russia, Ukraine) on compensation (fuel for the uranium) and security assurances. [ESTABLISHED]
- 5 December 1994, Budapest, at the CSCE summit: the Budapest Memorandum on Security Assurances, signed by the US, the UK and Russia with Ukraine (and parallel memoranda for Belarus and Kazakhstan). Its undertakings include "to respect the independence and sovereignty and the existing borders of Ukraine" and "to refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of Ukraine". [ESTABLISHED; quote from the text, verify wording]
- Ukraine acceded to the NPT in December 1994; the last warheads left for Russia by 1 June 1996; the silos and bombers were destroyed or transferred over the following years. [ESTABLISHED; verify date]
- Whether the Memorandum was a legally binding guarantee or a political assurance is DISPUTED; the Ukrainian account from 2014 is that Russia broke it; the Russian account is that the 2014 government was not the state the assurances were given to. [DISPUTED]

## 3. Crimea, Sevastopol and the Black Sea Fleet (1992–1997)
- 20 January 1991: Crimean referendum restoring the Crimean autonomous republic within the Ukrainian SSR; the Rada confirmed the autonomy in February 1991. [ESTABLISHED]
- 5 May 1992: the Crimean parliament declared independence conditional on a referendum, then withdrew it under pressure from Kyiv; 6 May a Crimean constitution. [ESTABLISHED; sequence to verify]
- 21 May 1992: the Russian Supreme Soviet resolved that the 1954 transfer had been unconstitutional; 9 July 1993 it declared Sevastopol a Russian city. President Yeltsin distanced himself from both; the UN Security Council (20 July 1993) called the Sevastopol vote incompatible with the UN Charter. [ESTABLISHED; verify the UNSC statement]
- January 1994: Yuri Meshkov elected president of Crimea on a pro-Russian platform; March 1995 the Rada abolished the Crimean presidency and the 1992 constitution; Meshkov left for Moscow. [ESTABLISHED]
- The Black Sea Fleet, about 300–400 ships and craft based at Sevastopol, was disputed from January 1992; interim agreements at Dagomys (June 1992), Yalta (August 1992) and Massandra (September 1993). [ESTIMATE on numbers]
- 28 May 1997: the Partition Treaty on the Black Sea Fleet. Russia took about 81.7% of the ships and Ukraine 18.3%; Russia leased the main bays and facilities at Sevastopol for 20 years, to 2017, for about US$97.75 million a year, credited against Ukraine's gas debt. [ESTABLISHED; verify percentages and rent]
- 31 May 1997, Kyiv: the Treaty on Friendship, Cooperation and Partnership ("the Big Treaty"), in which Russia recognised Ukraine's borders, including Crimea. Ratified by the Rada in January 1998 and by the Russian Duma and Federation Council in December 1998 and February 1999; in force 1 April 1999, for ten years with automatic renewal. [ESTABLISHED]
- 2003: the Treaty on the Russian–Ukrainian state border, signed 28 January 2003, fixing the land border. [ESTABLISHED]

## 4. The Kuchma years (1994–2005)
- Leonid Kuchma, president 1994–2005: closer economic ties with Russia alongside a declared "European choice"; Ukraine joined NATO's Partnership for Peace (1994) and signed a Charter on a Distinctive Partnership with NATO (July 1997). [ESTABLISHED]
- 2000–2001, the Gongadze affair: the murdered journalist Georgiy Gongadze and the "Melnychenko tapes" implicating Kuchma, large protests ("Ukraine without Kuchma"); Kuchma's standing in the West fell and he leaned toward Moscow. [ESTABLISHED; Kuchma's responsibility DISPUTED]
- September–October 2003, Tuzla: Russia's Krasnodar region built a dam from the Taman peninsula toward the Ukrainian island of Tuzla in the Kerch Strait; Ukraine sent border guards; the building stopped in late October after Kuchma and Putin met; the status of the Kerch Strait and the Sea of Azov stayed unsettled. [ESTABLISHED]
- 2003: Ukraine signed the Single Economic Space agreement with Russia, Belarus and Kazakhstan (19 September 2003); it was never fully implemented. [ESTABLISHED]

## 5. The Orange Revolution (2004)
- 31 October and 21 November 2004: presidential election, Viktor Yushchenko (opposition) against Viktor Yanukovych (prime minister, Kuchma's and Moscow's preferred candidate). Putin visited Kyiv during the campaign and congratulated Yanukovych after the second round. [ESTABLISHED]
- September 2004: Yushchenko fell ill and was found to have been poisoned with TCDD dioxin; who did it has never been established. [ESTABLISHED that he was poisoned; the perpetrator UNKNOWN]
- 21 November: the Central Election Commission declared Yanukovych the winner; observers reported widespread fraud; hundreds of thousands protested in Kyiv's Independence Square (the Maidan) in the cold for weeks, in orange. [ESTABLISHED; crowd sizes ESTIMATE]
- 3 December: the Supreme Court annulled the second round; 26 December: the re-run, Yushchenko about 52%, Yanukovych about 44%; Yushchenko inaugurated 23 January 2005. [ESTABLISHED; verify percentages]
- Russian account: a Western-sponsored "colour revolution". Ukrainian and Western account: a civic uprising against a stolen election. [DISPUTED framing]

## 6. The gas wars (2006, 2009)
- Ukraine carried most of Russia's gas exports to Europe through its pipelines (about 80% in the 2000s). [ESTIMATE]
- 1–4 January 2006: Gazprom cut supplies to Ukraine in a price dispute (from about US$50 to a demanded $230 per thousand cubic metres); pressure in several EU countries fell; settled with RosUkrEnergo as intermediary at $95. [ESTABLISHED; figures to verify]
- 7–20 January 2009: a second cut, this time of all transit through Ukraine; about eighteen European countries lost supply in mid-winter, Bulgaria, Slovakia and the Balkans worst. Settled by a ten-year contract negotiated by Prime Minister Yulia Tymoshenko and Putin on 19 January 2009. [ESTABLISHED; count of countries ESTIMATE]
- October 2011: Tymoshenko was sentenced to seven years for abuse of office over that contract, under President Yanukovych; the EU called the trial politically motivated. [ESTABLISHED; the charge's merit DISPUTED]

## 7. NATO, Bucharest and Georgia (2007–2008)
- 10 February 2007, Munich Security Conference: Putin's speech against a unipolar world and NATO expansion. [ESTABLISHED]
- January 2008: Yushchenko, Tymoshenko and the Rada speaker asked NATO for a Membership Action Plan. [ESTABLISHED]
- 2–4 April 2008, Bucharest summit: NATO declined a MAP for Ukraine and Georgia (Germany and France opposed) but declared that both "will become members of NATO". [ESTABLISHED; quote from the declaration]
- At the closed NATO–Russia Council session in Bucharest Putin is reported to have told President Bush that Ukraine "is not even a state", that part of its territory is Eastern Europe and the greater part a Russian gift. Reported by Kommersant (7 April 2008) from an unnamed source. [ACCOUNT; attribute to the report]
- 7–12 August 2008: the Russo-Georgian war; Russia recognised Abkhazia and South Ossetia. Ukraine's Yushchenko flew to Tbilisi in support. [ESTABLISHED]
- 11 August 2009: Medvedev's open letter to Yushchenko listing Russian grievances and announcing no new ambassador while Yushchenko remained. [ESTABLISHED]
- Opinion polling: NATO membership did not have majority support in Ukraine before 2014 (typically 20–30% in favour). [ESTIMATE; cite a poll]

## 8. Yanukovych and the Kharkiv Pact (2010–2013)
- 7 February 2010: Viktor Yanukovych elected president, about 49% to Tymoshenko's 45.5%, in an election observers called fair. [ESTABLISHED; verify figures]
- 21 April 2010, Kharkiv: the Kharkiv Pact extended the Black Sea Fleet lease by 25 years beyond 2017 (to 2042, with an option to 2047) in exchange for a discount of up to 30% (about US$100 per thousand cubic metres) on gas; ratified 27 April 2010 amid a brawl and smoke bombs in the Rada. [ESTABLISHED]
- 1 July 2010: law on the foundations of domestic and foreign policy declaring Ukraine non-aligned (no NATO membership). [ESTABLISHED]
- 2010–2013: Yanukovych's government nevertheless negotiated an Association Agreement with the EU, initialled on 30 March 2012 (political part) and 19 July 2012 (trade part); the EU made Tymoshenko's release a condition. [ESTABLISHED]
- Russia pressed Ukraine to join its Customs Union instead; in August 2013 Russian customs blocked Ukrainian imports for about a week. [ESTABLISHED]

## 9. Euromaidan (November 2013 – February 2014)
- 21 November 2013: the government suspended preparations for signing the Association Agreement, a week before the Vilnius summit (28–29 November). Protests began that night on the Maidan, led by journalists and students; a few hundred, then thousands. [ESTABLISHED]
- 30 November, about 4 a.m.: Berkut riot police violently cleared the students from the Maidan; dozens injured. 1 December: a crowd estimated at several hundred thousand filled the centre of Kyiv; the city hall and the trade-union building were occupied. [ESTABLISHED; crowd ESTIMATE]
- 17 December 2013: Putin and Yanukovych agreed a Russian purchase of US$15 billion of Ukrainian bonds and a gas price cut to US$268.5 per thousand cubic metres. [ESTABLISHED]
- 16 January 2014: the Rada passed laws restricting protest ("dictatorship laws") by show of hands; 19 January clashes on Hrushevskoho Street; 22 January the first protesters killed by gunfire (Serhiy Nihoyan and Mikhail Zhiznevsky); the laws were repealed 28 January and Prime Minister Azarov resigned. [ESTABLISHED]
- 18–20 February 2014: the bloodiest days: police and protesters fought on Instytutska Street; on 20 February snipers killed dozens of protesters; in all about 100 protesters died in the revolution (the "Heavenly Hundred") and about 13–18 police. Who fired on 20 February remains DISPUTED (Ukrainian investigations charged Berkut officers; Russian and some other accounts allege provocateurs). [ESTABLISHED deaths; ESTIMATE counts; DISPUTED responsibility]
- 21 February: an agreement between Yanukovych and the opposition leaders, witnessed by the foreign ministers of Germany, Poland and France (Russia's envoy did not sign), for early elections and a return to the 2004 constitution; that night Yanukovych left Kyiv. [ESTABLISHED]
- 22 February: the Rada voted 328 to 0 that Yanukovych had removed himself from his duties and set an election for 25 May; 23 February Oleksandr Turchynov became acting president; the same day the Rada voted to repeal the 2012 language law (Turchynov did not sign the repeal, and it did not take effect). [ESTABLISHED; verify the vote count and the language-law sequence]
- Russian account: an unconstitutional coup by nationalists backed by the West. Ukrainian account: a revolution of dignity after the state killed its citizens. [DISPUTED framing; both to be stated]
- 23 February: Putin later said (in the 2015 film "Crimea: the Way Home") that at the end of an all-night meeting that morning he ordered the operation to "return Crimea". [ACCOUNT, Putin's own]
- 27 February 2014: armed men without insignia seized the Crimean parliament in Simferopol. This page ends here; the Crimea 2014 page begins. [ESTABLISHED]
