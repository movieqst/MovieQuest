**Owner's rules for this page:**
- No copyrighted material: no press photographs, book covers, documentary stills or copied text. Books and articles (Carol

- No graphic violence. Deaths are stated plainly, without methods, injuries or descriptions of the dead. No images of the
  dead, of bodies found, or of the wounded. The destroyed villages are shown only as map marks, not drawn as ruins with people.

**The approach:**
- **Five parts:** Lika at war; Operation "Pocket 93"; the peacekeepers; the courts; three memories. Introduction cards are
  I1–I5; chapters run 1–21 in page order.
- **Accusation, finding, claim.** An indictment is an accusation. A verdict is a finding. A count by an organisation is
  that organisation's count. The spoken words say which, every time. Rahim Ademi was acquitted and is never called guilty.
  Janko Bobetko was never tried and is called neither guilty nor innocent.
- **The conviction's scope.** Mirko Norac's conviction concerned four civilians and two prisoners of war, according to the
  ICTY Prosecutor's final monitoring report [6]. That is not the number killed in the operation, and the narration says so.
- **Counts stay separate.** Civilians killed, prisoners killed, soldiers killed in combat, and an organisation's total of
  all its dead are different things. They are never joined into one range, and never added together. The counts are in
  on-screen tables (below), not in the narration.
- **No crime excuses another.** The page says plainly that Croats in Lika also suffered crimes by Serb forces from 1991,
  and that Serb rockets later killed Croatian civilians. These are separate crimes by different forces.
- **No collective guilt.** A commander's conviction does not make a people, an army or every member of a unit guilty.
- **The Canadian story is told from its own report.** "According to the Canadian operational report…". Croatian denials
  are named, with author and date, without presenting denial as equal evidence that no firing happened. The unconfirmed
  figure of 27 Croatian casualties is **not** narrated (review: keep it out of the main narration).
- **Spoken forms:** numbers and dates as the narrator says them. Initials written out at first use.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Medak Pocket, in Croatia, from the ninth to the seventeenth of September 1993. Croatian forces took a small area of Serb-held villages near the town of Gospić. A week later, Canadian and French soldiers of the United Nations came to take it over, and found the villages destroyed. Croatian courts later found that war crimes had been committed there. Croatians, Serbs and Canadians remember these days very differently. We will tell you what is known, who says what, and what is still not known. Some chapters describe killings. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### I1. MEDAK POCKET 1993
- On the ninth of September 1993, the Croatian Army and police attacked a Serb-held area south of Gospić, in the Lika region of Croatia. It was called the Medak Pocket. *(established)*
- After a week of fighting and international pressure, Croatia agreed to withdraw, and to hand the area to United Nations peacekeepers. *(established)*
- Canadian and French peacekeepers entered between the fifteenth and the seventeenth of September. They found extensive destruction, and bodies. They also found three survivors, whom they protected and evacuated. *(established; canbat)* [4]
- In 2008 a court in Zagreb found that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners had been committed. It convicted one Croatian commander, and acquitted another. *(court: conviction; court: acquittal)* [6]

### I2. Croatia and the Krajina
- In 1991, Croatia declared independence from Yugoslavia. Many Serbs in Croatia rejected it. With the support of the Yugoslav People's Army, they took control of large areas and declared their own state, the Republic of Serbian Krajina. *(established)*
- Lika is a high, wooded region of southern Croatia, between the coast mountains and Bosnia. In 1993 the front line ran close to the town of Gospić. *(established)*
- On this map, Croatian-held areas and Serb-held areas are approximate, as they stood in 1993. *(page; reconstruction)*

### I3. Who is who
- The Croatian Army, the HV, and the special police of Croatia's Interior Ministry, the MUP. On this page they are red. *(page)*
- The Serb Krajina Army, the SVK, the army of the self-declared Republic of Serbian Krajina. On this page they are blue. *(page)*
- The United Nations Protection Force, UNPROFOR: here, a Canadian battalion with two French companies. On this page they are white and light blue. *(page)*
- And the people of the villages: farmers, many of them elderly. They belonged to no army. *(established)* [1]

### I4. Three memories
- In Croatia, the operation is remembered as a justified action to stop the shelling of Gospić. *(croatian sources)*
- Serb organisations remember the civilians who were killed, and the villages that were burned. *(serb sources)*
- In Canada, it is remembered as a forgotten battle, in which peacekeepers held their ground under fire. Some Croatian writers deny that any such battle took place. *(government: Canada; croatian sources; disputed)*

### I5. How this page tells it
- Each line is tagged with where it comes from. An accusation is not a verdict, and a count by an organisation is that organisation's count. *(page)*
- The numbers of the dead are on screen, in tables, each with who counted and what they counted. They cannot be added together. *(page)*
- This page shows no pictures of the dead, and describes no injuries. *(page)*
- No crime excuses another. Both Croats and Serbs in Lika suffered crimes in this war. *(page)*

## Part 1: Lika at war, 1991 to 1993

### 1. A war in Lika
*Map: Croatia · 1991*
- From 1991, much of inland Lika was held by Krajina Serb forces and, at first, the Yugoslav People's Army. *(established)*
- Civilians on both sides were driven from their homes, and killed. *(established)*
- In 2007, the United Nations tribunal for the former Yugoslavia convicted the Krajina Serb leader Milan Martić of crimes from 1991 to 1995. They included attacks on Croat villages, Lovinac among them. *(court: conviction)*
- In 2003, a Croatian court convicted a Croatian officer, Mirko Norac, of crimes against civilians in Gospić in 1991. We will meet him again in this story. *(court: conviction; press: IWPR)*

### 2. The United Nations arrives
*Map: Croatia · 1992*
- In early 1992 a ceasefire, the Vance plan, brought a United Nations force to Croatia. It was called the United Nations Protection Force, or UNPROFOR. *(established)*
- The Security Council created it on the twenty-first of February 1992, as a temporary arrangement. It did not decide who should rule the disputed areas. *(un)*
- The UN watched over protected areas, divided into sectors. Some places near the front line, called pink zones, lay just outside them. *(un)* [1, 2]

### 3. A finger of land
*Map: Lika · before 9 September 1993*
- South of Gospić, a row of small Serb villages reached into Croatian-held land: Divoselo, Čitluk, and Počitelj, with their hamlets. *(established)*
- A United Nations investigation later described it as "a finger of Serb-controlled land jutting into Croat territory". *(un)* [2]
- It lay in a pink zone, just outside the protected area called Sector South. *(un; established)* [1, 2]
- Serb sources call these "villages under United Nations protection". The United Nations documents place them outside the protected area. *(serb sources; un; disputed)* [1, 2]

### 4. The shelling of Gospić
*Map: Lika · summer 1993*
- In 1993, Gospić was often shelled by Krajina Serb artillery. Croatian sources describe raids and shelling that troubled the whole area around the town. *(croatian sources)*
- The Croatian human rights group Documenta, which has criticised the crimes of the operation, also writes that Gospić was a frequent target of Serb artillery in mid-1993. *(established)*
- Croatian sources give this shelling as the reason for the attack on the pocket. *(croatian sources)*

## Part 2: Operation "Pocket 93", 9 to 15 September

### 5. Early morning, the ninth of September
*Map: Lika · 9 September 1993, early morning*
- The Croatian name for the operation was "Džep devedeset tri": Pocket 93. *(croatian sources)*
- The attack began early in the morning of the ninth of September 1993. A United Nations investigation dates it to about six o'clock. *(un; established)* [2]
- The main thrust was the Croatian Army's 9th Guards Brigade, with the 111th Brigade, local Home Guard battalions, and special police. Their exact strength is uncertain. *(croatian sources; estimate)*
- According to the tribunal's indictment, General Rahim Ademi was the acting commander of the Gospić Military District, and Colonel Mirko Norac commanded the 9th Guards Brigade and a combat group called Sector One. *(indictment)* [1]
- A Croatian historian later described the Serb Krajina forces in Lika as "unprepared but not surprised". *(croatian sources)*

### 6. The villages taken
*Map: Pocket · 9 to 11 September 1993*
- Within days, Croatian forces took Divoselo, Čitluk and part of Počitelj, with their hamlets. *(established)*
- Many Serb villagers fled as the attack began. *(established)*
- In the Croatian view, the operation pushed Serb guns a few kilometres back from Gospić. One Croatian scholar called it "a relatively small-scale operation correcting the Gospić frontline". *(croatian sources)*
- How large an area was taken is disputed. Different sources give very different figures, shown on screen. *(disputed)*

### 7. The people of the pocket
*Map: Pocket · 9 to 17 September 1993*
- Civilians who stayed in their homes, many of them elderly, were killed, and so were Serb soldiers who had been captured or wounded. *(court: conviction; indictment)* [1, 6]
- The tribunal's prosecutor later accused Croatian forces of killing at least twenty-nine Serb civilians, and at least five Serb soldiers who had been captured or wounded. That is an accusation, not a verdict. *(indictment)* [1]
- Croatia's own courts later found that war crimes against civilians and prisoners had been committed. *(court: conviction)* [6]
- Other counts, by Serb organisations and Croatian prosecutors, differ. They are on screen, each with what it counts. *(estimate; disputed)*

### 8. Rockets on Croatian cities
*Map: Croatia · from 10 September 1993*
- From the tenth of September, Krajina Serb forces answered with rockets and missiles on Croatian cities, far to the north. *(established)*
- Reports name Karlovac, Lučko on the edge of Zagreb, Samobor and Jastrebarsko. Civilians were killed in Karlovac. *(press; estimate)*
- These were separate crimes, by different forces. No crime excuses another. *(page)*

### 9. An agreement
*Map: Lika · 15 September 1993, noon*
- Under military and international pressure, the two sides agreed that the United Nations would take control of the pocket. *(established)* [3]
- A ceasefire began at noon on the fifteenth of September. Croatian forces were to withdraw to the positions they held before the attack, and Serb forces were to stay out. *(established)* [3]
- The tribunal's indictment names the signatories: the Croatian general Petar Stipetić, and the Krajina Serb general Mile Novaković. *(indictment)* [1]
- The United Nations Secretary-General later called the operation "the incursion by the Croatian Army into the Medak pocket". *(un)*

## Part 3: The peacekeepers, 15 to 17 September

### 10. CANBAT One
*Map: Lika · 9 to 14 September 1993*
- The United Nations troops in this sector were a Canadian battle group: the Second Battalion, Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry. The UN called it CANBAT One. Its commander was Lieutenant-Colonel James Calvin. *(established)* [4]
- Two French mechanised companies, from the French battalions FREBAT One and FREBAT Three, served under Canadian command. *(canbat)* [4]
- The battle group's first units were still arriving in Sector South on the ninth of September, as Serb civilians fled the attack. *(established)*
- Veterans Affairs Canada says four Canadian soldiers were wounded during the operation. No Canadian was killed in the fighting. *(government: Canada)* [5]

### 11. The fifteenth of September
*Map: Pocket · 15 September 1993*
- On the fifteenth, Canadian C Company and a French company moved forward, to take over the Serb front-line positions. *(canbat)* [4]
- According to the Canadian operational report, Croatian forces fired on the United Nations soldiers "with small arms, heavy machine gun and in some instances 20 mm cannon fire". *(canbat)* [4]
- The report describes repeated exchanges of fire over about fifteen hours, some of them lasting up to ninety minutes. *(canbat)* [4]
- The same report records three minor shrapnel injuries earlier in the operation, and none during these exchanges. *(canbat)* [4]

### 12. The roadblock
*Map: Pocket · 16 September 1993*
- On the sixteenth of September, the Canadian column was stopped at a Croatian checkpoint. *(canbat)* [4]
- Instead of forcing a way through, Lieutenant-Colonel Calvin spoke to about twenty international journalists, there in front of the barrier. *(canbat; press)* [4]
- Canadian accounts say he told them the Croatian forces clearly had something to hide, and that the barrier was lifted soon after. *(government: Canada; press)* [5]
- Croatian troops withdrew over the sixteenth and seventeenth of September. *(established)*

### 13. What they found
*Map: Pocket · 16 and 17 September 1993*
- As the peacekeepers entered the villages, they found extensive destruction. Houses and barns had been burned, and they found the bodies of people who had been killed. *(canbat; un)* [2, 4]
- They also found three survivors, whom they protected and evacuated. Absence from a village does not mean that everyone there died. *(canbat)* [4]
- The Canadians carefully recorded what they found. Their work is said to have become standard United Nations practice for war crimes investigations. *(press: The Canadian Encyclopedia)*
- In 1993, the Croatian government rejected the United Nations' conclusions, and denied that there had been any planned or ordered destruction of the villages. *(government: Croatia)*

### 14. A battle, or not?
*Map: Pocket · 15 September 1993, and after*
- Canadian accounts call the fifteenth of September a battle. Veterans Affairs Canada says many consider it the toughest action faced by Canadian soldiers between Korea and Afghanistan. *(government: Canada)* [5]
- Croatian officials and writers deny it. Croatia's defence ministry said its soldiers did not fight the United Nations, and the Croatian historian Miroslav Međimorec has called the Canadian story "half-truths". *(croatian sources; disputed)*
- What Croatian losses there were in the exchanges, if any, is not known. Figures that circulate are unconfirmed, and this page does not use them. *(disputed; page)*

## Part 4: The courts

### 15. Accused in The Hague
*Map: Croatia · 2001 to 2004*
- The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, in The Hague, indicted General Rahim Ademi in 2001. He surrendered, and pleaded not guilty. *(established)*
- Mirko Norac was indicted later. In 2004 their two cases were joined into one. *(established)* [1]
- In 2002 the tribunal also indicted the chief of Croatia's General Staff, General Janko Bobetko. He died in 2003, before he could be transferred, and the case was closed. He was never tried, so he was never found guilty or innocent. *(indictment; established)*

### 16. Sent to Zagreb
*Map: Croatia · 2005*
- On the fourteenth of September 2005, the tribunal sent the case of Ademi and Norac to be tried in Croatia. It found that a fair trial and the protection of witnesses were assured there. *(court: referral)* [9]
- It was the first case the tribunal sent to Croatia. The referral was a decision about where to hold the trial, not a verdict. *(established; page)* [9]
- The Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, the OSCE, watched the trial for the tribunal's prosecutor. *(established)* [6]

### 17. The verdict
*Map: Croatia · 30 May 2008*
- On the thirtieth of May 2008, the County Court in Zagreb acquitted Rahim Ademi of all charges. *(court: acquittal)* [6]
- It acquitted Mirko Norac of ordering the crimes, but convicted him as a commander, for failing to prevent and punish crimes by his subordinates. He was sentenced to seven years. *(court: conviction)* [6]
- According to the tribunal prosecutor's final report, the findings that were upheld concerned the destruction of property, the mistreatment of prisoners, and the killing of four civilians and two prisoners of war. That was the scope of one man's conviction, not the number killed in the operation. *(court: conviction)* [6]
- Croatia's Supreme Court heard the appeals in November 2009. In March 2010 it upheld Ademi's acquittal, and reduced Norac's sentence to six years. *(court: appeal)* [6, 7]

### 18. After the verdict
*Map: Croatia · 2008 to 2020*
- The Zagreb County Court and Croatia's Supreme Court both accepted that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners had been committed. The argument was about who was responsible. *(court: conviction; court: appeal)* [6]
- Some Croatian veterans said the verdict condemned every soldier who fought with Norac. Human rights groups in Croatia and in Serbia said it brought too little justice to the victims. *(press)*
- In 2020, Croatia's Supreme Court upheld a three-year sentence for a former soldier, Josip Krmpotić, for ordering the destruction of civilian houses. He was acquitted of a separate charge of killing a prisoner. *(court: appeal; press: HRT)* [8]

## Part 5: Three memories

### 19. Gospić remembers
*Map: Lika · every September*
- Every September in Gospić, the Croatian state, veterans and the Church mark the anniversary of the operation, with a mass and wreaths. *(croatian sources)*
- Croatian officials call it a legitimate action to end the shelling of the town. *(government: Croatia)*
- A Croatian human rights group of young people says the Serb civilian victims are not mentioned at the state commemorations, and holds its own visits to Divoselo. *(press)*

### 20. Divoselo remembers
*Map: Pocket · every September*
- Among Serbs, the anniversary is marked with a memorial service in Belgrade organised by Veritas, and by a commemoration in Croatia held by the Serb National Council. *(serb sources)*
- Serb memory centres on the civilian dead, and on what Serb organisations see as justice not done. *(serb sources)*
- The villages never recovered. Few people live in Divoselo today. *(established)*

### 21. Canada remembers
*Map: Croatia · 1993 to today*
- For years, the action was little known in Canada. Some writers, among them the journalist Carol Off in her 2004 book, have argued that Ottawa kept it quiet. *(press; disputed)*
- In 2002, the Governor General, Adrienne Clarkson, presented the battle group with the Commander-in-Chief Unit Commendation, for its "courageous and professional execution of duty" in the Medak Pocket. *(government: Canada)*
- The same week of September 1993 is remembered as a liberation in Gospić, as a massacre in Divoselo, and as a battle in Canada. History asks what happened, to whom, and how we know. *(page)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- On the ninth of September 1993, Croatian forces took the Serb-held Medak Pocket, south of Gospić. *(established)*
- Serb Krajina forces answered with rockets on Croatian cities. *(established)*
- Under pressure, Croatia agreed to withdraw, and United Nations peacekeepers, Canadian and French, took over the area between the fifteenth and the seventeenth of September. *(established)*
- The peacekeepers found the villages destroyed, and bodies. Three survivors were evacuated. *(canbat)* [4]

### The courts
- The tribunal in The Hague accused two Croatian generals, then sent their case to Croatia. *(indictment; court: referral)*
- A Zagreb court acquitted Rahim Ademi, and convicted Mirko Norac for failing to prevent and punish crimes by his subordinates. The Supreme Court upheld both outcomes, and reduced Norac's sentence to six years. *(court: acquittal; court: conviction; court: appeal)* [6]
- Croatia's courts found that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners were committed. *(court: conviction)* [6]

### The numbers (see the tables on screen)
- The tribunal's prosecutor accused Croatian forces of killing at least twenty-nine civilians and at least five captured or wounded soldiers. *(indictment)* [1]
- Each count on this page counts something different. They cannot be added together. *(page)*

### Still unknown
- How many people were killed in the pocket in all, and who killed each of them. *(disputed)*
- The exact size of the forces on each side, and their losses. *(disputed)*
- What happened on the Croatian side during the exchanges of fire with the United Nations. *(disputed)*
