**Owner's rules for this page:**
- No copyrighted material: no press photographs, book covers, documentary stills or copied text. Books and articles (Carol
  Off's *The Ghosts of Medak Pocket*, Lee Windsor's article) are named and cited only.
- No graphic violence. Deaths are stated plainly, without methods, injuries or descriptions of the dead. No images of the
  dead, of bodies found, or of the wounded. The destroyed villages are shown only as map marks, not drawn as ruins with people.

**The approach:**
- **Five parts:** Lika at war; Operation "Pocket 93"; the peacekeepers; the courts; remembering. Introduction cards are
  I1–I5; chapters run 1–21 in page order.
- **Accusation, finding, claim.** An indictment is an accusation. A verdict is a finding. A count by an organisation is
  that organisation's count. The spoken words say which, every time. Rahim Ademi was acquitted and is never called guilty.
  Janko Bobetko died before trial; his case ended without a verdict.
- **The conviction's scope.** Mirko Norac's conviction concerned four civilians and two prisoners of war, according to the
  ICTY Prosecutor's final monitoring report (S6). That is not the number killed in the operation, and the narration says so.
  Commander responsibility is explained as a failure to take required action, not automatic guilt for every subordinate's act.
- **Counts stay separate.** Civilians killed, prisoners killed, soldiers killed in combat, and an organisation's total of
  its dead and missing are different things. They are never joined into one range, and never added together. The counts are
  in on-screen tables (below), each with source, date and category, not in the narration.
- **No crime excuses another.** The page says plainly that Croats in Lika also suffered at the hands of Serb forces from
  1991, and that Serb rockets were fired at Croatian population centres. These are separate acts by different forces.
  Not every strike is labelled a court-established crime.
- **No collective guilt.** A commander's conviction does not make a people, an army or every member of a unit guilty, and
  an acquittal or a limited conviction does not absolve anyone else.
- **Memories are attributed, not national.** Official Croatian commemorations, Serb organisations and Canadian
  commemorations are named, with dated examples. No line says what "Croatia", "Serbs" or "Canada" as a whole remembers.
- **The Canadian story is told from its own report.** "According to the Canadian operational report…" (S4). The
  unconfirmed figure of 27 Croatian casualties is not narrated. Croatian criticism of the Canadian account is mentioned
  without quotation until its texts are checked.
- **Spoken forms:** numbers and dates as the narrator says them. "Croatian Army" is said before HV, "Krajina Serb Army"
  before SVK, and CANBAT "One". Legal terms and source tags stay on screen; the narrator does not read the metadata.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Medak Pocket, in Croatia, from the ninth to the seventeenth of September 1993. Croatian forces attacked a small area of Serb-held villages near the town of Gospić. A week later, Canadian and French soldiers of the United Nations came to take it over, and found the villages destroyed. Croatian courts later found that war crimes had been committed there. The people involved remember these days very differently. We will tell you what is known, who says what, and what is still not known. Some chapters describe killings. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### I1. MEDAK POCKET 1993
- On the ninth of September 1993, the Croatian Army and police launched an attack on a Serb-held area south of Gospić, in the Lika region of Croatia. It was called the Medak Pocket. *(established)*
- After a week of fighting and international pressure, Croatia agreed to withdraw, and to hand the area to United Nations peacekeepers. *(established)* S3
- Canadian and French peacekeepers entered between the fifteenth and the seventeenth of September. They found extensive destruction, and bodies. They also found three survivors, whom they protected and evacuated. *(canbat)* S4
- In 2008 a court in Zagreb found that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners had been committed. It convicted one Croatian commander, and acquitted another. *(court: conviction; court: acquittal; monitoring report)* S6

### I2. Croatia and the Krajina
- In 1991, Croatia declared independence from Yugoslavia. Many Serbs in Croatia rejected it. With the support of the Yugoslav People's Army, they took control of large areas and declared their own state, the Republic of Serbian Krajina. *(established)*
- Lika is a high, wooded region of southern Croatia, between the coast mountains and Bosnia. In 1993 the front line ran close to the town of Gospić. *(established)*
- On this map, Croatian-held areas and Serb-held areas are approximate, as they stood in 1993. *(page; reconstruction)*

### I3. Who is who
- The Croatian Army, called the HV, and the special police of Croatia's Interior Ministry. On this page they are red. *(page)*
- The Krajina Serb Army, called the SVK, the army of the self-declared Republic of Serbian Krajina. On this page they are blue. *(page)*
- The United Nations Protection Force, UNPROFOR: here, a Canadian battalion with two French companies. On this page they are white and light blue. *(page)*
- The villages also had civilian inhabitants, including elderly people. *(established; indictment)* S1

### I4. Remembered differently
- Croatian official commemorations emphasise the operation's military purpose: to end the shelling of Gospić. *(government: Croatia)* S13
- Serb organisations commemorate the victims, and the destroyed villages. *(organisation: Veritas; organisation: SNV)*
- Canadian commemorations honour the peacekeepers. *(government: Canada)* S15
- The chapters at the end give named, dated examples of each. *(page)*

### I5. How this page tells it
- Each line is tagged with where it comes from. An accusation is not a verdict, and a count by an organisation is that organisation's count. *(page)*
- The numbers of the dead are on screen, in tables, each with who counted and what they counted. They cannot be added together. *(page)*
- This page shows no pictures of the dead, and describes no injuries. *(page)*
- No crime excuses another. Both Croats and Serbs in Lika suffered crimes in this war. *(page)*

## Part 1: Lika at war, 1991 to 1993

### 1. A war in Lika
*Map: Croatia · 1991*
- From 1991, much of inland Lika was held by Krajina Serb forces and, at first, the Yugoslav People's Army. *(established)*
- Civilians on both sides were driven from their homes, and killed. *(established)*
- In 2007, the United Nations tribunal for the former Yugoslavia convicted the Krajina Serb leader Milan Martić of war crimes and crimes against humanity. Its judgment also found that he took part in an attack on the Croat village of Lovinac in June 1991. *(court: conviction; court: finding)* S10
- In 2003, a court in Rijeka convicted a Croatian officer, Mirko Norac, of crimes against civilians in Gospić in 1991, and sentenced him to twelve years. We will meet him again in this story. *(court: conviction)* S16

### 2. The United Nations arrives
*Map: Croatia · 1992*
- A ceasefire in January 1992 opened the way for a United Nations deployment, under the Vance peace plan. *(established; un)* S11
- The Security Council established the United Nations Protection Force, UNPROFOR, on the twenty-first of February 1992. The peacekeeping arrangement was temporary, and did not settle the conflict. *(un)* S11
- The United Nations watched over protected areas, divided into sectors. Some places near the front line, called pink zones, lay just outside them. *(un)* S1, S2

### 3. A finger of land
*Map: Lika · before 9 September 1993*
- South of Gospić, a row of small Serb villages reached into Croatian-held land: Divoselo, Čitluk, and Počitelj, with their hamlets. *(established)* S1
- A United Nations investigation later described it as "a finger of Serb-controlled land jutting into Croat territory". *(un)* S2
- It lay in a pink zone, just outside the protected area called Sector South. *(un; indictment)* S1, S2
- Serb organisations call these villages that were under United Nations protection. Outside a designated protected area did not mean outside every United Nations responsibility. *(organisation: Veritas; un)* S11

### 4. The shelling of Gospić
*Map: Lika · summer 1993*
- Croatian accounts say that in 1993 the town of Gospić was often shelled by Krajina Serb artillery from the pocket area. *(croatian sources)*
- Croatia's Ministry of Defence presents the operation as a military action to end that threat to Gospić. *(government: Croatia)* S13

## Part 2: Operation "Pocket 93", 9 to 15 September

### 5. Early morning, the ninth of September
*Map: Lika · 9 September 1993, early morning*
- The Croatian name for the operation was "Džep devedeset tri": Pocket 93. *(croatian sources)*
- The attack began early in the morning of the ninth of September 1993. A United Nations investigation dates it to about six o'clock. *(un)* S2
- According to the tribunal's indictment, the attacking forces included the Croatian Army's 9th Guards Brigade, other army and Home Guard units, and special police of the Interior Ministry. Their exact strength is uncertain. *(indictment; estimate)* S1
- According to the indictment, Rahim Ademi was the acting commander of the Gospić Military District, and Mirko Norac commanded the 9th Guards Brigade and a combat group called Sector One. That is the prosecution's description, not a court finding. *(indictment)* S1

### 6. The villages taken
*Map: Pocket · 9 to 11 September 1993*
- Within days, Croatian forces took Divoselo, Čitluk and part of Počitelj, with their hamlets. *(established)* S1
- Many Serb villagers fled as the attack began. *(established)* S2
- In the Croatian official view, the operation pushed Serb guns back from Gospić. *(government: Croatia)* S13
- How large an area was taken is disputed. Different sources give very different figures, shown on screen. *(disputed)*

### 7. The people of the pocket
*Map: Pocket · 9 to 17 September 1993*
- Croatian courts later established that civilians and prisoners of war were killed. *(court: finding; monitoring report)* S6
- The tribunal's indictment also alleged killings of elderly civilians, and of Serb soldiers who had been captured or wounded. *(indictment)* S1
- It accused Croatian forces of killing at least twenty-nine Serb civilians, and at least five Serb soldiers who had been captured or wounded. That is an accusation, not a verdict. *(indictment)* S1
- Other counts, by Serb organisations and others, differ. They are on screen, each with what it counts. *(estimate; disputed)*

### 8. Rockets on Croatian cities
*Map: Croatia · 10 and 11 September 1993*
- The United Nations reported that Krajina Serb forces fired heavy rockets and missiles at Croatian population centres on the tenth and eleventh of September. *(un)* S12
- Those attacks do not excuse crimes in the pocket, and crimes in the pocket did not excuse them. *(page)*

### 9. An agreement
*Map: Lika · 15 September 1993, noon*
- Under military and international pressure, the two sides agreed that the United Nations would take control of the pocket. *(established)* S3
- The agreement called for a general ceasefire at noon on the fifteenth of September, and for Croatian forces to withdraw to the positions they held before the attack. Serb forces were to stay out. *(established)* S3
- The tribunal's indictment names the signatories: the Croatian general Petar Stipetić, and the Krajina Serb general Mile Novaković. *(indictment)* S1
- In a report that December, the United Nations Secretary-General called the operation an incursion by the Croatian Army into the Medak pocket. *(un)* S9

## Part 3: The peacekeepers, 15 to 17 September

### 10. CANBAT One
*Map: Lika · 9 to 14 September 1993*
- The United Nations troops in this sector were a Canadian battle group: the Second Battalion, Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry. The UN called it CANBAT One. Its commander was Lieutenant-Colonel James Calvin. *(established)* S4
- Two French mechanised companies, from the French battalions FREBAT One and FREBAT Three, served under Canadian command. *(canbat)* S4
- At the time of the attack, CANBAT One was establishing observation posts in the area. *(canbat)* S4
- Veterans Affairs Canada says four Canadian soldiers were wounded during the operation. No Canadian was killed in the fighting. *(government: Canada)* S5

### 11. The fifteenth of September
*Map: Pocket · 15 September 1993*
- On the fifteenth, Canadian C Company and a French company moved forward, to take over the Serb front-line positions. *(canbat)* S4
- According to the Canadian operational report, Croatian forces fired on the United Nations soldiers "with small arms, heavy machine gun and in some instances 20 mm cannon fire". *(canbat)* S4
- The report describes repeated exchanges of fire over about fifteen hours, some of them lasting up to ninety minutes. *(canbat)* S4
- The report suggests that Serb sniping near the United Nations positions may have played a part at first. It describes Croatian fire continuing after that. *(canbat)* S4

### 12. The roadblock
*Map: Pocket · 16 September 1993*
- On the sixteenth of September, the Canadian column was stopped at a Croatian checkpoint. *(canbat)* S4
- The Canadian report says Lieutenant-Colonel Calvin used the presence of journalists, filming the delays, to press for passage. The barrier was later lifted. *(canbat)* S4
- Croatian troops withdrew over the sixteenth and seventeenth of September. *(established)* S4

### 13. What they found
*Map: Pocket · 16 and 17 September 1993*
- As the peacekeepers entered the villages, they found extensive destruction. Houses and barns had been burned, and they found the bodies of people who had been killed. *(canbat; un)* S2, S4
- They also found three survivors, whom they protected and evacuated. Absence from a village does not mean that everyone there died. *(canbat)* S4
- The Canadians recorded evidence of what they found. *(canbat)* S4

### 14. A battle, or not?
*Map: Pocket · 15 September 1993, and after*
- Canadian accounts call the fifteenth of September a battle. Veterans Affairs Canada says it is considered by many to be the toughest action faced by Canadian soldiers between the wars in Korea and Afghanistan. *(government: Canada)* S5
- Some Croatian writers dispute the Canadian account of a battle. *(croatian sources; disputed)*
- What Croatian losses there were in the exchanges, if any, is not known. Figures that circulate are unconfirmed, and this page does not use them. *(disputed; page)*

## Part 4: The courts

### 15. Accused in The Hague
*Map: Croatia · 2001 to 2004*
- The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, in The Hague, indicted Rahim Ademi in 2001. He surrendered, and pleaded not guilty. *(established)*
- Mirko Norac was indicted later. In 2004 their two cases were joined into one. *(established)* S1
- In 2002 the tribunal also indicted the chief of Croatia's General Staff, General Janko Bobetko. He died before trial, and the case ended without a verdict. *(indictment; established)*

### 16. Sent to Zagreb
*Map: Croatia · 2005*
- On the fourteenth of September 2005, the tribunal sent the case of Ademi and Norac to be tried in Croatia. Its referral bench considered that a fair trial and the protection of witnesses could be provided there. *(court: referral)* S19
- It was the first case the tribunal sent to Croatia. The referral was a decision about where to hold the trial, not a verdict. *(established; page)* S19
- The Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, the OSCE, watched the trial for the tribunal's prosecutor. *(monitoring report)* S6, S7

### 17. The verdict
*Map: Croatia · 30 May 2008*
- On the thirtieth of May 2008, the County Court in Zagreb acquitted Rahim Ademi of all charges. *(court: acquittal; monitoring report)* S6
- It acquitted Mirko Norac of ordering the crimes. It convicted him as a commander, for failing to take the action required of him: to prevent crimes by his subordinates, and to punish them. He was sentenced to seven years. *(court: conviction; monitoring report)* S6
- According to the tribunal prosecutor's final monitoring report, the findings that were upheld concerned the destruction of property, the mistreatment of prisoners, and the killing of four civilians and two prisoners of war. That was the scope of one man's conviction, not the number killed in the operation. *(court: finding; monitoring report)* S6
- Croatia's Supreme Court heard the appeals in November 2009. In March 2010 it upheld Ademi's acquittal, and reduced Norac's sentence to six years. *(court: appeal; monitoring report)* S6, S7

### 18. After the verdict
*Map: Croatia · 2008 to 2020*
- The Zagreb County Court and Croatia's Supreme Court both accepted that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners had been committed. The legal argument was about who was responsible. *(court: finding; monitoring report)* S6
- In 2020, Croatia's Supreme Court upheld Josip Krmpotić's three-year sentence for ordering civilian houses burned. He was acquitted of the allegation that he failed to prevent the killing of four Serb prisoners. *(court: appeal; press: HRT and Hina)* S8

## Part 5: Remembering

### 19. Gospić remembers
*Map: Lika · September*
- In September 2026, at the thirty-third anniversary in Gospić, officials and veterans attended a mass and a commemorative ceremony. *(government: Croatia; press: HRT)* S17
- Croatia's Ministry of Defence presents the operation as a military action to protect the town. *(government: Croatia)* S13
- In September 2023, the Youth Initiative for Human Rights joined a visit to the villages and a commemoration by the Serb National Council. The initiative said that Serb civilian victims were left out of the official state commemorations. *(organisation: YIHR)* S14

### 20. Divoselo remembers
*Map: Pocket · September*
- Serb organisations, among them Veritas and the Serb National Council, hold commemorations for the dead of the pocket. *(organisation: Veritas; organisation: SNV)* S14
- Their remembrance centres on the civilian victims, and on what they see as justice not done. *(organisation: Veritas; organisation: SNV)*
- In its 2023 account of a visit, the Youth Initiative for Human Rights described the villages' greatly reduced population. *(organisation: YIHR)* S14

### 21. Canada remembers
*Map: Croatia · 1993 to today*
- In Canada, the action is often called a forgotten battle. *(press)*
- On the first of December 2002, the Governor General, Adrienne Clarkson, presented the battle group with the Commander-in-Chief Unit Commendation, for its "courageous and professional execution of duty" in the Medak Pocket. *(government: Canada)* S15
- Official commemorations in Gospić, Serb organisations' memorials, and Canadian honours each tell part of this story. History asks what happened, to whom, and how we know. *(page)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- On the ninth of September 1993, Croatian forces launched an attack on the Serb-held Medak Pocket, south of Gospić. *(established)*
- The United Nations reported Krajina Serb rockets and missiles fired at Croatian population centres. *(un)* S12
- Under pressure, Croatia agreed to withdraw, and United Nations peacekeepers, Canadian and French, took over the area between the fifteenth and the seventeenth of September. *(established)* S3, S4
- The peacekeepers found the villages destroyed, and bodies. Three survivors were evacuated. *(canbat)* S4

### The courts
- The tribunal in The Hague accused two Croatian commanders, then sent their case to Croatia. *(indictment; court: referral)* S1, S19
- A Zagreb court acquitted Rahim Ademi, and convicted Mirko Norac for failing to prevent and punish crimes by his subordinates. The Supreme Court upheld both outcomes, and reduced Norac's sentence to six years. *(court: acquittal; court: conviction; court: appeal; monitoring report)* S6
- Croatia's courts found that war crimes against Serb civilians and prisoners were committed. *(court: finding; monitoring report)* S6

### The numbers (see the tables on screen)
- The tribunal's prosecutor accused Croatian forces of killing at least twenty-nine civilians, and at least five captured or wounded soldiers. *(indictment)* S1
- Each count on this page counts something different. They cannot be added together. *(page)*

### Still unknown
- The complete death toll, and responsibility for killings not resolved by the courts, remain unsettled. *(disputed)*
- The exact size of the forces on each side, and their losses. *(disputed)*
- What happened on the Croatian side during the exchanges of fire with the United Nations. *(disputed)*
