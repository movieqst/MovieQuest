## **intro-1**: BATTLE OF WATERLOO
- Napoleon had escaped from Elba and returned to power. He had 100 days, and this was the battle that decided them.
- The Allied army and the Prussians had been driven apart two days before. Wellington took the ridge at Mont-Saint-Jean; Blücher was coming to join him.
- This introduction separates what the sources say from what historians have reconstructed. Watch for the coloured tags.

## **intro-2**: The ridge
- Wellington placed most of his infantry on the reverse slope of the ridge, out of sight and out of the range of the French guns.
- The sunken road along the crest, bordered by hedges, was the line the French had to reach.
- Hougoumont, a walled farm complex, stood on the Allied right. La Haye Sainte, a farm on the Brussels road, stood in the centre.
- It had rained hard all night. The French attack did not begin until the late morning, when the ground had dried a little.

## **intro-3**: The French army
- Napoleon massed the guns of the army in a grand battery opposite the centre-left of the Allied line. The number of guns is disputed: sources give between about forty and eighty.
- D’Erlon’s infantry corps attacked the Allied left. Later in the afternoon, cuirassiers and lancers charged the Allied right-centre again and again.
- The French army numbered about 72,000, the Allied about 68,000, and the Prussians about 50,000. These are common estimates; the sources do not agree.

## **intro-4**: The Prussians
- Blücher had been beaten at Ligny two days before. His army withdrew, and then came toward Waterloo.
- The first Prussians, Bülow’s corps, reached the French right in the late afternoon. The exact time is disputed; the sources give between about 16:30 and 17:00.
- Napoleon had sent Marshal Grouchy with about 33,000 men to follow the Prussians. Grouchy fought their rearguard at Wavre and never reached Waterloo.

## **intro-5**: The sources
- Many of the stories of this battle were written by the men who lived through it, decades afterwards. Some were written to defend a commander’s reputation.
- The French side has its own collection of eyewitness accounts, many published for the first time in recent years.
- Where a story is famous but not confirmed, the Record says so.

## **event-1**: The ridge
Wellington’s infantry lies on the reverse slope. The guns stand on the crest. The French army waits on the far side of the valley.

**explain-1**: Why the reverse slope? Infantry behind the crest could not be shelled. They could stand up and fire when the French came over the top.

## **event-2**: Hougoumont
The battle opens at Hougoumont, on the Allied right, in the late morning. Reille’s infantry attacks the walled farm, and the fighting there goes on all day.

**explain-2**: Many historians think the attack was meant to draw in Wellington’s reserves. Instead it drew in more and more French battalions. The defenders closed the north gate after the French forced it: a famous story, told differently by different witnesses.

## **event-3**: The grand battery opens
The French grand battery fires on the Allied line. The number of guns is disputed.

**explain-3**: Napoleon massed the guns to break the Allied line before the infantry attack. How many guns there were, and how much damage they did, is not agreed.

## **event-4**: d’Erlon’s attack
D’Erlon’s corps advances against the Allied left-centre, east of the Brussels road. Picton’s division meets it at the hedges along the crest.

**explain-4**: D’Erlon’s divisions advanced in unusually broad, deep formations, which were slow to deploy and made big targets. Sir Thomas Picton was killed leading the counter-attack.

## **event-5**: The Union Brigade charges
Uxbridge sends the heavy cavalry over the crest. The Union Brigade, with the Scots Greys, breaks d’Erlon’s attack and captures two eagles.

**explain-5**: Carried away, the cavalry rode on into the grand battery and was cut up by French lancers and cuirassiers. How far it got, and how many guns it put out of action, is disputed.

## **event-6**: Ney’s cavalry charges
Marshal Ney sends wave after wave of cuirassiers and lancers up the slope between Hougoumont and La Haye Sainte, against the Allied right-centre.

**explain-6**: Why Ney attacked is disputed: he may have taken movement behind the crest for a retreat. The charges went in without infantry to support them.

## **event-7**: Squares hold
The Allied infantry forms square. The French cavalry cannot break the squares, and falls back.

**explain-7**: A square with bayonets outward was the infantry’s answer to cavalry. Without infantry or guns to follow up, the cavalry could not break it.

## **event-8**: The Prussians at Plancenoit
Bülow’s Prussians come in from the east and attack Plancenoit, on the French right. Lobau’s corps is sent to hold them.

**explain-8**: The Prussian arrival forced Napoleon to commit troops to his right, away from the centre. The exact time is disputed; the sources give between about 16:30 and 17:00.

## **event-9**: La Haye Sainte falls
Out of ammunition, the King’s German Legion is driven out of La Haye Sainte. French guns and skirmishers come up close to the centre of the Allied line.

**explain-9**: Major Baring’s men held the farm for most of the day, until their rifle ammunition ran out. With the farm lost, the Allied centre was at its weakest of the day.

## **event-10**: Zieten reaches the Allied left
Zieten’s Prussian corps reaches Wellington’s far left. The Allied light cavalry on that flank can now move to the centre.

**explain-10**: Zieten’s arrival freed Vivian’s and Vandeleur’s cavalry brigades to steady the Allied centre, shortly before the Imperial Guard attacked.

## **event-11**: The last attack
The Imperial Guard goes forward in the last French attack. Wellington’s reserves meet it on the crest.

**explain-11**: The Guard’s attack is the last throw of the day. The words attributed to its officers are disputed, and the Record does not repeat them as fact.

## **event-12**: The aftermath
The French attack is broken. The Prussians press on, and the French army retreats south. Napoleon abdicated three days later.

**explain-12**: The battle ended the Napoleonic Wars. Its result is not in doubt; the detail of who did what, and when, is what the sources argue about.

## **outcome**
- The French attacks on the ridge failed, and the Prussians came in on the French right.
- Napoleon’s army broke and retreated south. Napoleon abdicated three days later, and the Napoleonic Wars ended.
- The sources give very different casualty figures. Historians treat them as estimates.
- The stories of the day, such as the Hougoumont gate and the Scots Greys charge, are told from both sides, and not always accurately.
