## 3. GAO original: requested checks

Source: **US General Accounting Office, NSIAD-89-159, 4 May 1989**, https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf . Page numbers here are the printed page numbers; PDF positions count the cover and intervening pages.

### Chapter 20: six camps — PASS

Printed **p. 9** (PDF page 11) says:

> “In July 1988, UNHCR and others noticed that some of the refugees at six … of the 14 refugee camps in Somalia were carrying arms.”

The ellipsis removes a footnote marker/line transition, not substantive words. Draft 2 correctly credits the UN refugee agency and other observers. It does not claim that GAO staff witnessed this in July. Printed **p. 1** records GAO's own February–March 1989 visit.

### Chapter 20: shipment — PASS

Printed **p. 8** (PDF page 10):

> “It finally arrived on June 28 at the port of Berbera and was used by the government at a critical point in the conflict.”

Printed **p. 9** (PDF page 11):

> “A senior Somali military official confirmed that the U.S. equipment was distributed to troops in the north and was used during the conflict.”

Draft 2 accurately separates GAO's finding about use in the northern conflict from Africa Watch's rifles-to-refugees account. GAO does not establish that every armed refugee received weapons from this specific consignment.

### Chapter 14: seventy per cent — PASS

Printed **p. 6** (PDF page 8):

> “The U.S. Embassy estimated that 70 percent of the city has been damaged or destroyed. Our rough visual inspection confirms this estimate.”

Draft 2 correctly treats this as an embassy estimate supported by a rough GAO visual inspection, not an engineering census. It also correctly distinguishes the State Department's different Isaaq-property measure.

### Additional GAO findings relevant to the final check

Printed p. 4 explicitly dates the recapture of Burao to 30 May and Hargeisa to 13 July; p. 6 mentions military control since late July. These are the basis of the chronology correction in section 1. The inconsistent Hargeisa dates are a reason for qualification, not a reason to omit the source. Printed p. 6 also attributes the suggested broader pattern of attacks on refugees to what investigators were told. Chapter 21 should preserve that distinction.

The quotations above come from an official US government report. They are included to make the requested verification reproducible; the narrative should continue using the shorter paraphrases.
