## 1. The Siad Barre regime: coup, ideology, clan basis of power, Ogaden War and refugees in the north

### Takeaway
Siad Barre took power in a coup on 21 October 1969. Officially the regime opposed clannism, but in practice power rested on the "MOD" clans (Marehan, Ogaden, Dhulbahante). After the defeat in the Ogaden War (1977–78), very large numbers of Ogaden refugees settled in the north. Some were armed, including WSLF fighters, and Isaaq sources and later reports describe these armed men as a force inside Isaaq territory and accuse them of abuses.

### Cited Findings
- Siad Barre led the coup of 21 October 1969, a few days after President Abdirashid Ali Sharmarke was assassinated. At the time Barre commanded the army. The new ruling body was the Supreme Revolutionary Council (SRC), which aligned itself with the USSR. — [Wikipedia: Siad Barre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siad_Barre); [Wikipedia: Supreme Revolutionary Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Revolutionary_Council_(Somalia))
- "MOD" stands for Marehan, Ogaden and Dhulbahante. Marehan was Barre's own clan and Ogaden his mother's. Dhulbahante was the clan of his son-in-law Col. Ahmad Sulaymaan Abdullah, head of the National Security Service (NSS). — [Wikipedia: MOD Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOD_Alliance) (the article draws on the Library of Congress country study)
- Despite its anti-clan rhetoric, the regime raised members of the president's family clans into its inner circle. — [Wikipedia: MOD Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOD_Alliance); scholarly treatment in Ingiriis, *The Suicidal State in Somalia: The Rise and Fall of the Siad Barre Regime, 1969–1991* (2016) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383901297_The_Suicidal_State_in_Somalia_The_Rise_and_Fall_of_the_Siad_Barre_Regime_1969-1991)
- An academic paper says this arrangement "systematically barred the Northerners, except the Dhulbahante". It also says the excluded northern clans made up about 85% of the former British Somaliland's population. Treat this as an author's estimate. — via search extract of [Ingiriis, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383901297_The_Suicidal_State_in_Somalia_The_Rise_and_Fall_of_the_Siad_Barre_Regime_1969-1991) (attribution in the extract is uncertain)
- A Max Planck working paper (Hoehne) says Barre gave "key positions in the government and the military to people from Marrexaan, Dhulbahante and Ogadeen", the clans he was related to through his father, his mother and his marriage. — [MPI Social Anthropology Working Paper 82](https://www.eth.mpg.de/pubs/wps/pdf/mpi-eth-working-paper-0082.pdf)
- Isaaq grievances predate Barre. A publication cited by the US DOJ Resource Information Center lists Darod and Hawiye domination of power after independence and the south's dominance over the north. It names the abortive 1961 coup by northern officers as an early sign of this dissatisfaction. — [US DOJ RIC, Somalia SOM98002](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/vll/country/ric_reports/Somalia/SOM98002.pdf)
- After the 1977–78 war, Barre armed Ogaden refugees and so "created an irregular army operating inside Isaaq territories" (Wikipedia, summarising scholarly sources). — [Wikipedia: Afraad Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afraad_Rebellion)
- Africa Watch (1990), as quoted on Wikipedia, reported that the WSLF "terrorized the Isaak civilian population living in the border region, who came to fear them more than the Ethiopian army". Killings, looting and rapes of civilians by WSLF fighters were reported from 1978 onward. In 1979 an Isaaq delegation of elders asked Barre to stop the abuses, but the violence continued. — [Wikipedia: Afraad Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afraad_Rebellion) (citing Africa Watch 1990)
- The Afraad were Isaaq fighters of the WSLF's Fourth Brigade. They rebelled in 1978, clashed with the WSLF after 1979, won broad Isaaq support by 1982, pushed many WSLF fighters out of the Haud, and joined the SNM. — [Wikipedia: Afraad Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afraad_Rebellion)
- By 1980 the refugee camps held "hundreds of thousands". Imported donor food was distributed under the control of Somali camp commanders and staff. — [Uppsala/DiVA report "Somali Refugees in the Horn of Africa"](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:272940/fulltext01.pdf)
- The US GAO (1989) reported that the Somali government told UNHCR the refugees were "armed for their own protection". This is the government's stated position. — [GAO NSIAD-89-159](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf)
- Some Somali historiography (as summarised in search extracts) says that in the north, "the Isaaq clans confronted a massive influx of Ogadeni refugees from eastern Ethiopia whom Siyad encouraged to loot property, attack people, and destabilize cities". This is an interpretive claim. — [Wikipedia: Afraad Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afraad_Rebellion)

### Inferences
- Several conflicts fed into the war of 1988. One was the Isaaq versus WSLF/Ogaden refugee rivalry over the Haud grazing lands, which began in 1978–79. Another was the long-standing north–south grievance dating from 1960–61. A third was the MOD's exclusion of the Isaaq from power.
- The government's stated line was that the refugees were armed for self-defence (to GAO/UNHCR). Isaaq and Africa Watch accounts describe these armed refugees as a proxy force.

### Gaps
- No primary-source extract was found on "scientific socialism" (the regime's ideology from 1970). It is widely documented in Lewis's *Modern History of the Somali*, but that was not reached in this session.
- No exact refugee numbers settled in the north (Togdheer, Woqooyi Galbeed) were found. The overall camp population figures commonly cited (several hundred thousand up to more than 1 million, a disputed figure) could not be checked.
- No date was found for when refugees were formally recruited into army units.

## 2. Grievances in the north: economic measures, arrests, emergency rule, the Uffo trial and February 1982

### Takeaway
In the 1980s the regime placed the north under military administration, applied emergency powers (a Regional Security Council and a Mobile Military Court that could pass death sentences), and took measures that hit Isaaq commerce (franco valuta abolished in 1982, qaad banned in 1983–84). The February 1982 trial of the "Uffo" group in Hargeisa (young professionals who had organised volunteer improvements at Hargeisa Group Hospital) set off student demonstrations, the "Dhagax Tuur" (stone-throwing) protests. The exact dates are disputed between sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Military administration:** The World Bank (2005) says Isaaq grievances deepened in the 1980s "when the Barre regime placed the northwest under military control and used the military administration to crack down on the Isaaq and dispossess them of their businesses". — [World Bank, Conflict in Somalia: Drivers and Dynamics (Jan 2005)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/655761468332449283/pdf/360320SO0Conflict0in0Somalia01PUBLIC1.pdf)
- **Emergency powers:** Amnesty International (1999) writes that "extraordinary powers were given to the military and police under draconian emergency legislation" and were used to repress the Isaaq, who were accused of supporting the SNM. A Regional Security Council and a Mobile Military Court were set up with powers to arrest and impose the death penalty. — [Amnesty, Human rights in Somaliland: Awareness and action (AFR 52/001/1999)](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)
- Amnesty attributes a 1986 identity card system in the north to the military governor, Gen. Mohamed Said Hersi "Morgan". — [Amnesty AFR 52/001/1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)
- **Qaad:** An Amnesty footnote says "In March 1983 qaat was banned", and that qaat farms were affected. The extract is truncated. — [Amnesty AFR 52/001/1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf). An Oxford QEH working paper says the regime banned qat production and "in 1984 qat fields were destroyed by government teams". — [QEH Working Paper 100](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps100.pdf)
- **Franco valuta:** This system let traders import goods with privately held foreign currency. It was officially abolished in 1982, under the IMF adjustment programme, because of foreign-exchange pressures, but in practice it continued. Some northern businessmen saw the cancellation as a setback, and at least one was jailed in the early 1980s on suspicion of franco valuta dealing. — [QEH Working Paper 100](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps100.pdf); [FES, "Migration-Development Nexus: Somalia"](https://library.fes.de/libalt/journals/swetsfulltext/16725935.pdf)
- **Livestock:** Livestock exporters had to surrender only 40% of their foreign-exchange earnings to the government. — [QEH Working Paper 100](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps100.pdf)
- **Merchants:** Wikipedia (summarising sources) says the regime imprisoned wealthy Isaaq merchants and traders to stop them funding the SNM. — [Wikipedia: Isaaq genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaaq_genocide)
- **Uffo group:** This was a group of about 28 young Hargeisa professionals (doctors, teachers and others) who started a voluntary programme at Hargeisa Group Hospital. They were arrested in late 1981 and charged with treason/subversion for belonging to an illegal organisation, a charge that carried a mandatory death sentence. — Somaliland sources: [Somaliland Standard, "The Rebirth of Somaliland (9): The UFO Group"](http://somalilandstandard.com/the-rebirth-of-somaliland-9-hargeisa-group-hospital-the-ufo-group/); [Somaliland Sun](https://somalilandsun.com/somalilandpresident-hosts-heroic-ufo-survivors/); [DLP research paper](https://dlprog.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/mwU9JokvSErECzBekElE0bUwcjau3CcsTT7QYwSW.pdf)
- **Trial and protests, February 1982 (dates disputed):**
  - Wikipedia gives the trial as 20 February 1982, with sentences on 6 March. It says the riots in Hargeisa lasted three days. — [Wikipedia: Somaliland War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somaliland_War_of_Independence)
  - A Future Generations case study gives 22 February 1982. It says the government answered with a curfew, arrests of "trouble-makers", and closure of the road from Djibouti. — [Future Generations, Somaliland peacebuilding case study](https://www.future.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/somaliland-peacebuilding.pdf)
  - Somaliland press dates the charges to 19 February 1982, the "stone-throwing" (Dhagax Tuur) to 20 February 1982, and the conviction to 23 February 1982. — [Somaliland Sun](https://somalilandsun.com/somalilandpresident-hosts-heroic-ufo-survivors/)
  - The DLP research paper says the student demonstrations left several people killed and hundreds arrested. — [DLP](https://dlprog.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/mwU9JokvSErECzBekElE0bUwcjau3CcsTT7QYwSW.pdf)
- After the government suppressed the 1982 student protests in Hargeisa, the SNM set up in Ethiopia and began cross-border raids. — summary in [US DOJ RIC](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/vll/country/ric_reports/Somalia/SOM98002.pdf)
- **Amnesty, June 1988 (*Torture in Somalia*):** Since 1981 the government's response to the SNM in the north had included "widespread arbitrary arrests, ill-treatment, and summary executions by government military and security forces" of civilians suspected of collaboration. Many people were jailed for criticising northern policy, or simply for being Isaaq. — [Amnesty, Torture in Somalia (June 1988), Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/417578891/Amnesty-International-Torture-in-Somalia-June-1988); see also [Amnesty POL 10/0003/1990](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/POL1000031990ENGLISH.pdf)

### Inferences
- The qaad ban and the end of franco valuta were national measures, framed by the government as economic or IMF policy. They fell especially hard on northern trade networks. Whether they were aimed at the Isaaq is a matter of interpretation; the World Bank text supports dispossession under military rule in general terms.
- For the trial and protest dates, the safe wording is "around 20 February 1982". Note that sources give 19, 20 and 22 February for the trial and 23 February or 6 March for the sentences.

### Gaps
- The Uffo verdicts (names, sentences, the leading defendants such as Dr Adan Yusuf Abokor) were not confirmed from a primary source in this session.
- No source was found for a Saudi ban on Somali livestock imports (commonly dated 1983 and linked to rinderpest). It is widely cited as an economic blow to Berbera, but it is unverified here.
- No text of the emergency regulations was found, and no date of the first emergency in the north (some sources link a state of emergency to the aftermath of the January 1983 Mandera raid; see section 3).
- Further student protests (for example in Burao and Hargeisa, 1984) were not documented in the sources found.

## 3. The Somali National Movement (SNM): founding, leaders, Ethiopian bases, raids 1982–1988

### Takeaway
The SNM was announced in London on 6 April 1981 by Isaaq emigrés. Wikipedia says a formal founding took place in Jeddah. It moved its headquarters to Ethiopia in 1982 and carried out cross-border raids with Ethiopian (Mengistu) backing; the best known is the Mandera prison raid near Berbera on 2 January 1983. Its chairmen up to 1988 were Ahmed Mohamed Gulaid (first), a disputed 1982–83 chairman, Col. Abdiqadir Kosar Abdi (1983–84) and Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud "Silanyo" (1984–1990).

### Cited Findings
- The Library of Congress country study says the SNM was "formed in London on April 6, 1981, by 400 to 500 Isaaq emigres" and "remained an Isaaq clan-family organization dedicated to ridding the country of Siad Barre". It later became the strongest insurgent movement. — [LoC/DTIC Somalia: A Country Study (1993)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA264997.pdf); [countrystudies.us](https://countrystudies.us/somalia/30.htm)
- A Somaliland source says the formation was announced at Connaught Hall, London, on 6 April 1981. Wikipedia instead says the formal founding was in Jeddah in April 1981, so the place is disputed (London announcement vs. Jeddah founding). — [Horn Diplomat, "Rebirth of Somaliland (5)"](https://www.horndiplomat.com/?p=16534); [Wikipedia: SNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_National_Movement)
- A USCIS report says the SNM was created in 1981 in the UK by Isaaq exiles. It was mostly Isaaq but included members of other clans, and it moved its headquarters to Ethiopia in 1982. — [USCIS/INS "Somalia: Things Fall Apart" (Jan 1993)](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/ins/somala93.pdf); [Refworld copy](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/uscis/1993/93809)
- **Chairmen (as listed on Wikipedia and Somaliland sources):**
  - Ahmed Mohamed Gulaid, first chairman, with Ahmed Ismail Abdi "Duqsi" as secretary. — [Horn Diplomat](https://www.horndiplomat.com/?p=16534); [Wikipedia: SNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_National_Movement)
  - 1982–83: sources conflict. One lists Sheikh Yusuf Ali Sheikh Madar, another Hassan Adan Wadadid. — [Wikipedia: SNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_National_Movement); [Military Wiki](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Somali_National_Movement)
  - 1983–84: Col. Abdiqadir Kosar Abdi. — [Wikipedia: Abdiqadir Kosar Abdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdiqadir_Kosar_Abdi)
  - 1984–1990: Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud "Silanyo", the longest-serving chairman. — [Wikipedia: Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Mohamed_Mohamoud)
  - No source found names an "Ahmed Jimaale" as chairman.
- **Mandera raid, 2 January 1983:** SNM fighters from Haud bases, led by Mohamed Hashi Lihle, attacked Mandera prison near Berbera and freed Isaaq political prisoners. On the same day a commando unit raided the Adadley armoury near Berbera. — [Wikipedia: Mohamed Hashi Lihle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Hashi_Lihle); [Wikipedia: Mandera Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandera_Prison); [country-studies.com, Opposition Movements](https://country-studies.com/somalia/opposition-movements.html)
  - The number freed is disputed. The SNM claimed "hundreds"; "subsequent independent estimates" said about 700. The Wikipedia Mandera Prison article gives both 744 and 664. Another account suggests only a few dozen. — same sources
  - Wikipedia says Mogadishu responded with a state of emergency, a curfew, and a search for the escaped prisoners. — [Wikipedia: Mandera Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandera_Prison)
- The SNM depended on Ethiopian sanctuary. The April 1988 accord required Ethiopia to stop support and expel SNM forces (see section 5). — [Wikipedia: 1988 Hargeisa-Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive); [IRB/ecoi](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1222426.html)
- Somaliland narrative sources on SNM operations from 1982 to 1988: [Saxafi Media, "Rebirth of Somaliland (6)"](https://saxafimedia.com/the-rebirth-of-somaliland-6-the-snm-liberation-struggle-and-tactical-operations/). These are partisan.
- UCDP lists the SNM as an armed actor. — [UCDP actor 1105](https://ucdp.uu.se/actor/1105)

### Inferences
- The SNM had both a diaspora/political wing (London, Gulf) and an armed wing based in the Haud and Ethiopia. Its Ethiopian bases tied its fate to Somali–Ethiopian relations.

### Gaps
- Not confirmed: the October 1982 SNM congress in Ethiopia, and the exact dates of the chairmanship handovers.
- No reliable list of SNM raids between 1984 and 1987 (for example on Burao, Sheikh or Erigavo areas) was found.
- The scale of Ethiopian material support is not documented here.

## 4. Government officials and measures 1981–1988; Gani, Morgan and the "Letter of Death"

### Takeaway
Gen. Mohamed Hashi Gani commanded the 26th Sector/Division (Hargeisa) around 1980–85. Gen. Mohamed Said Hersi "Morgan" (Barre's son-in-law) commanded it and acted as military governor of the north from around 1986. A document attributed to Morgan, known as the "Morgan Report" or "Letter of Death", was dated January 1987 (sources give 21 or 23 January). Described as a top-secret report to the president on "implemented and recommended measures" for a "final solution" to the "Isaaq problem", it circulated in the press in 1987. Its authenticity is asserted by Isaaq/Somaliland sources and its translator. No independent forensic verification was found, and reputable outlets describe it as "attributed to" or "allegedly authored by" Morgan.

### Cited Findings
- **Gani:** Mohamed Hashi Gani commanded the 26th Division, with tenure listed as 1980–1985. Isaaq northerners accused him of overseeing torture and killings, and one account says he launched a crackdown on the SNM in 1983. This rests on single, Wikipedia-derived sources and is uncorroborated. — [Wikipedia: Mohammed Hashi Gani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Hashi_Gani); [Wikipedia: 26th Division (Somalia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/26th_Division_(Somalia))
- The International Crisis Group (2003) mentions ties with "General Mohamed Hashi Gani, one of Barre's..." (extract truncated). — [ICG, Somaliland: Democratisation and its Discontents](https://www.crisisgroup.org/sites/default/files/66-somaliland-democratisation-and-its-discontents.pdf)
- **Morgan:** He was military commander of the 26th Sector (north-west) from 1986 to 1988; the 26th Division list gives 1986 to 1989/90. Amnesty calls him "the military governor" in 1986. He was Barre's son-in-law. — [Wikipedia: Mohammed Said Hersi Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Said_Hersi_Morgan); [Amnesty AFR 52/001/1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)
- **"Letter of Death" / Morgan Report:**
  - Described as "officially a top secret report to the president on 'implemented and recommended measures' for a 'final solution' to Somalia's 'Isaaq problem'". — [Wikipedia: Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Said_Hersi_Morgan)
  - Dates given: January 1987; 21 January 1987; 23 January 1987. Some accounts say it leaked in February 1987. — [Wikipedia: Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Said_Hersi_Morgan); [isaaq-genocide.pages.dev, "Death Letter"](https://isaaq-genocide.pages.dev/learn/doc/death-letter) (advocacy site); [Horn Diplomat, "Rebirth of Somaliland (11)"](https://www.horndiplomat.com/?p=16625)
  - Press circulation is reportedly cited to *Africa Now* (July 1987) and *Africa Confidential* (1987). These were not seen directly. — via search summary of [Horn Diplomat (11)](https://www.horndiplomat.com/?p=16625)
  - Status: a 2025 news report on Morgan's death calls it a document "attributed to him ... allegedly authored in January 1987". — [ICB News, 29 May 2025](https://icbnews.co.ke/2025/05/29/general-morgan-somalias-controversial-military-figure-dies-in-nairobi/). The translator of the English version argues that the signature is Morgan's and the document genuine. This is a partisan source. — [Saxafi Media, "Morgan's Death Letter"](https://saxafimedia.com/morgan-death-letter-final-solution-somalias-isaaq-problem/)
  - One commentator reads the letter as Morgan describing what he was already doing, rather than proposing a new plan. — via search extract (attribution uncertain; [Horn Diplomat (11)](https://www.horndiplomat.com/?p=16625))
  - The search found no source in which Morgan publicly denied or confirmed authorship, and no confirmation that Africa Watch reprinted it.
- Morgan reportedly died in Nairobi in May 2025. — [ICB News](https://icbnews.co.ke/2025/05/29/general-morgan-somalias-controversial-military-figure-dies-in-nairobi/)
- Isaaq communities faced escalating repression from 1987 (secondary). — [Saxafi substack, Laleh Chini](https://saxafi.substack.com/p/the-isaaq-genocide-laleh-chini-examines)

### Inferences
- For a neutral page: describe the letter as "a document attributed to Gen. Morgan, dated January 1987". Say that its authenticity was not independently verified in sources found, while noting that Somaliland sources and its translator consider it genuine.

### Gaps
- Not found: who preceded or followed Gani and Morgan as civilian regional governors, and other named officials such as NSS heads in the north or Mobile Military Court judges.
- Not checked: what the Africa Watch report itself (January 1990) says about the Morgan letter. This must be verified in the full text.
- Not found: the government's official denial or response to the letter.

## 5. The April 1988 Barre–Mengistu accord and why it triggered the SNM offensive

### Takeaway
On 3 April 1988 Somalia and Ethiopia signed a peace accord. Each side pledged to stop supporting the other's armed opposition and to restore relations, and Ethiopia was to expel SNM forces. Losing its sanctuary, the SNM moved into Somalia and attacked Burao on 27 May and Hargeisa on 31 May 1988.

### Cited Findings
- The agreement was signed on 3 April 1988 (accounts link it to a Djibouti meeting). Each side pledged to cease supporting the other's armed opposition, and Ethiopia was to expel SNM forces from its territory. — [Wikipedia: 1988 Hargeisa-Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive); [Military Wiki: SNM](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Somali_National_Movement)
- An earlier Barre–Mengistu meeting in Djibouti on 19 March 1988 is reported to have achieved little. Another account says the accord was formally signed, and diplomatic ties restored, at a second meeting in April 1988. — [countrystudies.us, Somalia – Foreign Relations](https://countrystudies.us/somalia/78.htm). Note: Djibouti hosted IGADD summits around this time, but the March versus April venues are not fully resolved in the extracts.
- The IRB of Canada says the April 1988 peace treaty "ended Ethiopian support for the SNM". — [IRB via ecoi.net](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1222426.html); [Refworld IRB "The North" (1992)](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1992/en/93014)
- The SNM left its Ethiopian base camps in late May 1988 and launched a major offensive. Forces under Col. Ahmed Mire Mohamed attacked Burao on 27 May 1988, and Hargeisa was attacked on 31 May. — [Wikipedia: Battle of Burao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Burao); [Wikipedia: Battle of Hargeisa (1988)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hargeisa_(1988))
- USCIS: "In late May 1988, the SNM attacked Burau, Hargeisa, the port of Berbera, and several other towns in northwest Somalia." — [USCIS/INS "Things Fall Apart"](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/ins/somala93.pdf)

### Inferences
- The SNM's motive for attacking in Somalia is generally given as the loss of its Ethiopian bases after 3 April. The search found no SNM statement explaining the decision directly.

### Gaps
- The full text of the accord (other clauses, such as troop withdrawal from the border and prisoner exchange) was not found.

## 6. Population and geography: towns, Isaaq sub-clans, other northern clans and their positions

### Takeaway
The main Isaaq towns were Hargeisa (the north's largest city), Burao, Berbera (port), Sheikh and Erigavo (mixed). The Gadabuursi (west, Borama), Issa (far west), Dhulbahante (east, a MOD clan) and Warsangeli (north-east) generally sided with the Barre government against the SNM. Barre armed some of them as militias.

### Cited Findings
- Hoehne (Rift Valley Institute) says that in north-western Somalia the Gadabuursi, Dhulbahante and Warsangeli "had sided, as a rule, with the Barre government". — [Hoehne, *Between Somaliland and Puntland* (RVI 2015)](https://riftvalley.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Between-Somaliland-and-Puntland-by-Markus-Hoehne-RVI-Contested-Borderlands-2015.pdf)
- After Hargeisa was bombarded in 1988, "the fighting became a war between the Isaaq and the other clan-families and clans allied with Siyad Barre". When the SNM took the north-west in early 1991, the Gadabuursi, Ciisa (Issa), Dhulbahante and Warsangeli "had fought for Barre until his fall". — [MPI Working Paper 82 (Hoehne)](https://www.eth.mpg.de/pubs/wps/pdf/mpi-eth-working-paper-0082.pdf)
- The regime armed the Gadabuursi and encouraged reprisals against the Isaaq (Wikipedia; this concerns the late-1980s period). — [Wikipedia: Capture of Borama (1991)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Borama_(1991))
- A Cambridge chapter quotes an analysis describing Barre's method as arming clans and "implicating them in the repression" of a targeted clan segment. — [Cambridge, *Black Markets and Militants*, ch. 6](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/black-markets-and-militants/state-collapse-informal-networks-and-the-dilemma-of-state-building-in-somalia/190802472753A92D8970F284356B8871)
- Hargeisa was Somalia's major northern city and was destroyed by bombing in 1988. — [LoC/DTIC country study](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA264997.pdf)

### Inferences
- The perspectives of non-Isaaq northern clans in 1981–88 survive mainly as their alignment with the state. Present them neutrally: some had MOD kinship ties (Dhulbahante), some feared SNM/Isaaq dominance, and some were armed by the state.
