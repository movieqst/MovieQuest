## Q1. The SNM offensive: Burao, Hargeisa, Berbera, Erigavo; SNM forces and commanders; what it held and for how long

### Takeaway
The SNM attacked Burao on 27 May 1988 and took it within hours. It entered Hargeisa about 29 May (some sources say 31 May) and by about 1 June held most of the city except the airport. Government artillery, tanks and aircraft then retook both towns over the following weeks. Berbera stayed in government hands throughout. Erigavo saw short SNM incursions, including a three-hour occupation on 16 March 1989. The SNM chairman was Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud "Silanyo". Field commanders are named mostly by Wikipedia/Wikidata and remain thinly sourced.

### Cited Findings
**Background / trigger**
- On 3 April 1988 Somalia and Ethiopia signed an accord that ended cross-border support for each other's rebels. That pushed the SNM, which had been based in Ethiopia, to attack the northern towns. [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive). An ECOI/Home Office compilation also describes the SNM, "fearing the collapse of its long insurgency" after the accord, attacking Hargeisa and Burao — [ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195690)

**Burao**
- **27 May 1988:** the SNM attacked Burao early in the morning and captured it "within two hours". [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive); [Battle of Burao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Burao)
- Wikipedia names **Col. Ahmed Mire Mohamed** as leader of the SNM assault on Burao. [WP], single source — [Battle of Burao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Burao)
- The GAO (May 1989) says the SNM "had control of the northern town of Burao until Somali forces retook it". The excerpt gives no date for the recapture — [GAO NSIAD-89-159](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf)
- Gersony documents killings of non-Isaaq men in Burao "within days of the May 27, 1988 arrival of the SNM". See Q5 — [Gersony 1989 (CJA copy)](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf), as quoted in search excerpt

**Hargeisa**
- The SNM entered Hargeisa on **29 May 1988** and by **1 June** had overrun most of the city except the airport. [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive); [Battle of Hargeisa (1988)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hargeisa_(1988)). **Date dispute:** other sources give **31 May** for the attack on Hargeisa (search summary of the same Wikipedia cluster).
- Under heavy fire from government artillery and tanks, the SNM force sent to take Hargeisa airport fell back to **Adadley (Cadaadley)**. During the army's counterattack the SNM's defensive line ran behind the Hargeisa radio station. [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive)
- The SNM made a surprise attack on the army garrison at Cadaadley (Adadle) with three brigades, led by commanders including **Colonel Xuseen Dheere**. [WP] — [Battle of Adadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adadle)
- **Duration of SNM hold:** not firmly dated in the sources retrieved. Refugee flight from Hargeisa began in late June (Perlez, NYT, below), and foreign aid workers left Hargeisa in July. The army was therefore back in control of the city's centre and airport area by mid-summer 1988, but no exact recapture date was found.

**Berbera**
- Berbera remained under government control and was the port through which troops and supplies arrived. [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive)
- Mass arrests took place in Berbera after the SNM attacked Burao (search summary of Wikipedia/HRW material). For killings in Berbera, see Q3.

**Erigavo / Sanaag**
- **16 March 1989:** *Africa Confidential*, cited by Canada's IRB, reports a case "after a three-hour occupation of the town of Erigavo by SNM forces" — [IRB via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1222426.html)
- The State Department's 1989 human-rights report says "SNM members of the Isaak clan, in fighting around the northern town of Erigavo in March [1989], killed a number of unresisting civilians" — [USDOS Country Report 1989 via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)

**SNM leadership**
- **Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud "Silanyo"** was SNM chairman from 1984 to 1990 (one source gives 9 August 1984 – April 1990), its longest-serving chairman. He was later President of Somaliland (2010–2017). — [Wikipedia: Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Mohamed_Mohamoud); [Dawan Africa](https://dawan.africa/news/former-somaliland-president-ahmed-mohamed-mohamud-silanyo-passes-away-at-86)
- Wikidata lists **Ibrahim Degaweyne (Dhegaweyne)** and **Mohamed Kahin Ahmed** as SNM commanders in the 1988 bombardment item. [Wikidata], low confidence for specific roles — [Wikidata Q140430018](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q140430018); [Wikidata Q110654542](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q110654542)
- **Abdirahman Ahmed Ali "Tuur"** left Barre's government to become SNM chairman (he succeeded Silanyo in 1990) and later became Somaliland's first president — [IRB/ECOI "Update on the Situation in the North"](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2739)

### Inferences
- The SNM's quick seizure of Burao and most of Hargeisa was not matched by a capture of the airports or garrisons. That left the army able to use Hargeisa airport as a base to bomb the city (see Q2).
- After mid-1988 the war became a rural insurgency with raids on towns, such as Erigavo in March 1989, rather than a struggle to hold the towns.

### Gaps
- No primary source retrieved for exact recapture dates of Burao or Hargeisa, or for a day-by-day log of June 1988.
- SNM field commanders (e.g. Col. Ahmed Mire Mohamed, Col. Xuseen Dheere, Ibrahim Dhegaweyne, Mohamed Kahin Ahmed, Hassan Yonis Habane) are sourced only to Wikipedia/Wikidata. Their exact 1988 roles need Ingiriis, Bradbury or Compagnon to confirm.
- SNM troop strength in May 1988: no figure found.
- Silanyo's exact date of death was not given in the excerpts. Reports say he "died at 86". Tuur's later fate (widely said to be 2003, London) was not confirmed in this session.

## Q2. The government response: bombing, shelling, units, commanders, Ogaden refugees and paramilitaries

### Takeaway
The army answered with heavy artillery, tanks and aircraft against Hargeisa and Burao, and the aircraft flew from Hargeisa's own airport. A US official audit (GAO, May 1989) found most of Hargeisa damaged or destroyed by artillery and aerial shelling. One Somali Air Force pilot, Lt. Col. Ahmed Mohamed Hassan "Ahmed Dheere", refused to bomb Hargeisa on 12 or 13 July 1988 and flew his MiG-17 to Djibouti. The northern command (26th Sector) was under Gen. Mohamed Said Hersi "Morgan", Siad Barre's son-in-law, from 1986 to 1988. The government also armed Ogaden refugees as militia. **Correction to the brief:** in 1988 Mohamed Ali Samatar was Prime Minister and First Vice-President, **not** Defence Minister. The Defence Minister was Gen. Aden Abdullahi Nur "Gabyow" until 1 February 1989.

### Cited Findings
**Bombardment and shelling**
- The GAO report (NSIAD-89-159, May 1989) says most of Hargeisa was destroyed or damaged by artillery and aerial shelling. It was written at congressional request and covers the government–SNM conflict — [GAO PDF](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf); [GAO product page](https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad-89-159)
- The GAO says the army responded to the SNM attacks of May 1988 "with extreme force" (as quoted in an ECOI compilation) — [ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195690)
- Human Rights Watch (2009, recapping) says the government bombed Hargeisa from its own airport after the SNM took parts of the city in 1988, and strafed fleeing civilians — [HRW, "Hostages to Peace" (2009)](https://www.hrw.org/report/2009/07/13/hostages-peace/threats-human-rights-and-democracy-somaliland)
- Amnesty International (1999 retrospective) says the SNM attacks drew a harsh response that included air bombardment, which destroyed much of Hargeisa — [Amnesty AFR 52/001/1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)

**The pilot who refused**
- **Lt. Col. Ahmed Mohamed Hassan ("Ahmed Dheere")**, Somali Air Force. Sources date the order to 12 or 13 July 1988. Ordered to bomb Hargeisa, he switched off his radio and flew a **MiG-17** to Djibouti, landing on a beach. Djibouti refused Somalia's request to send him back and gave him asylum. In October 2020 Somaliland honoured him, and his biography was launched in Hargeisa — [Hiiraan 2008](https://www.hiiraan.com/news2/2008/jun/former_somali_air_force_pilot_who_refused_to_bomb_civilians_in_hargiesa.aspx); [Hiiraan 2020](https://www.hiiraan.com/news4/2020/Oct/180388/former_somali_air_force_pilot_honoured_for_refusing_to_bomb_hargeisain_1988.aspx); [Somaliland Standard](https://somalilandstandard.com/my-conscious-wouldnt-allow-me-to-bomb-my-people-says-celebrated-ex-fighter-pilot-ahmed-deere-at-his-biographical-launching-in-hargeisa/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Mohamed_Hassan_(pilot))
- One outlet says he saw white Zimbabwean mercenaries piloting jets that bombed Hargeisa. **This is unverified**: it comes from a single, less established source and was not confirmed by any mainstream or human-rights source found here — [sii1991.org](https://sii1991.org/conscience-over-command-the-incredible-story-of-an-air-force-pilot-ordered-to-bomb-civilians-in-somaliland/)
- An Institute for Peace testimony paper is titled "How Captain Ahmed Defied a Dictator's Orders to Bombard Hargeisa". Note the rank discrepancy: Captain here versus Lt. Col. in other sources — [IPCS paper](https://instituteforpeace.org/publications/ipcs-papers/A-Testimony-How-Captain-Ahmed-Defied-a-Dictators-Orders-to-Bombard-Hargeisa.pdf)
- No other aircraft types were confirmed by reliable sources (Hawker Hunter claims were not verified). A specialist site summarises the Somali Air Force's combat record — [migflug.com](https://migflug.com/air-forces/somali-air-force/combat-record/), not reviewed in detail.

**Commanders and officials (government)**
- **Gen. Mohamed Said Hersi "Morgan"** was military commander of the 26th Sector (the north-west) from 1986 to 1988 and oversaw the bombardment of the northern cities. Wikipedia credits him with ordering the bombing of Hargeisa, citing HRW; this attribution was not verified against the HRW text. A policy letter known as the "Morgan Report" is tied to January 1986/87; the date and contents were not verified. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Said_Hersi_Morgan) [WP]
- **Brig. Gen. Mohamed Hashi "Gani"** was commander of the 26th Division at Hargeisa (placed in charge in 1980 per Wikipedia) and also governor. He was related to Barre's second wife. Isaaq clan members accused him of overseeing torture and killings during his command. Wikipedia lists him among commanders in the 1988 offensive, but his 1988 role is not well documented in what was retrieved. [WP] — [Wikipedia: Mohammed Hashi Gani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Hashi_Gani); [26th Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/26th_Division_(Somalia))
- **Gen. Mohamed Ali Samatar (Samantar)**: Minister of Defence and First Vice-President January 1980 – December 1986, then Prime Minister January 1987 – September 1990, per CJA. Wikipedia gives conflicting dates for his time as Defence Minister. — [CJA](https://cja.org/?p=953); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Ali_Samatar). A US court found in 2012 that he "not only knew about" the abuses "but he in fact ordered and affirmatively permitted such violations". See Q6 — [In re Samantar (CourtListener)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8526199/yousuf-v-samantar-in-re-samantar/)
- **Gen. Aden Abdullahi Nur "Gabyow"** was Defence Minister. Sources disagree on his start date: 20 June 1986 or the reshuffle of 22 December 1987. Mogadishu radio reported that on **1 February 1989** he was moved to Information and Tourism. He was arrested, in July 1989 per *Africa Confidential*, or in 1988 per another source. Ogadeni soldiers in Kismayo mutinied over his demotion in March 1989, which led to the founding of the SPM — [ECOI/Home Office](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195689); [ECOI SOM4065](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195510); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aden_Abdullahi_Nur)
- **Col. Yusuf Abdi Ali "Tukeh"** commanded the army's **Fifth Brigade** in the north-west. A US jury found him liable for torture in 2019. The underlying events are dated December 1987 in case papers, or 1988 per CNN, so they may fall just before this timeline. — [CJA](https://cja.org/former-somali-army-commander-living-in-virginia-found-liable-for-torture-during-countrys-civil-war/); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2019/05/21/us/accused-war-criminal-uber-ordered-to-pay-invs)
- **President Mohamed Siad Barre** was head of state and commander-in-chief throughout — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mohamed-Siad-Barre)

**Armed Ogaden refugees / paramilitaries**
- The government formed a militia made up almost entirely of Ogaden refugees. Its official purpose was to fight Ethiopia alongside the Western Somali Liberation Front (WSLF); in effect it "conducted clan vendettas against the local populace" — [IRB Canada 1992, "The North"](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1992/en/93014)
- The GAO reports that in July 1988 UNHCR and others saw armed refugees in six of the 14 refugee camps in Somalia. The government told UNHCR the refugees were armed "for their own protection" — [GAO NSIAD-89-159](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf)
- Africa Watch, as quoted on Wikipedia, says the WSLF was supposedly training to retake the Ogaden but in practice terrorised Isaaq civilians in the border region. The quote was not verified against the original. [WP]

### Inferences
- Because the airport stayed in government hands, aircraft could take off within sight of the targets. That fits HRW's statement that Hargeisa was bombed "from its own airport".
- The brief's "Defence Minister Mohamed Ali Samatar" should be corrected. In 1988 Samatar was Prime Minister and First VP, and Gabyow held Defence. The court finding against Samatar rests on his senior command role and on what he conceded in the case.

### Gaps
- Names of other pilots, the squadrons involved and the number of sorties: not found. Mercenary-pilot claims (Zimbabwean/Rhodesian) are unverified.
- The army units used in the counterattack (beyond the 26th Sector/Division and the Fifth Brigade) and the officers commanding in Burao and Berbera in June 1988: not found.
- Morgan's exact tenure. Wikipedia says 1986–88, but whether he was still in command in mid/late 1988 and who succeeded him was not established. Whether Gani was in a northern command in 1988 or had been moved earlier is unclear.
- The contents of the "Morgan Report" letter: not verified.

## Q3. Civilians: killings, executions, destruction of Hargeisa, landmines, flight, refugee camps and numbers

### Takeaway
Sources agree on mass killings of Isaaq civilians from May 1988. Estimates differ widely and must be attributed:
- **Gersony (US State Dept, Aug 1989):** at least 5,000 unarmed civilians "purposely murdered" by the army, May 1988 – March 1989. He called this conservative.
- **Africa Watch (Jan 1990):** 50,000–60,000 killed by the start of 1990.
- **Amnesty:** about 10,000 within two months.
- **Later writers:** higher figures.

Hargeisa damage estimates run from 70% (US Embassy, quoted by GAO) to 90% (Wikipedia/others). Refugees began crossing into Ethiopia in late June 1988, and the count passed 300,000 by mid-August 1988 (NYT). Hartisheik became one of the world's largest camps.

### Cited Findings
**Killings and executions**
- Gersony estimated, "conservatively", that the Somali Armed Forces "purposely murdered" at least 5,000 unarmed civilians, mostly Isaaq, between May 1988 and March 1989 — [Washington Post, 10 Sep 1989](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1989/09/10/5000-somalis-reported-killed-by-army/a64da75c-afe7-4a35-94a9-e3e298ef0708/); [USDOS 1989 report via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)
- Gersony documents killings of Isaaq men in northern Somalia from May to August 1988, mainly by the armed forces — [IRB 1992](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1992/en/93014), summarising Gersony
- The State Department's 1989 human-rights report describes extrajudicial killing as a method of warfare in 1989 (search summary) — [USDOS via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)
- **Africa Watch (January 1990)** estimated that 50,000–60,000 had been killed by the start of 1990, through summary executions, shelling and aerial bombardment. Its research rested on interviews with newly arrived refugees in Djibouti (August 1989) and in England and Wales (June–October 1989) — [HRW World Report 1990 via Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/hrw/1991/en/23790); [Stanford catalogue entry](https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/1953105)
- HRW World Report 1990 describes the army killing unarmed civilians in Burao and other towns and destroying livestock, water reservoirs and homes — [Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/hrw/1991/en/23790)
- Amnesty (1999 retrospective) says about 10,000 people were killed within two months of the SNM attacks. Other human-rights groups put the civilian toll for the period at 50,000–60,000 — [Amnesty AFR 52/001/1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)
- Amnesty (1999) says the grave at Berbera held prisoners executed by government forces in **May 1988**. It also describes about two dozen medical workers killed outside Berbera hospital. This comes from a 1999 retrospective, not a 1988 report — [Amnesty 1999](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr520011999en.pdf)
- Amnesty published two reports in **September 1988**: *Somalia: a long-term human rights crisis* and a document on the imprisonment of Isaaq since mid-1988. They described widespread arbitrary arrests, ill-treatment and summary executions of suspected SNM collaborators. This comes from secondary citation; the text was not retrieved — [Saxafi / secondary](https://saxafi.substack.com/p/the-isaaq-genocide-laleh-chini-examines)
- A secondary source, unverified, says about 500 men were killed in groups of 30–40 in June 1988 at "Buraosheikh" near Berbera. The place name is probably garbled — check against Africa Watch 1990.
- Wikipedia says government troops executed 25 civilians in reprisal; the place was not specified. [WP] — [1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive)
- **Jane Perlez, New York Times, 13 August 1988** (datelined Addis Ababa): foreign aid workers who left Hargeisa in July reported mass executions of Isaaq civilians — [blog reproduction of NYT article](https://wargeyskasaxafi.wordpress.com/1988/08/13/over-300000-somalis-fleeing-civil-war-cross-into-ethiopia/). It was not checked against nytimes.com.
- Government warplanes strafed refugees as they fled — [HRW 2009](https://www.hrw.org/report/2009/07/13/hostages-peace/threats-human-rights-and-democracy-somaliland)

**Execution sites / mass graves (Malko Durduro etc.)**
- A Center for Justice and Accountability forensic report (preliminary assessment of mass graves near Hargeisa) describes sites south and south-west of Hargeisa, including the **Malko Durduro Elementary School** site. It attributes the remains to mass killings and burials by government troops in 1988 — [CJA forensic report](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/Forensic%20report-%20preliminary%20assessment%20of%20mass%20graves%20in%20the%20vicinity%20of%20Hargeisa,%20Somalia.pdf)
- Malko Durduro, near the former 26th Division headquarters, is called the "Valley of Death". More than 200 mass graves have been reported in Somaliland, most of them there — [Lacuna Magazine](https://lacuna.org.uk/war-and-peace/somalilands-hope-for-transitional-justice-after-the-isaaq-genocide/); [The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/in-the-valley-of-death-somalilands-forgotten-genocide/)
- A 2020 report describes a new grave in the Malko Durduro area, with remains reburied after forensic examination — [Araweelo News](https://www.araweelonews.com/breaking-news/somaliland-uncovers-new-mass-grave-32-years-after-military-regime-massacres/)
- A University of Tennessee thesis on forensic human-rights anthropology in Somaliland is relevant; it was not reviewed — [UT Trace](https://trace.tennessee.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5ecb8e6f-4222-41ba-b654-786b5a512c65/content)

**Destruction of Hargeisa (and Burao), by source**
- **GAO (May 1989):** most of Hargeisa destroyed or damaged. Secondary sources say the GAO quoted the **US Embassy estimate of 70%** damaged or destroyed. The GAO gives no death toll — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf); [Somaliland Chronicle summary](https://somalilandchronicle.com/2024/05/07/the-gao-report-on-1988-atrocity-in-somaliland-a-crucial-knowledge-for-the-young-to-know-reminder-to-the-elders/)
- **Africa Watch, September 1990 newsletter ("Evading Reality"):** cites the claim that 70% of Hargeisa had been destroyed — [HRW PDF](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/s/somalia/somalia909.pdf)
- **Wikipedia infobox:** 90% of Hargeisa and 70% of Burao destroyed. [WP] — [Bombardment of Burao and Hargeisa 1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardment_of_Burao_and_Hargeisa_1988)
- **Al Jazeera (2014):** more than 40,000 killed and the city reduced to rubble — [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2014/2/6/investigating-genocide-in-somaliland)
- About 5,000 civilians reportedly died in the heavily shelled northern part of Hargeisa. [WP], the original source is unclear — [Bombardment… (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardment_of_Burao_and_Hargeisa_1988)

**Flight and refugees**
- **NYT, 13 August 1988 (Perlez):** "Over 300,000 Somalis, Fleeing Civil War, Cross Into Ethiopia". The flow began in late June and was among the most sudden relief officials had seen — [reproduction](https://wargeyskasaxafi.wordpress.com/1988/08/13/over-300000-somalis-fleeing-civil-war-cross-into-ethiopia/)
- **CDC (MMWR, 1990/91):** refugees from northern Somalia began entering eastern Ethiopia in **June 1988**. They were housed in six camps: **Hartisheik A and B, Daror, Rabasso, Camaboker, Aysha**. By June 1990 about **360,000** had registered for relief — [CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00001795.htm)
- **GAO:** relief continued at the original planning level of **370,000** refugees — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf), via secondary summary
- **UNHCR:** Hartisheik was set up in 1988. At its peak it held "more than 250,000" refugees, mostly from Gabiley and Hargeisa. A separate UNHCR paper says that at one point in 1988 Hartasheikh was "the largest camp in the world, with over 400,000 refugees". A 1991 census counted 220,000. About 600,000 came to Ethiopia overall. The camp closed in 2004. Harshin was where refugees first went before settling at Hartisheik — [UNHCR 2004](https://unhcr.org/news/latest/2004/7/40e426de4/worlds-largest-refugee-camp-closes-ethiopia.html); [UNHCR population movements paper](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/legacy-pdf/3d5d0f3a4.pdf); [UNHCR "A camp is born"](https://www.unhcr.org/publications/refugees-magazine-issue-105-life-refugee-camp-camp-born)

**Landmines**
- Not documented in the sources retrieved (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- Death-toll figures fall into three groups, and they should not be blended. **5,000** is Gersony's floor for deliberate army murders only, May 1988 – March 1989. **50,000–60,000** is Africa Watch's total by early 1990, covering all causes including shelling and bombing. **10,000 in two months** is Amnesty's. Higher later figures of 100,000 to 200,000 come mainly from advocacy and Somaliland sources.
- The refugee figures fit together over time: more than 300,000 by August 1988 (NYT), planning at about 370,000 (GAO), 360,000 registered by June 1990 (CDC). Hartisheik peak figures of 250,000 versus 400,000 or more depend on the source and moment.

### Gaps
- The full Africa Watch 1990 text (named execution sites, Berbera airport, Burao, Jaameeco Weyn and so on) could not be opened, because the Halle/hrw.org PDFs were blocked. Specific execution sites other than Malko Durduro, and the Berbera killings, rest on secondary sources.
- Berbera "airport" executions: not confirmed by any retrieved source.
- Physicians for Human Rights 1989/1992 ("Hidden Massacres"): not retrieved. Its landmine findings are a known area of PHR work but were not verified here.
- Jane Perlez articles other than 13 Aug 1988: not retrieved.

## Q4. 1989: continuing operations, named events; the Gersony report; Africa Watch 1990

### Takeaway
In 1989 the war continued as an insurgency, with SNM raids on towns (Erigavo, 16 March 1989) and army reprisals. On 1 February 1989 the Defence Minister was reshuffled out, and he was later arrested. The Gersony report (August 1989, released at Congress's request; publicised by the Washington Post on 10 September 1989) put army murders at 5,000 or more and SNM killings at "at least several hundred or more", plus attacks on refugee camps. Africa Watch (January 1990) put deaths at 50,000–60,000.

### Cited Findings
- **Gersony, "Why Somalis Flee" (US State Dept, Bureau for Refugee Programs, August 1989):** based on **252 interviews** at 31 locations in Ethiopia, Somalia and Kenya, per the Washington Post and HRW World Report 1989. The State Department's own human-rights report says about 300; this discrepancy is unresolved. The State Department released it at the request of Congress — [Washington Post 10 Sep 1989](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1989/09/10/5000-somalis-reported-killed-by-army/a64da75c-afe7-4a35-94a9-e3e298ef0708/); [HRW World Report 1989, Somalia chapter](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/worldreports/world.89/somalia.pdf); [USDOS via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)
  - Army: "conservatively" at least 5,000 unarmed civilians "purposely murdered", May 1988 – March 1989.
  - SNM: "killed unarmed civilians in individual instances which together may have resulted in the deaths of at least several hundred or more persons" — quoted by [IRB 1992](https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/irbc/1992/en/93014)
  - SNM attacks on UN-administered camps for Ethiopian (Ogadeni) refugees killed **more than 400**, per the Washington Post summary. It found camp residents had not been armed before the attacks. See Q5.
- **Critique:** a Heritage Foundation paper (December 1989) argued that the report "must be viewed skeptically" because it relies on refugee interviews — [Heritage Backgrounder 745 via PolicyArchive](https://www.policyarchive.org/download/11307)
- **HRW World Report 1989:** includes a dispute in which the State Department rebuked Africa Watch over its claims of possible chemical-weapons use — [HRW 1989 Somalia chapter](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/worldreports/world.89/somalia.pdf). These were allegations only, and no finding was found.
- **1 February 1989:** Defence Minister Aden Abdullahi Nur "Gabyow" moved to Information and Tourism (Mogadishu radio). **March 1989:** Ogadeni soldiers mutinied in Kismayo, leading to the founding of the SPM. **July 1989:** Gabyow arrested, per *Africa Confidential* — [ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195689); [ECOI SOM4065](https://www.ecoi.net/de/dokument/195510)
- **16 March 1989:** the SNM occupied Erigavo for three hours; civilians were killed around Erigavo in March — [IRB via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1222426.html); [USDOS via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)
- **Africa Watch, *Somalia: A Government at War with Its Own People* (January 1990):** 50,000–60,000 killed by the beginning of 1990. The report recounts abuses before the war and the conduct of both government forces and the SNM — [HRW World Report 1990 via Refworld](https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/hrw/1991/en/23790)
- **GAO NSIAD-89-159 (May 1989):** see Q2 and Q3.

### Inferences
- The "May 1988 – March 1989" window in Gersony matches the period of his fieldwork. Killings after March 1989 are covered mainly by Africa Watch and the State Department's 1989 report.

### Gaps
- Specific named army operations or massacres in the rest of 1989 (for example around Gebiley, Sheikh, El Afweyn or Borama) were not found in the retrieved excerpts.
- Africa Watch's 1990 chapter-level findings on the SNM: not retrieved.

## Q5. SNM abuses against non-Isaaq civilians (same sources)

### Takeaway
Gersony, the State Department and the GAO all report SNM killings of non-Isaaq civilians. These include men killed in Burao after 27 May 1988, raids on refugee camps for Ethiopian Ogadeni refugees (more than 400 killed per Gersony, as summarised by the Washington Post; 43 killed in May 1988 at Agabar and Las Dhure per a Heritage paper) and killings around Erigavo in March 1989. Gersony's overall figure for SNM killings is "at least several hundred or more".

### Cited Findings
- Within days of the SNM's arrival in Burao on 27 May 1988, non-Isaaq men were killed. In one documented case, all five victims were non-Isaaq and were shot — [Gersony 1989](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf) (search excerpt)
- The SNM "conducted systematic violent attacks against [refugee] camps", some of them defenceless, whose residents had played no role in the conflict — Gersony as quoted in [Heritage 1989](https://www.policyarchive.org/download/11307)
- The SNM attacked the refugee camps at **Agabar and Las Dhure**, killing about **43** people in **May 1988** — [Heritage Backgrounder 745 (Dec 1989)](https://www.policyarchive.org/download/11307). Heritage is a policy think tank and may not be citing Gersony for this figure.
- The GAO says the SNM attacked several Ogadeni camps in 1988, which forced the inhabitants of one camp to flee further north — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf)
- Gersony found the SNM attacked UN-administered camps, killing more than 400. One secondary source says nine of the 12 UN-administered camps in the region were attacked — [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1989/09/10/5000-somalis-reported-killed-by-army/a64da75c-afe7-4a35-94a9-e3e298ef0708/) (search summary)
- The Washington Post reported that SNM fighters killed people simply for not being Isaaq (search summary) — [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1989/09/10/5000-somalis-reported-killed-by-army/a64da75c-afe7-4a35-94a9-e3e298ef0708/)
- **Erigavo, March 1989:** "SNM members of the Isaak clan… killed a number of unresisting civilians" — [USDOS 1989 via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)
- Wikipedia says SNM militias killed Dhulbahante civilians in Dararweyne, Hudun and Sanirgood. **No date is given, and it may fall outside 1988–89.** [WP] — [Dararweyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dararweyne)

### Inferences
- The same sources the brief relies on for army killings also document SNM killings, at a much lower scale ("several hundred or more", plus more than 400 in camps). A neutral page can present both, scaled as those sources scale them.

### Gaps
- Camp names in Gersony beyond Agabar and Las Dhure, and his camp-by-camp figures: not retrieved.
- Africa Watch 1990's own account of SNM abuses: not retrieved.
- Gadabursi or Dhulbahante victims in 1988–89 specifically: no dated source found.
