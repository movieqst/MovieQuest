# An Army Turned on Northern Somalia, 1988

**Page:** "Somalia 1988: War in the North and the Isaaq Genocide" (educational, neutral, source-tagged). **Report date:** 13 November 2026.

In 1988 the government of President Mohamed Siad Barre answered an offensive by the Somali National Movement (SNM), a rebel movement drawn mainly from the Isaaq clan-family, with bombing, artillery fire and mass executions. The violence fell overwhelmingly on Isaaq civilians in what is today Somaliland. The SNM attacked Burao on **27 May 1988** and Hargeisa on **31 May 1988** ([Gersony 1989, p. 12](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf); [Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 13](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The army shelled and bombed both cities. Aircraft taking off from Hargeisa's own airport struck the city and the columns of people fleeing it. In Berbera, a town the SNM never attacked, the army killed Isaaq men in batches by cutting their throats. The US State Department's investigator, Robert Gersony, gave a deliberately conservative floor: **at least 5,000** unarmed Isaaq civilians "purposefully murdered" by the army between May 1988 and March 1989 ([Gersony, p. 61](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Africa Watch estimated **50,000–60,000** people killed by government forces, or dead from shelling, bombing and war-related wounds and disease, over the nineteen months to January 1990 ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 13, 20, 227](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Roughly half a million people fled abroad, most of them to camps in Ethiopia. The same contemporary sources also record SNM killings of non-Isaaq civilians and SNM attacks on camps of Ethiopian Ogadeni refugees, on a much smaller scale (Gersony: "at least several hundred or more", plus at least 400 refugees). Those attacks belong in the story. Whether the campaign was **genocide** is a characterisation, not a ruling. A 2002 preliminary study commissioned by the UN, many scholars, advocacy institutes and the Somaliland government use the word. The UN's own human-rights experts of 1997–2005 spoke of war crimes and crimes against humanity. **No court has ever ruled on genocide.** The two US civil cases ended in a conceded default ($21 million against former Prime Minister Mohamed Ali Samantar, 2012) and a jury verdict for torture against a former colonel (2019). The war ended when the SNM took the northern garrisons in January 1991, and on **18 May 1991** it declared the Republic of Somaliland.

## How to read this report: evidence labels and source base

Each claim carries one of these labels, and claims are not merged across labels. **[COURT]** is a judicial ruling. **[CONCEDED]** is liability accepted by a defendant without trial. **[JURY]** is a civil jury verdict. **[OFFICIAL]** covers reports by UN organs or UN-mandated experts and US government investigations (State Department, GAO). **[CONSULTANT]** is a UN-commissioned study expressing its author's view. **[HR REPORT]** covers human-rights organisations (Africa Watch / Human Rights Watch, Amnesty). **[TESTIMONY]** is the account of a named or anonymous witness, as reported by the source cited. **[SCHOLARSHIP]** is academic writing. **[GOV STATEMENT]** is a statement by a government or official. **[ADVOCACY]** covers campaign organisations and partisan outlets. **[PRESS]** is journalism. **[TERTIARY]** is Wikipedia or Wikidata. **[ALLEGATION]** is pleadings or uncorroborated claims.

The strongest sources, all read in full for this report, are three. The first is Robert Gersony's *Why Somalis Flee* (US State Department, Bureau for Refugee Programs, August 1989). It rests on 302 interviews; 252 of them relate to the north and form the basis of its findings. Fieldwork took place March–May 1989 at 31 locations ([Gersony, pp. 2, 4](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). The second is Africa Watch's *Somalia: A Government at War with Its Own People* (January 1990, 281 PDF pages). It is based on interviews with newly arrived refugees in Djibouti (August 1989) and in England and Wales (June–October 1989), and it was written without access to northern Somalia or the Ethiopian camps ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 9–10](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The third is the US court records and the UN Commission on Human Rights reports of 1997–2005. Page references to Africa Watch 1990 are to PDF pages of the HRW scan. Gersony references are to printed pages. Much of the rest of the source base (Wikipedia, Somaliland outlets, advocacy sites) was reached only through search extracts; it is labelled as such and treated as weaker.

## Two decades of grievance turned the north into a war zone before May 1988

Siad Barre seized power in a coup on **21 October 1969** ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 17](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The regime proclaimed itself against clannism, but power came to rest on the "MOD" clans: Barre's own Marehan, his mother's Ogaden and the Dhulbahante of his son-in-law, the security chief [TERTIARY] ([Wikipedia: MOD Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOD_Alliance); [Hoehne, MPI Working Paper 82](https://www.eth.mpg.de/pubs/wps/pdf/mpi-eth-working-paper-0082.pdf)). After Somalia's defeat in the Ogaden war in March 1978, hundreds of thousands of Ogadeni refugees entered Somalia, many of them settled near the north-western towns. Africa Watch reports that the government formed paramilitary groups among them and conscripted them into the army. It also says government-encouraged militias, the Western Somali Liberation Front (WSLF) among them, "committed atrocities against the Isaak civilian population living along the Somali/Ethiopian border" [HR REPORT] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 18](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). On **30 March 1982** a memorandum from 21 Isaaq elders to Barre complained that the WSLF subjected rural people to "murder and robbery". It also complained that northerners were shut out of refugee administration and that the north was neglected in commerce, industry and public works [TESTIMONY, as reproduced by Africa Watch] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 42–47](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Isaaq officers who deserted formed the "Afraad" (fourth unit) in 1980. Africa Watch says that when **Mohamed Hashi Gani**, "a cousin of the President", became military commander of the north in 1980, he disarmed the unit and moved it to Adaadley near Berbera (PDF pp. 41–42).

The SNM was formed in London on **6 April 1981** by Isaaq emigrés [OFFICIAL] ([Library of Congress country study](https://countrystudies.us/somalia/30.htm)); Wikipedia places its formal founding in Jeddah [TERTIARY]. It moved its bases to Ethiopia in 1982 ([USCIS 1993](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/ins/somala93.pdf)). The event Africa Watch treats as the political trigger in the north was the arrest in **December 1981** of the "Hargeisa Group": about thirty doctors, engineers and teachers who had organised self-help work at Hargeisa's hospital. Their trial in **February 1982** set off demonstrations; witnesses date the serious disturbances to **20 February 1982** ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 47–48, 64, 108](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Somaliland outlets call the group "Uffo" and the protests "Dhagax Tuur", and their dates differ (19, 20, 22 February) [ADVOCACY/PRESS] ([Somaliland Sun](https://somalilandsun.com/somalilandpresident-hosts-heroic-ufo-survivors/)). From 1982 emergency regulations placed civilians under military tribunals. Three bodies ran this system: the Mobile Military Court, nicknamed "Guilty or Innocent, You Will be Found Guilty", whose executions took place at **Badhka**, open ground in south-west Hargeisa; a Regional Security Council chaired by the military commander; and the military intelligence force HANGASH [HR REPORT] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 7, 53–56](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). In February 1982 the government also reintroduced the letter-of-credit system and confiscated goods at Berbera port, which Africa Watch values at about 300 million shillings (PDF p. 45). The SNM's raid on **Mandera prison** near Berbera on **2 January 1983** freed prisoners. The number freed is disputed, from "hundreds" to about 700 [TERTIARY] ([Wikipedia: Mandera Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandera_Prison)).

Africa Watch dates the start of systematic reprisal killings to 1984, when the SNM stepped up its incursions. It documents six men shot at Gogol Wanaag near Arabsiyo on **15 October 1984**. It documents 26 Isaaq (nine civilians, seventeen SNM fighters) sentenced by the Mobile Military Court and executed in Hargeisa on **17 November 1984**, with the town's elders forced to watch. It also documents **43 men executed in Burao in December 1984** after their criminal charges were changed to SNM association ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 73–75](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). According to Africa Watch, Gani was military commander and, in effect, governor until **General Mohamed Saeed Hirsi "Morgan"**, "a son-in-law of the president", took over at the **end of 1986**. Morgan introduced photo identity cards to control movement (PDF pp. 54, 81). Africa Watch writes that "a confidential report from General Morgan to President Barre leaked to the international press in **February 1987**". It says the report detailed policies Morgan had been implementing to "liquidate" what he called the "Isaak problem". Africa Watch lists these measures: confiscating Isaaq property; suspending business licences and freezing bank accounts; purging Isaaq from sensitive posts; enrolling refugee children in local schools to ensure a "balance"; relocating villages; destroying water reservoirs; and resettling Ethiopian refugees on Isaaq land [HR REPORT] (PDF p. 54). The document itself is dated 23 January 1987 in Somaliland reproductions. Its English translation was sworn in London on 27 April 1987 by a translator who stated that the signature was Morgan's [ADVOCACY] ([longlivesomaliland.com](https://www.longlivesomaliland.com/letterofdeath_by_general_morgan.htm)). The "Letter of Death" and "final solution" wording comes from those reproductions; it does not appear in Africa Watch. Africa Watch did not question the report's authenticity. No independent forensic authentication was found, and no statement by Morgan addressing the document specifically.

In **March 1988** Barre visited the north. At a rally in Hargeisa stadium, students threw stones and the presidential guard fired into the crowd, killing five students. During the same visit 24 or 25 Isaaq men were executed at Gebiley and Tug Wajale on **14 March 1988** in reprisal for an SNM attack [HR REPORT/TESTIMONY] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 76, 136](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Africa Watch says mass executions resumed at the end of 1987, when the notorious military-court prosecutor Col. Yusuf Muse "Yusuf Dherre" was reinstated (PDF pp. 55–56, 76). On **3 April 1988** Somalia and Ethiopia signed a peace accord, each pledging not to aid the other's rebels (PDF p. 137). With its Ethiopian sanctuary closing, the SNM attacked.

## The 1988 war moved from a sudden offensive to a rural insurgency

The SNM attacked Burao on **Friday 27 May 1988** and, Africa Watch says, "captured the town in about two hours". It seized the airport military compound, the Goon Ad compound, the police stations and the prison, and executed a number of officers and officials ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 137](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The same report elsewhere says the SNM attacked at 7 a.m. and "at 5:00 p.m. they had retreated to their base", after which soldiers re-entered the town (PDF p. 203). How long the SNM held central Burao therefore differs even within this source. Gersony mentions detainees held "until a short time after the SNM withdrew from its attack on Burao" ([Gersony, p. 41](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Soldiers then went on a rampage on 27–28 May. Witnesses say the army used loudspeakers to call non-Isaaq residents to leave and provided them with transport. Shelling began after that evacuation, and aerial bombardment followed on the Tuesday, 31 May [TESTIMONY] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 138–140](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The US General Accounting Office (May 1989), quoted by Africa Watch, reported that the army "responded to the SNM attacks in May 1988 with extreme force" and resorted to "artillery and aerial shelling in heavily populated urban centers", with armoured tanks in Burao [OFFICIAL] (PDF pp. 138–139).

In Hargeisa, mass arrests began as soon as news from Burao arrived. **53 businessmen and elders were seized on 28 May.** Detainees were held at Birjeh (the 26th Sector headquarters), the **Malka Durduro** military compound, the central prison and NSS sites (PDF pp. 143–144). The SNM attacked Hargeisa at **2:15 a.m. on 31 May** (PDF p. 146). Gersony also gives 31 May; no document read gives the 29 May found on Wikipedia. Africa Watch reports that after a day or two the army counter-attacked with heavy weapons, firing long-range artillery from hilltops near the Zoo and behind Badhka. The SNM's defensive line ran behind the Radio Station (PDF pp. 146, 156). Testimony describes aircraft taking off from **Hargeisa airport** to bomb the city, and pilots flying low over the people fleeing it (PDF pp. 3, 181, 188). Gersony, as the State Department's investigator, recorded that the armed forces "used military aircraft to strafe and bomb residential areas in which SNM forces were dispersed". He also recorded that the SNM "dispersed its combatants throughout the densely populated, principally Issak residential areas", and estimated civilian crossfire deaths "may have ascended into the thousands" [OFFICIAL] ([Gersony, pp. 20, 42, 64](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). One witness quoted by Africa Watch named the aircraft as British-made Hunters, MiG-19s, MiG-17s and MiG-21s, with Italian light aircraft spotting targets [TESTIMONY] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 181](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). One Somali Air Force pilot, **Lt. Col. Ahmed Mohamed Hassan ("Ahmed Dheere")**, is reported to have refused an order to bomb Hargeisa on 12 or 13 July 1988 and flown his MiG-17 to Djibouti, where he received asylum [PRESS] ([Hiiraan 2008](https://www.hiiraan.com/news2/2008/jun/former_somali_air_force_pilot_who_refused_to_bomb_civilians_in_hargiesa.aspx)). No human-rights report read confirms this. A claim that Zimbabwean or Rhodesian mercenaries flew the bombers rests on one weak outlet and is unverified ([sii1991.org](https://sii1991.org/conscience-over-command-the-incredible-story-of-an-air-force-pilot-ordered-to-bomb-civilians-in-somaliland/)).

The government kept the airport and the Sha'ab district, and Berbera port stayed in its hands throughout ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 157](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf); [Wikipedia: 1988 Hargeisa–Burao offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive)). Several Africa Watch witnesses place the SNM's withdrawal from Hargeisa in **August 1988**. They say that after it the army kept shelling, looted and mined buildings, including the Union Hotel (PDF pp. 158, 178, 194). No source gives an exact date for the army's recovery of either city. Gersony found that 70% of the killings he recorded fell between 27 May and 31 August 1988 and that most refugees reached Ethiopia between June and September 1988 ([Gersony, pp. 6, 33, 61](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). After that the war became a rural insurgency. The army and armed refugees raided villages between Hargeisa and Berbera from June to September 1988. Villages in Sanaag, Sool and Togdheer, around El Afweyn and Kalsheikh, were attacked in July and November 1988. The SNM briefly entered **Erigavo** in March 1989; Canada's IRB, citing *Africa Confidential*, gives a three-hour occupation on 16 March, and an Africa Watch witness says "the end of March". The army reached for armed refugees and clan militias where it lacked troops ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 166, 171–172, 205](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf); [IRB via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1222426.html)). Africa Watch's September 1990 newsletter records the war still going in 1989–90: 47 Isaaq men shot at Jezira Beach near Mogadishu on **16 July 1989**, 12 youths executed at Hahi near Odweine on **25 December 1989**, and 17 Isaaq men shot in central Berbera on **16 August 1990** [HR REPORT] ([Africa Watch, *Evading Reality*, pp. 4, 19, 21](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/s/somalia/somalia909.pdf)).

## Civilians were targeted by clan, and each body of evidence shows a different slice

The contemporary US investigation and the human-rights reporting agree on the pattern, though they count differently. Gersony's first finding is the strongest official language of the time. The armed forces "appear to have engaged in a widespread, systematic and extremely violent assault on the unarmed civilian Issak population", in places where neither resistance nor danger to the troops was present. In the killings he recorded, "the appearance that victims were selected for these killings principally because of their ethnic identity is unmistakable" [OFFICIAL] ([Gersony, pp. 60–61](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). His 130 Isaaq interviewees witnessed **629 killings** of unarmed civilians by the army. The categories were attacks on villages and water points (254), summary executions (122), reprisals (111), looting (49), attacks on people fleeing (54), killings near battle (26), rape (8) and ill-treatment (5). Of these, 86% were shot and 8% killed by strafing or bombing. The figures exclude crossfire deaths, Berbera and 101 prison executions (p. 32). He also documented **233 people killed in 32 summary-execution incidents**, 43% of them named (pp. 23–24).

**Berbera** is where Gersony's and Africa Watch's evidence converge most strongly. Gersony found that from shortly after 27 May until at least September 1988, soldiers "systematically collected large numbers of Issak males on the sole basis of their clan association". They held them near the Berbera airport, took them out at night in groups of five to fifty, and killed them "with large knives chopped into the necks of the victims". Witnesses placed one burial site about one kilometre from the end of the runway. "At least five hundred and perhaps many more" were killed (pp. 25–26, 61). Africa Watch independently reports "at least 500 people... killed, mainly in June, by having their throats slit", at night, about 10 km from Berbera near the airport, "in batches of 30–40". Most of the victims were seasonal port labourers from Hargeisa and Burao, and a survivor, Abdi Waal, escaped the first group of 21 ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 164](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The two batch sizes (5–50 and 30–40) come from different witnesses and should be given separately. Africa Watch adds three more Berbera accounts. One is the separation of Isaaq deportees from the ship *Emviyara* on 21 June 1988 by the HANGASH commander "Calas" and his deputy "Dakhare" (PDF p. 163). Another is the burning alive of eleven men east of Batalale on the order of three senior officers, including the Marine Commander, Col. Muse "Bigil" (PDF p. 166). The third is a prison built for 120 that held 485 people at one point (PDF p. 161). An early secondary note about "about 500 men killed in groups of 30–40 at 'Buraosheikh'" is a garble of the Africa Watch Berbera passage; no place called Buraosheikh appears in any primary source.

In **Hargeisa**, Africa Watch witnesses describe men shot in lines into a trench inside the Malka Durduro military compound. A detained truck driver describes daily executions of at least thirty people at Qaybta, the regional commander's headquarters, between about 7 and 15 June 1988. On one day eighty Isaaq soldiers, from Gebiley, Darar Weyne and Bogol Jirreh, were shot in batches of ten along the dry river bed. A prison employee describes 70 Isaaq military detainees shot at Malka Durduro on the fourth day of the arrests [TESTIMONY] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 145, 156](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Witnesses describe house-to-house searches in which men were shot or had their throats cut. One father's seven boys were found with their throats slit (PDF pp. 149, 152). The Dumbuluq district, which the SNM never entered, suffered most (PDF p. 148). Africa Watch reports that the army armed non-Isaaq civilians and released non-Isaaq prisoners, including men sentenced for murder, to guard Isaaq detainees or fight (PDF pp. 143–145). Smaller towns followed the same pattern without any SNM attack. In **Sheikh**, armed refugees from the Bihin and Biyoley camps did most of the killing and looting. In **Erigavo**, non-Isaaq men were armed and Isaaq men detained. **Arabsiyo** saw looting and the shooting of elderly people. In **Mogadishu**, more than 300 Isaaq were detained (PDF pp. 167–176). Gersony counted air attacks on villages and grazing areas, and on fleeing people. He judged that ground and air attacks on asylum seekers "probably resulted in the deaths of hundreds" ([Gersony, pp. 22, 61](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Africa Watch witnesses describe bombing from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. at Biyaha Shinaha, Annayo, Geed-Debleh (a centre for the wounded) and Udaan, and planes that "followed some people into Ethiopia" ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 180–188](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)).

**Armed refugees and militias.** Gersony confirms that after 27 May 1988 "at least several hundred and perhaps more" UN-camp refugees "were provided weapons and induced by the Somali Armed Forces to become active in para-military operations". Witnesses saw uniformed, armed refugees helping round up Isaaq in Berbera. The refugees' own account was that they demanded arms for self-defence after repeated SNM attacks, and about 90% of the 241 refugee deaths Gersony recorded came before the camps were armed ([Gersony, pp. 51, 55–56, 63](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Africa Watch "take[s] issue" with the claim that refugees were armed only after the SNM attacks. It cites AFP on a May 1987 press-gang raid at Bihin and *The Economist* (January 1988) on UNHCR protests over the conscription of up to 7,000 refugees ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 207](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). GAO staff saw armed refugees in six of fourteen camps in July 1988, and the government told UNHCR they were armed "for their own protection" [OFFICIAL, via secondary extract; GAO not read] ([GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-89-159.pdf)). Gersony also records a non-government Gadabursi clan militia, the "Gadabursi Front", which had at times received army weapons; one Isaaq refugee attributed a killing to it ([Gersony, pp. 43–44](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)).

**Livelihood, mines, rape.** Africa Watch documents, from 1984 on, the deliberate destruction of water reservoirs, the killing and confiscation of livestock, and the indiscriminate mining of roads, reservoirs and the approaches to army camps. It says the mines were mainly Egyptian-sourced, with American and Italian types. It adds that it could not investigate mines independently ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 104–105, 178–179](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Community Aid Abroad reported that after two soldiers were killed by a mine in Elafweyn District, the Sanaag military commander ordered 200 nomads killed and 103 were slaughtered the next day [HR REPORT quoting NGO] (PDF p. 105). Gersony recorded rapes of Isaaq women, the great majority before May 1988 ([Gersony, p. 31](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Africa Watch's introduction describes soldiers raping women in front of their families ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 12](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). HRW's World Report 1989 adds "the poisoning of wells" to the list of means used ([HRW WR 1989](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/worldreports/world.89/somalia.pdf)). Africa Watch raised the possible use of chemical weapons, citing a January 1989 NBC report of Libyan supplies. The State Department rejected the concern, and no finding exists ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 219–220](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)).

**The government's position.** The government denied that civilians had been killed or civilian targets deliberately destroyed. It blamed "bandits", called civilian casualties "exceptional episodes", and broadcast that "four lice-ridden bandits on a suicide mission" had been driven out of Hargeisa [GOV STATEMENT, as reported] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 13–14, 148](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). It refused Africa Watch access to the north, despite a promise by Prime Minister Samatar (PDF p. 10). Its January 1989 "Reconciliation Committee" visited only Berbera and Burao and never published findings ([AW *Evading Reality*, p. 5](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/s/somalia/somalia909.pdf)). Asked by the UN independent expert in 2001 about responsibility for tens of thousands of deaths in Hargeisa, Morgan "asserted that people had had a right to defend their country against perceived foreign intervention in that period". He "alleged that no civilians had been killed and that it was not possible to attribute specific gravesites to specific battles" [OFFICIAL, recording a denial] ([E/CN.4/2002/119, para. 69](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/2002/119&l=en&t=pdf)).

## The SNM's abuses fell on non-Isaaq civilians and refugees, at a smaller scale

The sources that document the army's killings also document the SNM's. Gersony's SNM findings say that in the first three months of the offensive, SNM combatants killed unarmed civilians "in individual instances which together may have resulted in the deaths of at least several hundred or more persons". The victims included "men sought out and killed apparently only because they were not Issaks", and in one region prisoners of war were executed after interrogation [OFFICIAL] ([Gersony, p. 62](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). In Burao, within days of 27 May, the SNM rounded up "several hundred" men, mostly non-Isaaq, and "about fifty were executed... in a pit dug in the earth under a tree" after courts-martial, one of which lasted ten minutes. Gersony concluded that "SNM commanders must have been aware of and at least condoned these executions". His five witnesses (a Hawiye, an Ogadeni, two Dhulbahante and a Warsangeli) were identified for him by the government, a non-random sample he discloses (pp. 40–42, 62). His directly recorded SNM toll is **76 deaths**: 5 at checkpoints, 21 near battle areas and 50 in Burao (p. 44). From about 30 May "and continuing through the balance of 1988", the SNM attacked UN camps for Ethiopian refugees. Gersony "conservatively estimated" **at least 400** unarmed refugees killed and recorded 241 deaths across nine camps. The tolls were Dam 52, Adiades 38, Sabaad 38, Biyoley 36, Bixin 33, Las Dhure 25, Agabar 18, Tug Wajale 1 and Arapsio 0. The attack on Agabar and Las Dhure on 30/31 May killed 43; those camps had never been armed, and the aim "appeared to be to employ violence to force the refugees out of the area" (pp. 49, 51–52, 63). Gersony also notes an internal inconsistency: one passage dates all SNM killings to 27 May–31 August 1988, while others run into 1989 (pp. 34, 62–63).

Africa Watch's own chapter on the SNM gives the Isaaq-side witnesses' view and its own judgement ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 202–208](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). In Burao the SNM, guided by crowds, shot officials identified as abusive. These were the local military commander "Ed'eed", the NSS head Abdiqani, the police commander Captain "Falso", his deputy Ina Afrah, and "Inda Dilo", the head of the qat-control force and of the main prison. Africa Watch condemns the executions of people in custody or hors de combat (PDF pp. 137, 203). It reports that Isaaq nomads who had armed themselves for the SNM stopped an SNM convoy and shot its Darood passengers. It also reports that young recruits killed unarmed non-Isaaq civilians, including the teacher Hassan Abole, shot while fleeing. Africa Watch found no evidence that the SNM "as an organization had a policy of deliberately targeting civilians" on the basis that they were non-Isaaq. Refugees told it the SNM warned them sternly against revenge, while treating armed non-Isaaq civilians as combatants (PDF pp. 203–204). The SNM also shot Isaaq suspected of spying. Before the war these included two nomads near Geed-Debleh (1987); after it, businessmen in Hargeisa, and in November 1988 Isaaq wives of soldiers at Habaas Weyne (PDF pp. 202, 204). In January 1987 it kidnapped an MSF medical team for about a week (PDF p. 202). Africa Watch does "not dispute Gersony's contention that the SNM has killed as many as four hundred Ogadeni refugees". It condemns those killings and reports that the SNM diverted UNHCR supplies (PDF p. 207). The GAO saw an "emerging SNM pattern of terrorizing the Ogadeni refugees to force their removal" after a March 1989 truck attack killed 11 refugees (quoted at PDF p. 206). The State Department's 1989 human-rights report says SNM fighters around Erigavo in March 1989 "killed a number of unresisting civilians". It judged that the SNM committed abuses "to a lesser extent" than the government, and that both sides gravely violated the protected status of Ethiopian refugees [OFFICIAL] ([USDOS 1989 via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)).

**Non-Isaaq northern perspectives.** The Gadabuursi, Dhulbahante, Warsangeli and Issa "had sided, as a rule, with the Barre government" and fought for it until its fall [SCHOLARSHIP] ([Hoehne, RVI 2015](https://riftvalley.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Between-Somaliland-and-Puntland-by-Markus-Hoehne-RVI-Contested-Borderlands-2015.pdf); [Hoehne, MPI WP 82](https://www.eth.mpg.de/pubs/wps/pdf/mpi-eth-working-paper-0082.pdf)). The reasons the sources give differ by group. The Dhulbahante had kinship ties to the regime. Others feared domination by the Isaaq or the SNM. Some were armed by the state. Africa Watch testimony records the government telling the Darood population that "the Isaaks planned to take over the whole country" and "destroy the Darood" ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 171](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). The Ethiopian Ogadeni refugees were both instruments and victims: conscripted by force, armed, and attacked by the SNM. Gersony records that people of each community saved lives in the other ([Gersony, p. 65](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). For **January–February 1991**, Canada's IRB, citing 1991 press and NGO sources, records "ethnic cleansing" by the SNM in the Borama region and the partial destruction of Dila [OFFICIAL compilation] ([IRB via ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1311612.html)). A Gadabuursi-perspective outlet gives up to 1,000 dead at Borama on 4 February 1991; this is a community claim without a primary source [ADVOCACY] ([Kormeeraha](https://www.kormeeraha.com/2022/02/03/borama-massacre-the-right-to-remember/)). Pro-Somaliland accounts describe Dila as a battle in which Barre-armed Gadabuursi militias fought [TERTIARY] ([Wikipedia: Battle of Dilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dilla_(1991))). Today some Dhulbahante writers say Somaliland's national memory "revolved primarily around the Isaaq struggle and SNM heroism" and leaves out their own losses [ADVOCACY/opinion] ([WardheerNews](https://wardheernews.com/from-protest-to-liberation-how-digital-platforms-transformed-the-ssc-community-struggle-for-self-determination/)). The Dhulbahante areas around Las Anod formed the SSC-Khaatumo administration after the 2023 conflict ([Hiraal Institute](https://hiraalinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/The-Las-Anod-Conflict.pdf)).

### Investigators, witnesses and later actors

### Destruction and graves

GAO (May 1989), as quoted by Africa Watch: "The U.S. Embassy estimated that 70 percent of the city [Hargeisa] has been damaged or destroyed. Our rough visual inspection confirms this estimate" [OFFICIAL] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF pp. 158–159](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Africa Watch in September 1990, in its own voice: "Seventy per cent of Hargeisa has been destroyed" ([*Evading Reality* p. 19](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/s/somalia/somalia909.pdf)). The State Department's 1989 report: "about 90 percent of the Isaak homes, businesses, and property" in Hargeisa destroyed or severely damaged, which is a narrower measure ([ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/1309167.html)). The commonly cited 90% for Hargeisa and 70% for Burao have only tertiary and advocacy sources ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardment_of_Burao_and_Hargeisa_1988)). The 1997 Physicians for Human Rights mission under OHCHR (17–21 December 1997) observed **92 to 116 alleged graves** at three sites: the Milk Factory, Malko Durduro Elementary School and Badhka. It test-excavated **two**, which held at least six individuals, three of them bound by one rope. It concluded that "human rights violations were committed against these individuals" [OFFICIAL] ([E/CN.4/1999/103/Add.1](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/1999/103/Add.1&l=en&t=pdf)). Later Somaliland exhumations, with the Peruvian team EPAF from 2012, are reported as 11 graves and 318 skeletons, or about two dozen graves and about 250 bodies, depending on the source; more than 200 mass graves are believed to exist [PRESS/SCHOLARSHIP] ([Lacuna](https://lacuna.org.uk/war-and-peace/somalilands-hope-for-transitional-justice-after-the-isaaq-genocide/); [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12973517/)). These finds corroborate the execution pattern. They are not a basis for a total.

## "Genocide" is a characterisation by investigators and scholars, not a court finding

**The legal test.** Article II of the 1948 Convention requires "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" ([UN GA res. 260 (III)](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=A/RES/260(III)&l=en&t=pdf)). Clans are not named in it. Africa Watch itself calls Somalia "the one nation in Africa that is truly homogeneous — ethnically, culturally, and linguistically" ([PDF p. 11](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). Whether a patrilineal clan-family qualifies would turn on the ICTR's *Akayesu* reasoning about "stable and permanent" groups, known here only through secondary digests ([HRW digest](https://www.hrw.org/reports/2004/ij/ictr/3.htm)), or on a perpetrator-defined approach that commentators dispute ([Georgetown JIL](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/international-law-journal/wp-content/uploads/sites/21/2024/09/GT-GJIL240030-1.pdf)). No court and no peer-reviewed legal analysis found has decided this for the Isaaq.

**What contemporaries said.** Gersony wrote that victims were selected "principally because of their ethnic identity" and that the Berbera men died "solely because they were Issaks" [OFFICIAL] ([pp. 60–61](https://cja.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Gersony-Why-Somalis-Flee-Aug-1989.pdf)). Africa Watch's 1990 report uses the word "genocide" **exactly once**, and not as its own conclusion. In its Erigavo section it quotes Peter Kieseker, spokesman for the Australian agency Community Aid Abroad, who said of government policies in Sanaag (Erigavo and the "scorched earth" of the Elafweyn plains): "Genocide is the only word for it". The source was Alison Whyte in *The Independent*, 13 February 1989 [PRESS quoted in HR REPORT] ([Africa Watch 1990, PDF p. 170](https://www.hrw.org/reports/somalia_1990.pdf)). This is the earliest public use of the word for these events found in these sources. Africa Watch's own framing is "a government at war with its own people" and "state-sponsored terrorism": the army treated being Isaaq as being "anti-government" (PDF pp. 12–13). HRW's World Report 1989 speaks of "vicious discrimination against certain ethnic groups — currently the Isaaqs" and a "savage counterinsurgency campaign" ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/worldreports/world.89/somalia.pdf)).

**What the UN mechanisms said.** The UN independent expert Mona Rishmawi wrote that "if these are mass killings, they will constitute a crime against humanity" ([E/CN.4/1998/96 para. 63](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/1998/96&l=en&t=pdf)) and that "mass killings constitute war crimes and crimes against humanity" ([E/CN.4/1999/103 para. 130](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/1999/103&l=en&t=pdf)). She dismissed as lacking credibility the counter-claim that the Hargeisa dead were refugees killed by the SNM (1998/96 para. 61). None of the expert reports from 1997 to 2005 uses "genocide" for the Isaaq [OFFICIAL]. The genocide conclusion comes from a **preliminary study completed in March 2002**, "jointly commissioned by the United Nations Coordination Unit (UNCU)/UNDP-Somalia and OHCHR" ([E/CN.4/2003/115 para. 12](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/2003/115&l=en&t=pdf)). It is catalogued as Chris Mburu, *Past Human Rights Abuses in Somalia* ([Stanford SearchWorks](https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/6330721)), and is often misdated 2001. Its key sentence is known only through secondary quotation: "the consultant firmly believes that the crime of genocide was conceived, planned and perpetrated by the Somali Government against the Isaaq people of northern Somalia between 1987 and 1989" [CONSULTANT, original not seen] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaaq_genocide)). It is an expert's belief in a commissioned study, not the finding of a UN organ. The UK government said in June 2025 that it "is aware of the findings of the report" ([HL7940](https://parallelparliament.co.uk/question/HL7940/somalia-human-rights)).

**Who uses the label now.** Scholarship: Mohamed Haji Ingiriis, "'We Swallowed the State as the State Swallowed Us'", *African Security* 9(3), 2016, treats the Isaaq case as one of Somalia's genocides [SCHOLARSHIP] ([T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19392206.2016.1208475)). Yale's Genocide Studies Program carries it as a case ([Yale GSP](https://macmillan.yale.edu/gsp/somalia-isaaq-genocide)). Wikipedia cites Charny (1999), Stanton (Genocide Watch, 2012) and Mayersen & Pohlman (2013), but none of these was read. Advocacy: the Lemkin Institute's 2022 statement, as reported, links recognition of the genocide to recognition of Somaliland [ADVOCACY] ([Lemkin](https://www.lemkininstitute.com/statements-new-page/statement-on-the-isaaq-genocide-committed-between-1987-and-1989-by-the-somali-government)). Somaliland's government recognises the events as genocide and hopes the evidence will support international recognition [GOV STATEMENT] ([Lacuna](https://lacuna.org.uk/war-and-peace/somalilands-hope-for-transitional-justice-after-the-isaaq-genocide/)). The sources found no sovereign state that has formally recognised it.

**Who qualifies it.** No prominent scholar was found arguing explicitly that it was *not* genocide. The qualifications are of three kinds. Some accounts frame the violence as a counter-insurgency against the SNM that "devolved" into mass atrocity [TERTIARY] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Hargeisa-Burao_offensive)). Lidwien Kapteijns, writing on the 1991 killings of Darod civilians, reportedly finds the word "not particularly productive" for Somali clan violence and prefers "clan cleansing" [SCHOLARSHIP] ([GSP review](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1402&context=gsp)); her book is itself contested ([Méthod(e)s 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/23754745.2017.1362138)). The UN mechanisms themselves used the war-crimes and crimes-against-humanity framing. On the perpetrators' side, Morgan denied to the UN that any civilians were killed. Samantar conceded civil liability while denying guilt, and Ali's lawyer called the 2019 verdict clan vengeance. No position of the Somali federal government on the label was found.

**What courts have and have not found.** [COURT] *Samantar v. Yousuf*, 560 U.S. 305 (2010): the FSIA does not govern an individual official's immunity. This was procedural, and no facts were found. [COURT] 4th Cir., 2 Nov 2012, 699 F.3d 763: no immunity "for jus cogens violations, even if the acts were performed in the defendant's official capacity". The court listed the acts "involved in this case" as "torture, summary execution and prolonged arbitrary imprisonment". "Genocide" appears in the opinion only where it restates the plaintiffs' argument and in generic lists of jus cogens norms ([cert petition appendix, pp. 26a–32a](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/Samantar%20v%20Yousuf%20Petition%20for%20Writ%20of%20Certiorari%20FINAL%20(1).pdf)). [CONCEDED + COURT on a default record] E.D. Va., 28 Aug 2012, ruled on claims of extrajudicial killing, attempted killing, torture, cruel treatment, arbitrary detention, crimes against humanity and war crimes. **No genocide claim was pleaded**, and the word does not occur in the 38-page opinion. The facts were "accepted as true" because Samantar defaulted. He was found to have ordered and permitted the violations, and $21m was awarded, with execution stayed for bankruptcy ([Dkt. 366](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/Samantar%20Final%20Judgment.pdf)). [JURY] *Warfaa v. Ali*, 21 May 2019: liable for torture, not for attempted extrajudicial killing. The war-crimes and crimes-against-humanity claims had been dismissed on extraterritoriality grounds in 2014, affirmed in February 2016, with certiorari denied on 26 June 2017; again, no genocide claim ([amended complaint](https://cja.org/cja/downloads/Tukeh%20Amended%20Complaint.pdf); [CJA](https://cja.org/?p=14617)). No criminal prosecution anywhere, and no international court, has dealt with these events.

## The war ended with the SNM's capture of the north and the declaration of Somaliland

By October 1990 the SNM had driven government forces from almost all of Woqooyi Galbeed, Togdheer and Sanaag outside the besieged garrisons. It took the Berbera, Burao and Hargeisa garrisons at the end of January 1991, as Barre fled Mogadishu on 26/27 January ([UNHCR Refworld archive](https://webarchive.archive.unhcr.org/20240420015338mp_/https://www.refworld.org/docid/469f38dbc.html); [Tufts](https://sites.tufts.edu/atrocityendings/?p=50)). The day each town fell is not documented in the sources reached. The SNM's push into Gadabuursi country (Dila, then Borama in early February) produced the 1991 killings that Gadabuursi communities remember (see above). Two clan conferences followed: Berbera in February 1991, with Issa, Gadabuursi, Dhulbahante, Warsangeli and Isaaq delegates, and the "Grand Conference of the Northern Clans" at Burao from 27 April 1991. On **18 May 1991** the SNM Central Committee, backed by elders of the major northern clans, declared the restoration of the Republic of Somaliland within the borders of the former British protectorate. Tuur announced it, and the Dhulbahante Garad Abdiqani Garad Jama was among the signatories [TERTIARY] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somaliland_Declaration_of_Independence); [Future Generations](https://www.future.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/somaliland-peacebuilding.pdf)). The Borama conference (24 January – May 1993) replaced Tuur with Mohamed Haji Ibrahim Egal on 5 May 1993, with a Gadabuursi vice-president, Abdirahman Aw Ali Farah, in a power-sharing settlement ([Princeton ISS](https://successfulsocieties.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf5601/files/Navigating%20a%20Broken%20Transition_Somaliland_ToU_1.pdf)). Peace was incomplete: there was fighting in 1992 and 1994–96. The Dhulbahante largely opposed secession, and the Warsangeli and Dhulbahante joined the process late ([Interpeace/APD](https://www.interpeace.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/07/2008_SomP_PDRC_Interpeace_A_Bottom_Up_Approach_To_Peace_And_Statebuilding_EN.pdf)).

After 1991 came the evidence and memory work. Graves were exposed by rain in May 1997. A Technical Committee for the Investigation of War Crimes was set up in Hargeisa in 1997 with a budget of US$8,000 a year ([E/CN.4/2002/119 para. 102](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=E/CN.4/2002/119&l=en&t=pdf)). The PHR mission followed in December 1997, and EPAF exhumations from 2012. The UNHCR repatriation from Hartisheik ran from 1997 to 2004 ([UNHCR](https://unhcr.org/news/latest/2004/7/40e426de4/worlds-largest-refugee-camp-closes-ethiopia.html)). HALO Trust has cleared mines since 1999; by 2012 it had cleared about 18,000 hectares and destroyed about 100,000 mines and explosive items ([HALO](https://halotrust.org/media-centre/news/international-mine-awareness-day-in-somaliland)). A Somali Air Force MiG on Hargeisa's Freedom Square commemorates the bombing ([Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/hargeisa-war-memorial)), and 31 May is remembered there ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hargeisa_(1988))). Israel recognised Somaliland on 26 December 2025. No evidence was found that this included recognition of the genocide ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Somaliland_relations)); check current status before publishing.

## Conclusion

Neutrality on this subject does not mean splitting the difference. It means keeping each claim attached to its evidence type. The core facts are as well documented as anything in the Somali civil war: the bombing of Hargeisa and Burao, the clan-based executions in Berbera, the arming of refugees, and the flight of hundreds of thousands. Two independent investigations of 1989–90, one by the US government and one by a human-rights NGO, used different witnesses, reached the same pattern and named the same sites. Their figures, 5,000 and 50,000–60,000, differ because they measure different things. A page that shows both, with their definitions, is more accurate than one that picks a single number. The SNM's abuses are documented by the same sources at a smaller scale, and including them strengthens rather than weakens the account.

The word "genocide" has a traceable history. In 1989 it was an aid worker's cry quoted in a newspaper. By 2002 it was a UN consultant's "firm belief". Later it became a scholarly category and an element of Somaliland's case for recognition, while no court has ever tested it. That history is itself part of what happened after 1988, and the page should tell it that way. It should also say that the open legal question, whether a Somali clan-family is a protected group, has never been put before a judge.
