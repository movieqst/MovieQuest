**The approach:**
- **The whole war in the north, in eight parts:** the years of emergency rule, the offensive and Burao, Hargeisa, the towns the SNM never attacked, the flight, the refugees and the SNM's abuses, the long war to 1991, and what came after. Chapter numbers run in page order (1–30).
- **Every claim keeps its kind of evidence.** A court ruling, a conceded liability, a jury verdict, a government investigation, a human-rights report, a witness, a government statement and a scholar's view are never merged. The spoken words say who says what.
- **Who did what is named by force:** "the army", "soldiers", "the National Security Service", "military intelligence", "armed refugees", "clan militias", "the SNM", "SNM fighters". The script never makes "the Isaaq", "the Darood", "the Ogadeni" or any clan the actor. Not every Isaaq was SNM, and no clan is collectively guilty.
- **Every number says who counted it, what it counts, where and when.** Numbers from different counts are never added together.
- **The word genocide** is explained in Introduction 5, in chapter 29 and in the Debrief: who uses it and why, what the United Nations' own experts said, and that no court has ruled on it.
- **No graphic detail.** Killings are told plainly, once, without lingering. Witnesses are people: their words are given as Africa Watch or Gersony recorded them, with their names only where the source gives them.
- **Spoken forms:** numbers and dates are written as the narrator says them. "SNM" is introduced in full first. Other initials are written out.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the war in northern Somalia in 1988. A rebel movement, the Somali National Movement, attacked two northern cities, Burao and Hargeisa. The government of President Mohamed Siad Barre answered with bombing, shelling and mass killings. Most of the victims were civilians of the Isaaq clan-family. Hundreds of thousands of people fled. This is a recent and painful history. Many families in Somaliland, in Somalia and in the refugee camps still carry it. We will tell you what is known, who says what, and what is still not known. Some chapters describe killings. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### 1. SOMALIA 1988
- In May 1988, rebels of the Somali National Movement attacked Burao and then Hargeisa, in northern Somalia. *(established)*
- The army answered with artillery, air raids and mass executions. The violence fell overwhelmingly on civilians of the Isaaq clan-family, in what is today Somaliland. *(established)*
- An investigator for the United States State Department, Robert Gersony, gave a deliberately cautious lowest figure. He estimated that at least five thousand unarmed Isaaq civilians were purposefully murdered by the army between May 1988 and March 1989. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- The human-rights group Africa Watch estimated fifty to sixty thousand dead over nineteen months. Its figure counts more kinds of death, including deaths from bombing, shelling, wounds and disease. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch)*
- Close to half a million people fled abroad, most of them to camps in Ethiopia. *(estimate)*
- The same investigators also recorded killings by the rebels, of non-Isaaq civilians and of refugees from Ethiopia, on a much smaller scale. *(report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Many researchers, and Somaliland's government, call the campaign against the Isaaq a genocide. No court has ever ruled on that question. *(established)*

### 2. The land and its peoples
*Map: North · 1988*
- Somalia lies in the Horn of Africa. Its north-west borders Djibouti and Ethiopia. *(established)*
- Africa Watch called Somalia "the one nation in Africa that is truly homogeneous: ethnically, culturally, and linguistically". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Somali society is organised into clans. A clan-family is a very large kin group, made up of many clans. Membership passes through the father's line. *(established)*
- Most people in the north-west belonged to the Isaaq clan-family. The rural areas were almost entirely Isaaq, and so were most people in the big towns, Hargeisa and Burao. *(report: Gersony)*
- Other clans lived in the north too, among them the Gadabuursi, the Issa, the Dhulbahante and the Warsangeli. *(established)*
- The main northern towns were Hargeisa and Burao. Berbera was the north's port. *(established)*
- The borders of today's Somaliland are those of the former British protectorate in the north. *(established)*

### 3. Barre's Somalia
*Map: North · 1969 to 1982*
- Mohamed Siad Barre took power in a military coup on the twenty-first of October 1969. *(established)*
- His government said it stood against clannism, the politics of clan loyalty. Historians and other accounts say that power came to rest on a few clans, among them Barre's own Marehan, and the Ogaden and the Dhulbahante. *(established; historians' view)*
- In March 1978 Somalia was defeated in the Ogaden war against Ethiopia. Hundreds of thousands of refugees from the Ogaden, in Ethiopia, then crossed into Somalia. Many were settled in camps near the north-western towns. *(established)*
- Africa Watch reports that the government formed armed groups among these refugees and conscripted many into the army. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- These refugees appear twice in this story. Some were used and armed by the government. Many others were attacked and killed by the rebels. *(report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*

### 4. The Somali National Movement
*Map: North · 1981 to 1988*
- The Somali National Movement was founded in London on the sixth of April 1981, by Isaaq emigrants. From here on we call it the SNM. *(report: United States Library of Congress)*
- Some accounts place its formal founding in Jeddah, in Saudi Arabia. *(disputed)*
- In 1982 the SNM moved its bases to Ethiopia, across the border from the north-west. *(established)*
- The SNM drew its fighters mainly from the Isaaq. But being Isaaq did not make a person a rebel. Most of the people in this story were civilians who had no part in the fighting. *(established)*
- Accounts say the SNM was led from 1984 to 1990 by Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud, known as Silanyo. *(snm)*

### 5. Why the title says genocide
- The title of this page uses a name that many researchers and Somaliland's government use: the Isaaq genocide. *(established)*
- Genocide is a crime in international law. It means acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group, in whole or in part. *(established)*
- Scholars, a genocide-prevention institute, Somaliland's government and a study written for the United Nations in 2002 by a consultant use the word. *(established; their view)*
- The United Nations' own human-rights experts, writing between 1997 and 2005, spoke instead of war crimes and crimes against humanity. *(report: UN expert)*
- No court has ruled on whether these events were genocide. We explain why in chapter twenty-nine. *(established)*

### 6. The investigators
- Robert Gersony wrote his report for the United States State Department in August 1989. It rests on three hundred and two interviews, two hundred and fifty-two of them about the north, gathered in Somalia, Ethiopia and Kenya between March and May 1989. *(report: Gersony)*
- Gersony spoke with Isaaq refugees, with non-Isaaq Somalis who stayed, and with refugees from Ethiopia in the camps. *(report: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch, a human-rights organisation, published its report in January 1990. Its researchers interviewed newly arrived refugees in Djibouti and in Britain in 1989. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The government did not allow Africa Watch into the north. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The two reports used different witnesses. They describe the same pattern of events, and they name the same places. *(established)*
- Later evidence came from United Nations experts, forensic scientists who examined graves, and two civil cases in American courts. *(established)*

### 7. How this page tells it
- Each line carries a tag. "Established" means the evidence strongly supports it. "Government" marks an account from the Barre government. "SNM" marks an account from the SNM or from Somaliland. *(established; editorial definition)*
- "Witness" marks one person's testimony. "Estimate" marks an uncertain number. "Disputed" means sources disagree, or rest on one unconfirmed account. *(established; editorial definition)*
- "Court" marks a court record, and says what kind. "Report" marks a human-rights or official investigation, and names it. *(established; editorial definition)*
- Where numbers differ, it is usually because they count different things. We give each number with who counted it, and what it counts. We never add them together. *(established; editorial convention)*

## Part 1: The north under emergency rule, 1978 to 1987

### 1. Refugees and militias
*Map: North · 1978 to 1982*
- After 1978, armed groups grew up among the refugees from Ethiopia. One was the Western Somali Liberation Front. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch says that militias encouraged by the government "committed atrocities against the Isaak civilian population living along the Somali/Ethiopian border". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- On the thirtieth of March 1982, twenty-one Isaaq elders sent a memorandum to President Barre. *(witness: document reproduced by Africa Watch)*
- They complained that the Western Somali Liberation Front subjected rural people to "murder and robbery". They complained that northerners were shut out of running the refugee programme. And they said the north was neglected in trade, industry and public works. *(witness: document reproduced by Africa Watch)*
- In 1980 a new military commander was appointed in the north, Major General Mohamed Hashi Gani. Africa Watch says the military commanders were also, in effect, the governors. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 2. The Hargeisa Group
*Map: Hargeisa · December 1981 to February 1982*
- In December 1981 the authorities arrested about thirty doctors, engineers and teachers in Hargeisa. They had organised volunteer work to improve the city's hospital. Africa Watch calls them the Hargeisa Group. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch treats their arrest as the political trigger in the north. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Their trial in February 1982 set off demonstrations in Hargeisa and across the north. Witnesses date the serious disturbances to about the twentieth of February. A curfew was imposed. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In Somaliland the group is remembered as "Uffo", and the protests as "Dhagax Tuur". Somaliland accounts give different dates for the protests. *(snm)*
- In the same month the government brought back a system of letters of credit for trade, and confiscated goods at Berbera port. Africa Watch puts their value at about three hundred million Somali shillings. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 3. Courts of the emergency
*Map: Hargeisa · 1982 to 1986*
- From 1982, emergency regulations put civilians in the north under military courts. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The Mobile Military Court travelled from town to town. People nicknamed it "Guilty or Innocent, You Will be Found Guilty". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In Hargeisa, its executions took place at Badhka, an open ground in the south-west of the city. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Alongside the court were a Regional Security Council, chaired by the military commander, and the military intelligence force. Africa Watch describes the three as one system of control. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The court's prosecutor was a colonel, Yusuf Muse, known as Yusuf Dherre. Africa Watch calls him "notorious for his cruelty and his insistence on the death sentence". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- For a time, from 1986, he was replaced by Colonel Abdillahi Debob. Africa Watch says Debob "refused to carry out some unlawful executions". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- On the second of January 1983, SNM fighters raided Mandera prison, between Berbera and Hargeisa, and freed prisoners. Accounts of how many were freed range from "hundreds" to about seven hundred. *(disputed)*

### 4. Reprisals, 1984
*Map: Core · October to December 1984*
- Africa Watch dates the start of systematic reprisal killings to 1984, when the SNM stepped up its raids from Ethiopia. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- On the fifteenth of October 1984, six men were shot at Gogol Wanaag, near Arabsiyo. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- On the seventeenth of November, twenty-six Isaaq men were executed in Hargeisa after sentences by the Mobile Military Court. Africa Watch says nine were civilians and seventeen were SNM fighters. The town's elders were forced to watch. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In December, forty-three men were executed in Burao. Witnesses told Africa Watch that the charges against them had been changed from ordinary crimes to links with the SNM. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- From 1984, Africa Watch also documents the destruction of water reservoirs, the killing and seizing of livestock, and the laying of mines on roads and near reservoirs. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Water and herds were what rural families lived on. *(established)*

### 5. General Morgan's report
*Map: Core · late 1986 to May 1987*
- At the end of 1986, General Mohamed Saeed Hirsi, known as Morgan, took over as military commander in the north. Africa Watch says he brought in photo identity cards to control people's movements. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In February 1987, a confidential report from General Morgan to President Barre leaked to the international press. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch writes that the report set out policies Morgan had been carrying out to "liquidate" what he called the "Isaak problem". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch lists the measures. Confiscating Isaaq property. Suspending business licences and freezing bank accounts. Removing Isaaq from sensitive government posts. Relocating villages. Destroying water reservoirs. And resettling refugees from Ethiopia on Isaaq land. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch did not question the document. No independent check of its authenticity was found, and no statement by Morgan about this document. *(disputed)*
- In Somaliland it is known as the "Letter of Death". That name is not used by Africa Watch. *(snm)*
- In January 1987, SNM fighters kidnapped a medical team of the French charity Médecins Sans Frontières, Doctors Without Borders. The team was released after about a week. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In May 1987, the French news agency reported that an army raid at Bihin refugee camp, near Berbera, forced two to three hundred refugees into the army. *(report: Africa Watch)*

## Part 2: The offensive and Burao

### 6. The door closes
*Map: Core · late 1987 to 3 April 1988*
- At the end of 1987, Africa Watch says, Yusuf Dherre returned as prosecutor of the military court, and mass executions began again. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In March 1988 President Barre visited the north. At a rally in Hargeisa's stadium, students threw stones. Witnesses say the presidential guard fired into the crowd and killed five students. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- During the same visit, on the fourteenth of March, twenty-four or twenty-five Isaaq men were executed at Gebiley and Tug Wajale, near the Ethiopian border. Witnesses described it as a reprisal for an SNM attack. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- On the third of April 1988, Somalia and Ethiopia signed a peace accord. Each promised not to help the other's rebels. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The SNM's bases in Ethiopia were now at risk. Less than two months later, it attacked. *(established)*

### 7. Friday in Burao
*Map: Burao · 27 May 1988*
- On Friday the twenty-seventh of May 1988, SNM fighters attacked Burao. *(established)*
- Africa Watch says they "captured the town in about two hours". They took the military compound at the airport, the Goon Ad compound outside the town, the police stations and the prison. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Crowds of townspeople followed the fighters. The SNM questioned residents about local officials. Several officials named as abusive were shot, among them the local military commander, the head of the secret service and the police commander. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A Burao resident, Ahmed Saeed Elmi, told Africa Watch that soldiers known to be fair to civilians were not killed, but that those reported for killing, rape and theft were. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch condemns these killings. Under the laws of war, people in custody, or no longer able to fight, must not be executed. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- How long the SNM held the town is unclear. One passage in Africa Watch says the fighters withdrew to their base by five in the afternoon. Other accounts describe the SNM in the town for days. *(disputed)*
- The same day, the army arrested Isaaq men in Berbera, Sheikh, Erigavo and Borama, towns the SNM had not attacked. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 8. The army's answer in Burao
*Map: Burao · 27 to 31 May 1988*
- Witnesses say soldiers went on a rampage in Burao on the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth of May, looting and killing. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Witnesses also say the army used loudspeakers to call non-Isaaq residents to leave, and gave them transport out. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- After that evacuation, the shelling began. Aircraft began bombing the town on Tuesday the thirty-first of May. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- An American government investigating office, the General Accounting Office, reported in May 1989 that the army "responded to the SNM attacks in May 1988 with extreme force". *(report: GAO)*
- It said the army used "artillery and aerial shelling in heavily populated urban centers", and tanks in Burao. *(report: GAO)*
- Gersony judged that civilian deaths in the crossfire, in Hargeisa and Burao, may have reached the thousands. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- The government denied that civilians had been killed, or civilian targets deliberately destroyed. It blamed "bandits", and called civilian casualties "exceptional episodes". *(government)*

### 9. The SNM's prisoners in Burao
*Map: Burao · late May 1988*
- Within days of the attack, Gersony found, the SNM rounded up several hundred men in Burao, most of them non-Isaaq. *(report: Gersony)*
- About fifty were executed after hasty military trials. A witness said one trial lasted ten minutes. Some of the dead may have been soldiers or officers. Others were civilians who had played no part in government or the army. *(report: Gersony)*
- Gersony concluded that SNM commanders "must have been aware of and at least condoned these executions". *(report: Gersony)*
- He also disclosed a limit. His five witnesses to these events, a Hawiye, an Ogadeni, two Dhulbahante and a Warsangeli, were found for him by the government. *(report: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch records that the SNM took some arrested non-Isaaq civilians toward their home villages. On the way, Isaaq nomads who had armed themselves for the SNM stopped one convoy and shot its Darood passengers. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Neither Gersony nor Africa Watch names any SNM field commander. *(established)*

## Part 3: Hargeisa

### 10. Arrests before the attack
*Map: Hargeisa · 27 May to 1 June 1988*
- In Hargeisa, the arrests began as soon as the news from Burao arrived. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Jama Osman Samater had been a political prisoner from 1982 until February 1988. He told Africa Watch he was leaving Friday prayers when he heard of the attack on Burao, and that a curfew whistle blew that afternoon. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- The next morning, the twenty-eighth of May, he saw soldiers herding people into cars. Fifty-three businessmen and elders were seized that day. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Fearing re-arrest, he shaved his head, dressed as a nomad and hid with neighbours. He said soldiers searched his house that night and took its valuables. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Detainees were held at the army's sector headquarters at Birjeh, at the Malka Durduro military compound, at the central prison and at sites of the National Security Service, the country's main secret service. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Some were taken to Mandera prison, between Hargeisa and Berbera. On the first of June, witnesses say, the SNM freed the sixty detainees held there. *(witness: Africa Watch)*

### 11. The night attack
*Map: Hargeisa · 31 May 1988*
- The SNM attacked Hargeisa at a quarter past two in the morning on the thirty-first of May 1988. Gersony and Africa Watch both give this date. *(established)*
- Khadra Muhumed Abdi, also a former political prisoner, was hiding in her aunt's house in the Dumbuluq district. She told Africa Watch that next morning she saw her own district, near the Radio Station, burning. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- She said government loudspeakers announced that "four lice-ridden bandits on a suicide mission" had entered the town and been driven out. People were urged to go home. She said many believed it, and were killed. *(witness: Africa Watch; government)*
- Gersony found that the SNM spread its fighters, in uniform and in ordinary clothes, through the crowded, mainly Isaaq neighbourhoods. He wrote that this put civilians "in the immediate line of fire". *(report: Gersony)*
- Gersony did not use this to clear either side. He wrote that neither side was absolved of responsibility for avoidable harm to Hargeisa's civilians. *(report: Gersony)*
- The SNM's defensive line ran behind the Radio Station. The government kept the airport and the Sha'ab district. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 12. Shelling and bombing
*Map: Hargeisa · early June to August 1988*
- For a day or two, Africa Watch says, the army held back. Then it counter-attacked with heavy weapons. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Long-range guns on the hilltops near the zoo, and on the hills behind Badhka, shelled the city. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Aircraft took off from Hargeisa's own airport to bomb the city. Witnesses say pilots flew low over the people trying to escape. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony recorded that the armed forces "used military aircraft to strafe and bomb residential areas in which SNM forces were dispersed". *(report: Gersony)*
- Hassan Ismail told Africa Watch: "Who could believe that planes belonging to the Somali army would take off from Hargeisa airport to bomb Hargeisa itself and its population?" *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Jama Osman Samater described the shelling as relentless. He said he lost any sense of fear, and that in prison only he had suffered, while here everyone was a victim. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Dumbuluq, south of the dry river bed that divides the city, suffered most. The SNM had never entered it. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- A French hospital technician, Jean Metenier, told reporters after his evacuation that at least two dozen people had been shot against the wall of his house. *(witness: reported by Africa Watch from the Sunday Times, 12 June 1988)*
- One witness named the aircraft as British-made Hunters and MiG jets, with small Italian planes spotting targets. Only this one witness gives the types. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Somali news reports say that on the twelfth or thirteenth of July, an air force pilot, Lieutenant Colonel Ahmed Mohamed Hassan, refused an order to bomb Hargeisa and flew his jet to Djibouti instead. No human-rights report we used confirms this. *(disputed)*

### 13. Executions in the city
*Map: Hargeisa · June 1988*
- Witnesses told Africa Watch of men shot in lines into a trench inside the Malka Durduro military compound. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A prison employee said seventy Isaaq soldiers held as detainees were shot there on the fourth day of the arrests. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A detained truck driver described executions of at least thirty people a day, from about the seventh to the fifteenth of June, at Qaybta, the regional commander's headquarters. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- He said that on one day, eighty Isaaq soldiers, from Gebiley, Darar Weyne and Bogol Jirreh, were shot along the dry river bed. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Witnesses described soldiers searching house to house and killing the men they found. One father, they said, lost seven sons. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch reports that the army armed non-Isaaq civilians. It released non-Isaaq prisoners, including men convicted of murder, to guard Isaaq detainees or to fight. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony documented two hundred and thirty-three people killed in thirty-two summary executions, and recorded the names of forty-three per cent of them. *(report: Gersony)*

### 14. A city in ruins
*Map: Hargeisa · August 1988 to 1989*
- Several Africa Watch witnesses place the SNM's withdrawal from Hargeisa in August 1988. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- They say the army kept shelling afterwards, looted, and laid mines in buildings, among them the Union Hotel. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- No source gives an exact date when the army regained control of Hargeisa or of Burao. *(established)*
- Gersony found that seventy per cent of the army killings his witnesses described happened between the twenty-seventh of May and the thirty-first of August 1988. *(report: Gersony)*
- The American embassy estimated that seventy per cent of Hargeisa was damaged or destroyed. The General Accounting Office said its own rough inspection confirmed this. *(estimate; report: GAO)*
- The State Department's human-rights report for 1989 measured something narrower. It said about ninety per cent of the Isaaq homes, businesses and property in Hargeisa were destroyed or severely damaged. *(estimate; report: State Department)*

## Part 4: Where the SNM never came

### 15. Berbera: the roundups
*Map: Berbera · late May to September 1988*
- The SNM never attacked Berbera, the northern port. It stayed in government hands throughout. *(established)*
- Gersony found that from shortly after the twenty-seventh of May until at least September, soldiers "systematically collected large numbers of Issak males on the sole basis of their clan association". *(report: Gersony)*
- They were held near Berbera airport. At night they were taken out in groups and killed. Most had their throats cut, and they were buried in mass graves. *(report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony's witnesses described groups of five to fifty. They placed one burial site about a kilometre from the end of the runway. *(report: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch, from other witnesses, describes batches of thirty to forty, at a site about ten kilometres from the town, near the airport, mainly in June. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony's count is "at least five hundred, and perhaps many more" Isaaq men, killed "apparently solely because they were Issaks". Africa Watch, separately, also says at least five hundred. *(estimate; report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Most victims, Africa Watch says, were seasonal workers from Hargeisa and Burao, hired to load livestock and unload goods at the port. Among the dead whose names it learned were a restaurant owner, a taxi driver, a cement-factory worker, a port employee and a soldier. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- A witness, Kaiser Ismail Adan, told Africa Watch that the town learned of the killings from a survivor, Abdi Waal. He was in the first group of twenty-one. He pretended to be dead and escaped, badly wounded. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- The same witness said soldiers later took Abdi Waal from the hospital, and that no one knows what became of him. *(witness: Africa Watch)*

### 16. Berbera: the ship and the prison
*Map: Berbera · June to August 1988*
- On the twenty-first of June 1988, a ship, the *Emviyara*, docked at Berbera with people deported from Saudi Arabia. Witnesses say military intelligence officers separated out twenty-nine Isaaq passengers. Eight of them were later killed. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch also records an account that eleven men were burned to death east of Batalale, near Berbera, on the orders of three senior officers. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A prison in Berbera built for a hundred and twenty people held four hundred and eighty-five at one point. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A detainee told Africa Watch that the military commander in Berbera, General Ahmed Warsame, was "particularly brutal and responsible for most of the killings". *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Witnesses described his replacement, around July, General Yusuf Talan, as "more humane". He released forty sick and old detainees. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- These are witnesses' accounts. No court or official inquiry has examined the conduct of either officer. *(established)*

### 17. Towns without a battle
*Map: North · late May to December 1988*
- The pattern of Berbera was repeated in smaller towns that saw no SNM attack. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In Sheikh, armed refugees from the Bihin and Biyoley camps did most of the killing and looting, witnesses said. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In Erigavo, non-Isaaq men were armed and Isaaq men detained. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In Arabsiyo, witnesses described looting, and elderly people shot, in early June. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- In Borama, Isaaq drivers were arrested on the twenty-ninth of May. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Far to the south, in the capital Mogadishu, more than three hundred Isaaq were detained. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch's framing is "a government at war with its own people". It says the army came to treat being Isaaq as being "anti-government". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony wrote that in the killings he recorded, "the appearance that victims were selected for these killings principally because of their ethnic identity is unmistakable". *(report: Gersony)*

## Part 5: Flight

### 18. On the roads
*Map: Core · June to September 1988*
- When the fighting in the towns grew worse, people fled on foot and in vehicles, carrying the old and the sick in hand carts. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The General Accounting Office found that the evacuation of Hargeisa and Burao began in early June. Refugees told it they were strafed by aircraft while gathering their families on the edges of the cities. *(report: GAO)*
- Mohamed Karshe Mohamed, a former seaman, told Africa Watch that after nine days of fighting his family left with nothing. "Because of the planes you never lost the fear," he said, "so you just kept going." *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A man called Yusuf told Africa Watch: "It was certain death if you remained and it was death if you left." *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Witnesses describe bombing from six in the morning to six in the evening at places where people had gathered: Biyaha Shinaha, Annayo, Udaan and Geed-Debleh, where there was a centre for the wounded. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- A woman Africa Watch calls Fadumo, not her real name, set out with about a hundred people for Annayo, west of Hargeisa. She said they learned that a bomber always came after a spotter plane, and hid in the bush when the spotter appeared. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch says the planes "followed some people into Ethiopia". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony judged that ground and air attacks on people fleeing "probably resulted in the deaths of hundreds of asylum seekers". *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- Most refugees reached Ethiopia between June and September 1988. *(report: Gersony)*

### 19. The camps in Ethiopia
*Map: Core · June 1988 to 1990*
- Across the border, the largest camps were Hartisheik and Hartisheik B. Smaller camps were set up at Camp Aboker, Rabasso and Daror. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Jama Osman Samater told Africa Watch that at Hartisheik there were no tents in the first week, and no water. His family drank muddy rainwater from puddles and ate raw corn until they got ration cards. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Yassin Karshe Mohamed, who stayed in the camps from August 1988 to February 1989, said: "No one who hasn't seen them with his own eyes can imagine life in the camps." He described too little food and water, and many deaths among small children. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch reports malaria, jaundice, meningitis and diarrhoea in the camps. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony's report gave an estimated three to five hundred thousand arrivals in eastern Ethiopia between May 1988 and January 1989, up to four thousand a day. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch, in January 1990, counted over four hundred thousand in Ethiopia, about forty thousand in Djibouti, and close to four hundred thousand displaced inside Somalia. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch)*
- The State Department counted three hundred and twenty-four thousand refugees in Ethiopia and thirty thousand in Djibouti at the end of 1989. *(estimate; report: State Department)*
- These counts differ because they were made at different times, by different bodies, counting different groups. *(established)*
- The United Nations refugee agency later said Hartisheik held more than two hundred and fifty thousand people at its peak. *(estimate)*

## Part 6: Refugees, militias and the SNM's abuses

### 20. Refugees as soldiers
*Map: Core · 1987 to 1989*
- Gersony found that for many years before May 1988, the army forcibly conscripted refugees from the United Nations camps, despite strong protests from the United Nations refugee agency. *(report: Gersony)*
- After the twenty-seventh of May, he found, "at least several hundred and perhaps more" camp refugees "were provided weapons and induced by the Somali Armed Forces to become active in para-military operations". *(report: Gersony)*
- Witnesses saw armed refugees in uniform helping to round up Isaaq men in Berbera. *(witness: Gersony)*
- The refugees told Gersony they had asked for weapons to defend themselves, after repeated SNM attacks on their camps. *(witness: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch disagreed that the refugees were armed only after the SNM attacks. It cited press reports of the 1987 conscription raid at Bihin, and of refugee-agency protests in January 1988 over the conscription of up to seven thousand refugees. *(disputed)*
- The General Accounting Office saw armed refugees in six of fourteen camps in July 1988. The government told the United Nations refugee agency they were armed "for their own protection". *(report: GAO; government)*
- On the twenty-eighth of June 1988, an American shipment of rifles arrived. Africa Watch says the rifles were used to arm refugees. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony also recorded a Gadabuursi clan militia, the "Gadabursi Front", which was not part of the government but had at times received army weapons. *(report: Gersony)*

### 21. Attacks on the camps
*Map: Core · 30 May 1988 to March 1989*
- From about the thirtieth of May 1988 and through the rest of that year, Gersony found, the SNM carried out dozens of attacks on United Nations camps for refugees from Ethiopia. *(report: Gersony)*
- The first attack, on the thirtieth or thirty-first of May, struck the Agabar and Las Dhure camps, north-west of Hargeisa. Forty-three people were killed. *(report: Gersony)*
- Those two camps had never been armed. Gersony wrote that the aim "appeared to be to employ violence to force the refugees out of the area". *(report: Gersony)*
- Refugees in the camps described two hundred and forty-one deaths across nine camps to Gersony. The highest tolls were at Dam, Adiades, Sabaad, Biyoley and Bixin. *(report: Gersony)*
- About six in ten of those killed were men, including elderly men. About four in ten were women and children. *(report: Gersony)*
- Gersony "conservatively estimated" that at least four hundred unarmed refugees were killed in these attacks. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- The refugees said about ninety per cent of their dead were killed before the camps received weapons. *(witness: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch did "not dispute" Gersony's figure and condemned the killings. It also reported that the SNM diverted relief supplies meant for refugees. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In early March 1989, the General Accounting Office reported, an SNM attack on a truck killed eleven refugees. It saw an "emerging SNM pattern of terrorizing the Ogadeni refugees to force their removal". *(report: GAO)*
- Gersony's overall finding was that both sides gravely violated the protection that refugees in United Nations camps were owed. *(report: Gersony)*

### 22. The SNM's other abuses
*Map: Core · 1987 to 1989*
- Gersony found that in the first three months of the offensive, SNM fighters killed unarmed civilians in incidents that together may have caused at least several hundred deaths. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- The victims, he wrote, included people fleeing who resisted looting, and "men sought out and killed apparently only because they were not Issaks". In one region, prisoners of war were reported executed after questioning. *(report: Gersony)*
- His directly recorded toll for the SNM, outside the camps, was seventy-six deaths: five at checkpoints, twenty-one near battle areas and fifty in Burao. *(report: Gersony)*
- Africa Watch says that once the government began arming non-Isaaq civilians, young men who had newly joined the SNM killed some unarmed non-Isaaq civilians. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Refugees remembered Hassan Abole, a teacher at Hargeisa Technical Institute, and a teacher of Koranic studies, both shot while fleeing. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch found no evidence that the SNM "as an organization had a policy of deliberately targeting civilians" because they were not Isaaq. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Isaaq refugees told Africa Watch they were angry at the SNM's stern warnings not to take revenge on civilians. But the SNM treated armed non-Isaaq civilians as part of the government's forces. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- The SNM also shot Isaaq it suspected of spying for the government: two nomads near Geed-Debleh in 1987, businessmen in Hargeisa, and in November 1988, at Habaas Weyne, Isaaq women married to soldiers. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- The State Department's report for 1989 said SNM fighters around Erigavo in March 1989 "killed a number of unresisting civilians". It judged that the SNM committed abuses "to a lesser extent" than the government. *(report: State Department)*

### 23. The other northern clans
*Map: North · 1988 to 1991*
- The Gadabuursi, the Dhulbahante, the Warsangeli and the Issa, a scholar writes, "had sided, as a rule, with the Barre government", and fought for it until its fall. *(established; scholar's view)*
- The sources give different reasons for different groups. Some had kinship ties to the regime. Others feared domination by the SNM. Some were armed by the state. *(established; scholar's view)*
- A witness told Africa Watch that the government told the Darood population that "the Isaaks planned to take over the whole country" and "destroy the Darood". *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- When the war broke out, the government gave transport to non-Isaaq residents. But it could not move everyone. Africa Watch says thousands of non-Isaaq fled the bombing and shelling alongside the Isaaq. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony noted that once the non-Isaaq had left the big towns, the main concentrations of non-Isaaq people in the region were the refugee camps. *(report: Gersony)*
- He also recorded acts of courage. Witnesses told him of non-Isaaq civilians who saved the lives of Isaaq civilians, and of Isaaq civilians who saved the lives of non-Isaaq. *(report: Gersony)*

## Part 7: The long war, 1988 to 1991

### 24. War in the countryside
*Map: North · June 1988 to 1989*
- After the summer of 1988, the war became a rural insurgency, a war of raids in the countryside. *(established)*
- From June to September 1988, the army and armed refugees raided villages between Hargeisa and Berbera. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In July and November 1988, villages in the eastern regions were attacked, around El Afweyn and Kalsheikh. Witnesses say troops based at the El Afweyn secondary school killed about fifty people in the first attack. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- An Australian aid agency, Community Aid Abroad, reported that after a mine killed two soldiers in El Afweyn district, the military commander of Sanaag ordered two hundred nomads killed. It said a hundred and three were killed the next day. *(report: Africa Watch, quoting Community Aid Abroad)*
- Gersony recorded rapes of Isaaq women by soldiers, most of them before May 1988. Africa Watch describes soldiers raping women in front of their families. *(report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Africa Watch says mines were laid widely, on roads, at reservoirs and around army camps. It adds that it could not investigate the mines itself. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Human Rights Watch's report for 1989 adds "the poisoning of wells" to the means used against the population. *(report: Human Rights Watch)*
- Africa Watch raised the possibility that chemical weapons were used. The State Department rejected the concern. No finding of chemical weapons exists. *(disputed)*
- Where it lacked troops, the army relied on armed refugees and clan militias. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 25. Erigavo and Sanaag
*Map: North · 1988 to March 1989*
- In Erigavo, in the region of Sanaag, Isaaq men were detained from late May 1988, and non-Isaaq men were armed. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- On the twelfth of January 1989, Community Aid Abroad withdrew from Sanaag. Its statement said the military occupation of Erigavo had driven most of the town's people into the bush, and described a "scorched earth" policy in the villages of the El Afweyn plains. *(report: Africa Watch, quoting Community Aid Abroad)*
- Its spokesman, Peter Kieseker, said of the government's policies there: "Genocide is the only word for it." A British newspaper, the Independent, quoted him on the thirteenth of February 1989. *(report: Africa Watch, quoting the Independent)*
- This is the earliest public use of the word for these events that our research found. *(established)*
- In March 1989, the SNM briefly entered Erigavo. A Canadian government compilation gives a three-hour occupation on the sixteenth of March; an Africa Watch witness says the end of March. *(disputed)*
- Clan militias then carried out reprisals against Isaaq civilians. *(witness: Africa Watch)*
- The State Department reported that SNM fighters around Erigavo that month killed unresisting civilians. *(report: State Department)*

### 26. The world takes notice
*Map: North · August 1988 to August 1990*
- In August 1988, a State Department mission led by Kenneth Bleakley confirmed reports of the bombing. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- On the fourth of January 1989, an American assistant secretary of state, Chester Crocker, rebuked Africa Watch over its concern about chemical weapons. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- In January 1989, the government set up a "Reconciliation Committee". It visited only Berbera and Burao, and never published findings. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The same month, Prime Minister Mohamed Ali Samatar offered an "amnesty" in London. On the twelfth of March 1989, a hundred and twenty-five Isaaq detainees were released from Laanta Bur prison, near Mogadishu. *(government; witness: Africa Watch)*
- The government refused Africa Watch access to the north, despite a promise from the Prime Minister. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- Gersony's report was released in August 1989, at the request of Congress. *(established)*
- The killings did not stop. Africa Watch recorded forty-seven Isaaq men shot at Jezira Beach, near Mogadishu, on the sixteenth of July 1989. *(report: Africa Watch)*
- It recorded twelve young men executed at Hahi, near Odweine, on Christmas Day 1989, and seventeen Isaaq men shot in central Berbera on the sixteenth of August 1990. *(report: Africa Watch)*

### 27. The north is taken
*Map: North · October 1990 to 18 May 1991*
- By October 1990, the SNM controlled almost all of the north-west outside the besieged army garrisons. *(established)*
- At the end of January 1991, it captured the garrisons of Hargeisa, Berbera and Burao. The day each town fell is not recorded in our sources. *(established)*
- At the same time, on the twenty-sixth or twenty-seventh of January, Siad Barre fled Mogadishu. His government had fallen. *(established)*
- The SNM then pushed west into Gadabuursi country. It took Dila, then Borama, in early February. *(established)*
- A Canadian government compilation of 1991 press and aid reports records "ethnic cleansing" by the SNM in the Borama region, and the partial destruction of Dila. *(report: Canadian Immigration and Refugee Board compilation)*
- A Gadabuursi community outlet speaks of up to a thousand dead at Borama on the fourth of February 1991. No primary source for this figure was found. *(estimate; disputed)*
- Pro-Somaliland accounts describe Dila as a battle, in which Gadabuursi militias armed by Barre fought. *(snm)*
- In February 1991, clan delegates met at Berbera: Issa, Gadabuursi, Dhulbahante, Warsangeli and Isaaq. A larger conference of the northern clans opened at Burao on the twenty-seventh of April. *(established)*
- On the eighteenth of May 1991, the SNM's central committee, backed by elders of the major northern clans, declared the Republic of Somaliland, within the borders of the former British protectorate. *(established)*
- The SNM chairman, Abdirahman Ahmed Ali Tuur, announced it. A Dhulbahante leader, Garad Abdiqani Garad Jama, was among those who signed. *(established)*
- Peace was not complete. There was fighting in 1992, and again from 1994 to 1996. Most Dhulbahante opposed separation from Somalia. *(established)*
- In 1993, a conference at Borama chose Mohamed Haji Ibrahim Egal as president, with a Gadabuursi vice-president, Abdirahman Aw Ali Farah, in a power-sharing settlement. *(established)*

## Part 8: After the war

### 28. Graves and investigators
*Map: Hargeisa · 1997 to 2012*
- In May 1997, rain uncovered a grave in Hargeisa. The United Nations' independent expert on human rights in Somalia reported that it held at least two hundred and fifty bodies. *(report: UN expert)*
- That year a technical committee to investigate war crimes was set up in Hargeisa, with a budget of eight thousand American dollars a year. *(report: UN expert)*
- From the seventeenth to the twenty-first of December 1997, forensic scientists from Physicians for Human Rights visited Hargeisa for the United Nations. *(report: Physicians for Human Rights)*
- They observed ninety-two to a hundred and sixteen alleged graves at three sites: the Milk Factory, Malko Durduro Elementary School and Badhka. *(report: Physicians for Human Rights)*
- They test-excavated two graves. These held at least six people. Three had been tied together with one rope. The team concluded that "human rights violations were committed against these individuals". *(report: Physicians for Human Rights)*
- A truck driver told the United Nations expert that he had buried a hundred and fifty to a hundred and eighty bodies a day for fifty-eight days in 1988. This is one person's testimony. *(witness: UN expert)*
- From 2012, a Peruvian forensic team worked with Somaliland on exhumations. Reports of the results differ: eleven graves and three hundred and eighteen skeletons, or about two dozen graves and about two hundred and fifty bodies. *(estimate)*
- More than two hundred mass graves are believed to exist. *(estimate)*
- The graves confirm the pattern of executions that witnesses described. They cannot give a total of the dead. *(established)*
- In 2001, the United Nations' independent expert asked General Morgan about responsibility for deaths in Hargeisa. The expert recorded that he said people had a right to defend their country against perceived foreign intervention. *(government; report: UN expert)*
- He also "alleged that no civilians had been killed", and that specific graves could not be linked to specific battles. *(government; report: UN expert)*
- The United Nations expert rejected, as lacking credibility, a claim that the dead in Hargeisa were refugees killed by the SNM. *(report: UN expert)*
- General Morgan was never tried. He died in Nairobi in late May 2025. *(established)*

### 29. Is it genocide?
*Map: North · 1989 to 2025*
- Genocide is defined in the United Nations convention of 1948. It means certain acts, such as killing members of a group, committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such". *(established)*
- The key is intent: the purpose of destroying the group itself. *(established)*
- Clans are not named in the convention. Lawyers would have to decide whether a Somali clan-family counts as a protected group. Some argue that a lasting, stable group can qualify. Others point to how the perpetrators themselves defined their victims. These approaches are debated. *(disputed)*
- No court, and no legal study we found, has decided this question for the Isaaq. *(established)*
- In 1989, Gersony wrote that victims were selected "principally because of their ethnic identity", and that the Berbera men died "solely because they were Issaks". He did not use the word genocide. *(report: Gersony)*
- In its 1990 report, Africa Watch used the word only once, when quoting the aid worker Peter Kieseker. Its own words were "a government at war with its own people". *(report: Africa Watch)*
- The United Nations' independent expert wrote in 1998 and 1999 that mass killings would be crimes against humanity, and that they "constitute war crimes and crimes against humanity". None of the experts' reports from 1997 to 2005 calls the killings of the Isaaq genocide. *(report: UN expert)*
- In March 2002, a study commissioned jointly by United Nations offices in Somalia and the United Nations human-rights office was completed. It was written by a consultant, Chris Mburu. *(report: 2002 consultant's study)*
- As quoted by others, the consultant wrote that he "firmly believes that the crime of genocide was conceived, planned and perpetrated by the Somali Government against the Isaaq people of northern Somalia between 1987 and 1989". *(report: 2002 consultant's study)*
- That is an expert's view in a study written for the United Nations. It is not a finding by a United Nations body. The United Nations has not declared a genocide. *(established)*
- Scholars have since written about the Isaaq case as genocide, among them Mohamed Haji Ingiriis in 2016. Yale University's Genocide Studies Program lists it as a case. *(established; scholars' view)*
- The Lemkin Institute, an organisation that campaigns against genocide, called it genocide in 2022. It links recognition of the genocide to recognition of Somaliland. *(established; advocacy group's view)*
- Somaliland's government recognises the events as genocide, and hopes the evidence will win international recognition. *(snm)*
- Some writers describe the violence instead as a counter-insurgency that turned into mass atrocity. One historian, writing on clan violence in 1991, prefers the term "clan cleansing" for Somali clan violence. *(established; their view)*
- No scholar we found argues outright that it was not genocide. *(established)*
- No court has ruled on genocide, and none has been asked to. The two American civil cases, in the next chapter, were about torture, killings and detention. *(established)*

### 30. In American courts
*Map: Core · 1987 to 2019*
- Two civil cases were brought in the United States by survivors. Civil cases seek damages for harm. They are not criminal trials. *(established)*
- The first was against Mohamed Ali Samatar, also written Samantar. He was defence minister from 1980 to December 1986, and prime minister from January 1987 to September 1990. *(established)*
- The case raised questions of immunity first. In 2012 an appeals court ruled that a former official has no immunity for the gravest breaches of international law, even for acts done in office. It listed the acts in the case as "torture, summary execution and prolonged arbitrary imprisonment". *(court: ruling)*
- On the twenty-third of February 2012, Samatar chose not to contest liability. In court he said this "doesn't mean that... I'm guilty". *(court: conceded liability by default)*
- Because he did not contest the case, the court accepted the plaintiffs' facts as true. On that record, Judge Leonie Brinkema found in August 2012 that he had ordered and permitted the abuses, and awarded twenty-one million dollars. *(court: ruling on a default record)*
- The claims were killing, attempted killing, torture, cruel treatment, arbitrary detention, crimes against humanity and war crimes. No claim of genocide was made, and the word does not appear in the judge's opinion. *(court: ruling)*
- Samatar died in 2016, aged eighty-five. *(established)*
- The second case was brought by Farhan Warfaa against Colonel Yusuf Abdi Ali, known as Tukeh. From May 1987 to July 1988 he commanded the army's Fifth Brigade at Gebiley. *(established)*
- On the twenty-first of May 2019, a jury found the colonel liable for torturing Farhan Warfaa, and awarded five hundred thousand dollars. It found him not liable for attempted killing. *(court: jury verdict)*
- His lawyer said there was "virtually no evidence" beyond the plaintiff's own testimony, and called the verdict clan vengeance. *(established; defence lawyer's statement)*
- The war-crimes claims in that case had been dismissed earlier, because the events took place outside the United States. No genocide claim was made. *(court: ruling)*
- No criminal court, in any country, and no international court, has tried anyone for these events. *(established)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- In 1988, after SNM attacks on Burao and Hargeisa, the army shelled and bombed both cities, killed civilians in towns that saw no fighting, and attacked people as they fled. *(established)*
- Gersony, for the State Department, and Africa Watch, using different witnesses, found the same pattern and named the same places. *(established)*
- The victims were overwhelmingly Isaaq civilians. Gersony found they were chosen "principally because of their ethnic identity". *(report: Gersony)*
- The SNM also killed unarmed non-Isaaq civilians, prisoners and refugees from Ethiopia, on a much smaller scale. Africa Watch found no SNM policy of targeting civilians for their clan. Gersony found that SNM commanders must at least have condoned the Burao executions. *(report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Refugees from Ethiopia were both forced into the government's service and killed in the SNM's attacks on their camps. *(report: Gersony)*

### The numbers, side by side (most are estimates)
- At least five thousand unarmed Isaaq civilians purposefully murdered by the army, May 1988 to March 1989, not counting crossfire and bombing of battle zones: Gersony's cautious lowest figure. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- At least five hundred Isaaq men killed in Berbera, perhaps many more: Gersony, and separately Africa Watch. *(estimate; report: Gersony; report: Africa Watch)*
- Civilian deaths in the crossfire in Hargeisa and Burao "may have ascended into the thousands": Gersony. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- Fifty to sixty thousand killed by government forces, or dead from bombing, shelling, wounds and disease, over nineteen months to January 1990: Africa Watch. It gave no method, and said the true figure was probably higher. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch)*
- Fifty thousand to two hundred and fifty thousand Isaaq deaths, 1987 to 1989: the Lemkin Institute, without a stated method. *(estimate; advocacy group's view)*
- Two hundred thousand victims: Somaliland's government and campaigners, without a stated method. *(estimate; snm)*
- Killed by the SNM: at least several hundred civilians in the first three months, and at least four hundred refugees in the camp attacks: Gersony. *(estimate; report: Gersony)*
- Displaced: nearly half a million abroad and close to four hundred thousand inside Somalia, by Africa Watch's count of January 1990. *(estimate; report: Africa Watch)*
- These figures count different things, over different periods. They cannot be added together. *(established)*

### What the courts did and did not decide
- No court has ruled on genocide. *(established)*
- Samatar did not contest civil liability, and a court accepted the plaintiffs' facts on that default. The judgment was for torture, killings, detention, crimes against humanity and war crimes. *(court: conceded liability by default)*
- A jury found Colonel Yusuf Abdi Ali liable for the torture of one man, and not liable for attempted killing. *(court: jury verdict)*
- No one has been prosecuted in a criminal court for these events. *(established)*

### The word genocide
- The word was first used for these events, as far as our research found, by an aid worker in 1989, quoted in a newspaper. *(established)*
- In 2002, a consultant's study for the United Nations stated his firm belief that it was genocide. The United Nations itself has made no such finding. *(report: 2002 consultant's study)*
- The United Nations' own experts called the killings war crimes and crimes against humanity. *(report: UN expert)*
- Scholars, a campaigning institute and Somaliland's government call it genocide today. *(established; their view)*
- Whether a Somali clan-family is a protected group under the genocide convention has never been put before a judge. *(established)*

### Memory and aftermath
- Refugees began going home from Hartisheik in 1997. By the time the camp closed in 2004, the United Nations refugee agency counted two hundred and thirty thousand people returned. *(established)*
- Mines remained a danger. Since 1999, a mine-clearing organisation, the HALO Trust, has cleared about eighteen thousand hectares and destroyed about a hundred thousand mines and explosive items, by its 2012 count. *(estimate)*
- In Hargeisa, a Somali air force jet stands on a monument in Freedom Square, in memory of the bombing. The thirty-first of May is remembered there. *(snm)*
- Some Dhulbahante writers say Somaliland's national memory centres on the Isaaq and on the SNM, and leaves out their own losses. Gadabuursi communities remember Borama and Dila in 1991. *(established; their view)*
- Somaliland has governed itself since 1991. Our research records that Israel recognised Somaliland on the twenty-sixth of December 2025. Our research found no country that has formally recognised the genocide. *(established)*

### Still unknown
- How many people died in all. *(established)*
- The exact days the army regained Hargeisa and Burao, and the days the SNM took each town in 1991. *(established)*
- Who commanded the SNM's forces in the field in 1988, and how many fighters it had. *(established)*
- How many died at Borama and Dila in 1991. *(established)*
- Most of the graves have never been opened. *(estimate)*
