# Alexander’s first battles: Granicus and Issus (334–333 BC)
## Battles of History — research dossier, round 1

**Editorial status:** working research, not a recording script. Ancient references are chapter/section locators; ranges are intentional where a whole episode is involved. Modern topographic coordinates below are approximate production proposals, not surveyed archaeological positions. No exact battle day is established here.

## A. Story in chronological order

### 1. Philip’s unfinished war — 336 BC and earlier
**Map:** Macedon, Corinth, western Anatolia. **People:** Philip II, Alexander, Parmenion, Attalus, Darius III.
- Philip defeated the Greek coalition at Chaeronea in 338 BC and organized a common Greek alliance under Macedonian leadership. [DIODORUS 16.85–89]
- The campaign against Persia was presented as retaliation for earlier Persian attacks on Greece; this was a political justification, not an objective obligation shared by every Greek. [DIODORUS 16.89.2–3; INFERENCE]
- Philip dispatched Parmenion and Attalus with an advance force to Asia before the main expedition. [DIODORUS 16.91.2]
- Philip was assassinated by Pausanias in 336 BC; the ultimate motives and possible accomplices remain debated. [DIODORUS 16.93–94; PLUTARCH Alex. 10; DISPUTED]
- Alexander succeeded him; Darius III ruled the Achaemenid Empire, which extended across many peoples and regions. [DIODORUS 17.2–5; MODERN: Briant 2002, reference work]

### 2. Securing the rear — 335 BC
**Map:** Balkans, Thebes, Corinth. **People:** Alexander, Antipater.
- Alexander campaigned north of Macedon against several groups, including the Triballians. [ARRIAN 1.1–6]
- A revolt at Thebes followed reports that Alexander was dead. [ARRIAN 1.7]
- Alexander returned rapidly; Thebes was captured and destroyed, and many inhabitants were enslaved. [ARRIAN 1.8–9; DIODORUS 17.11–14]
- Athens was spared destruction. [ARRIAN 1.10]
- Antipater remained in Europe when Alexander departed. [ARRIAN 1.11.3]

### 3. Crossing into Asia — spring 334 BC
**Map:** Sestos, Abydos, Troy. **People:** Alexander, Parmenion.
- Parmenion supervised the main crossing of the Hellespont from Sestos to Abydos. [ARRIAN 1.11.5–6]
- Alexander crossed separately toward the Trojan coast. [ARRIAN 1.11.6–7]
- Diodorus describes Alexander casting a spear into Asian soil as a symbolic claim. [DIODORUS 17.17.2; literary tradition]
- At Troy he honoured Achilles and visited the sanctuary of Athena; the account emphasizes his association with heroic tradition. [ARRIAN 1.11.7–1.12.1; PLUTARCH Alex. 15]
- The expedition numbered roughly tens of thousands, not a single securely known total; see strength table below. [ARRIAN 1.11.3; DIODORUS 17.17.3–5; ESTIMATE]

### 4. The Persian council — before Granicus
**Map:** Zeleia, northwestern Anatolia. **People:** Memnon, Arsites, Persian satraps.
- Persian regional commanders assembled at Zeleia. [ARRIAN 1.12.8]
- Memnon of Rhodes, a Greek commander serving Persia, proposed avoiding battle and destroying supplies that Alexander could use. [ARRIAN 1.12.9–10; DIODORUS 17.18.2]
- Arsites rejected burning the property of people under his authority. [ARRIAN 1.12.10]
- Arrian attributes suspicion of Memnon’s motives to the Persian commanders; this is the historian’s explanation, not independently recorded Persian deliberation. [ARRIAN 1.12.10]
- A decision to fight near the river followed. The defence of local resources and the use of a river obstacle are intelligible military and political choices. [ARRIAN 1.13; INFERENCE]

### 5. The Granicus: two incompatible openings — May 334 BC (conventional month)
**Map:** Biga Çayı valley. **People:** Alexander, Parmenion, Amyntas son of Arrhabaeus, Socrates’ squadron.
- Persian cavalry stood along the opposite bank, with Greek mercenary infantry behind, in Arrian’s account. [ARRIAN 1.14.4]
- Parmenion advised delaying the crossing until morning; Alexander rejected the advice. [ARRIAN 1.13.3–7; PLUTARCH Alex. 16]
- Diodorus instead describes a dawn crossing before the main engagement. Do not animate this as a second crossing following Arrian’s afternoon attack. [DIODORUS 17.19.1–3; DISPUTED]
- In Arrian’s version the first assault included Amyntas’ mounted troops and Socrates’ Companion squadron, then led by Ptolemy son of Philip. [ARRIAN 1.14.6–7; 1.15.1]
- The first attackers met fierce resistance at the bank; Alexander led the main right-wing attack. [ARRIAN 1.15.1–3]

### 6. Granicus: cavalry battle
**Map:** river and eastern/opposite bank (orientation provisional). **People:** Alexander, Cleitus, Mithridates, Rhoesaces, Spithridates.
- Arrian describes close combat among mounted men at the river edge. [ARRIAN 1.15.3–5]
- Alexander struck Mithridates, identified as Darius’ son-in-law. [ARRIAN 1.15.7]
- Rhoesaces struck Alexander’s helmet; Cleitus intervened against Spithridates. [ARRIAN 1.15.7–8]
- Plutarch and Diodorus differ in the sequence and attribution of blows; avoid merging them into a precise choreography. [PLUTARCH Alex. 16; DIODORUS 17.20; DISPUTED]
- Persian cavalry eventually withdrew, leaving the infantry exposed. [ARRIAN 1.16.1–2]

### 7. Granicus: mercenaries and aftermath
**Map:** rear of Persian line, battlefield. **People:** Greek mercenaries, Alexander, Arsites.
- Alexander directed infantry and cavalry against the remaining Greek mercenaries. [ARRIAN 1.16.2]
- Arrian says most were killed and about 2,000 taken prisoner; the original total and exact deaths are uncertain. [ARRIAN 1.16.2; ESTIMATE]
- Captives were sent to Macedonia for forced labour; Arrian explains this through the alliance’s prohibition on serving Persia. [ARRIAN 1.16.6]
- Persian leaders died, including Mithrobuzanes and Spithridates; Arsites escaped and later killed himself according to Arrian. [ARRIAN 1.16.3]
- Alexander sent 300 Persian suits of armour to Athens as a dedication, excluding Sparta in the inscription. [ARRIAN 1.16.7; PLUTARCH Alex. 16]
- The victory opened western Anatolia to further campaigning; it did not destroy the Persian Empire. [ARRIAN 1.17–20; INFERENCE]

### 8. Sardis and Ionia — summer 334 BC
**Map:** Sardis, Ephesus. **People:** Mithrenes, Alexander.
- Mithrenes surrendered Sardis and its citadel. [ARRIAN 1.17.3–4]
- Alexander appointed officers to administer the region. [ARRIAN 1.17.7–8]
- At Ephesus he restored exiles and replaced oligarchic government with democracy. [ARRIAN 1.17.10–12]
- Arrian reports that Alexander stopped further killings of political opponents there. [ARRIAN 1.17.12]
- Greek cities were not politically uniform; Persian service and Macedonian alignment both had local supporters. [ARRIAN 1.17–18; INFERENCE]

### 9. Miletus and the fleet — summer 334 BC
**Map:** Miletus, offshore islands. **People:** Nicanor, Alexander, Persian fleet.
- Miletus resisted; the Macedonian fleet reached the harbour approaches before the Persian fleet could occupy them. [ARRIAN 1.18.3–6]
- Alexander rejected Parmenion’s proposal for a naval battle. [ARRIAN 1.18.6–9]
- Siege engines breached the defences; the city fell. [ARRIAN 1.19]
- Alexander dismissed most of his fleet, citing expense and the strategy of taking coastal bases in Arrian’s explanation. [ARRIAN 1.20.1; DIODORUS 17.23.1–3]
- This was not the permanent end of Macedonian naval operations. [ARRIAN 2.2; INFERENCE]

### 10. Halicarnassus and Ada — autumn 334 BC
**Map:** Bodrum, Alinda. **People:** Memnon, Orontobates, Ada.
- Memnon and Orontobates organized resistance at Halicarnassus. [ARRIAN 1.20–23; DIODORUS 17.24–27]
- The siege involved walls, engines and sorties; defenders inflicted losses. [ARRIAN 1.21–22]
- The defenders abandoned much of the city after setting fires, retaining fortified positions. [ARRIAN 1.23]
- Alexander left troops to continue operations rather than claiming every stronghold immediately. [ARRIAN 1.23.6]
- Ada, a displaced Carian dynast, offered Alexander Alinda and adopted him; he entrusted Caria to her. [ARRIAN 1.23.7–8; PLUTARCH Alex. 22]

### 11. Winter march — 334–333 BC
**Map:** Lycia, Pamphylia, Phrygia. **People:** Alexander, Parmenion.
- Alexander sent newly married soldiers home for winter leave. [ARRIAN 1.24.1–2]
- He marched through Lycia, receiving submissions from cities. [ARRIAN 1.24.4–5]
- He continued through Pamphylia, including the coastal passage near Mount Climax. [ARRIAN 1.26; PLUTARCH Alex. 17]
- Accounts attach miraculous significance to the sea passage; do not present supernatural intervention as fact. [PLUTARCH Alex. 17; ARRIAN 1.26.2]
- The army reunited at Gordium. [ARRIAN 1.29.3–4]

### 12. Gordium and the Persian naval response — spring 333 BC
**Map:** Gordium, Chios, Lesbos. **People:** Alexander, Memnon, Pharnabazus.
- At Gordium Alexander confronted a famous knot associated with a prediction of rule over Asia. [ARRIAN 2.3.1–6]
- One tradition has him cut it with his sword; Aristobulus’ version has him remove a pin securing the yoke. [ARRIAN 2.3.7–8; PLUTARCH Alex. 18; DISPUTED]
- Memnon took Chios and secured most of Lesbos before besieging Mytilene. [ARRIAN 2.1.1–2]
- He died of illness during that operation; Persian naval activity continued under other commanders. [ARRIAN 2.1.2–3; DIODORUS 17.29]
- The Persian fleet threatened Alexander’s communications and shows that the war was not simply a sequence of land battles. [ARRIAN 2.1–2; INFERENCE]

### 13. Into Cilicia — summer/autumn 333 BC
**Map:** Ancyra, Cilician Gates, Tarsus. **People:** Alexander, Arsames.
- Alexander moved through Ancyra and Cappadocia toward Cilicia. [ARRIAN 2.4.1–2]
- He secured the Cilician Gates, a narrow mountain passage. [ARRIAN 2.4.2–4]
- Arsames withdrew from Tarsus; Alexander hurried to secure the city. [ARRIAN 2.4.5–6]
- Alexander became seriously ill at Tarsus; Aristobulus blamed exhaustion, while other accounts connect it to bathing in the cold Cydnus. [ARRIAN 2.4.7; PLUTARCH Alex. 19; DISPUTED]
- Philip of Acarnania treated him; the story of a warning letter accusing the doctor is a literary anecdote. [ARRIAN 2.4.8–11; CURTIUS 3.5–6]

### 14. Darius prepares — before Issus
**Map:** Babylon to Sochoi, east of Amanus. **People:** Darius, Amyntas son of Antiochus, Thymondas.
- Darius assembled a royal army and moved west. [DIODORUS 17.30–31; CURTIUS 3.2–3]
- Persian Greek mercenaries were part of this army, including troops brought from the naval theatre. [ARRIAN 2.2.1–2; 2.8.6]
- Darius waited at Sochoi on open terrain. [ARRIAN 2.6.1]
- Amyntas, a Macedonian exile in Persian service, advised remaining on the plain according to Arrian. [ARRIAN 2.6.3–5]
- The Persian king chose to move; ancient claims about flattering courtiers are not independently established motives. [ARRIAN 2.6.2–6; DISPUTED]

### 15. Armies pass one another — autumn 333 BC
**Map:** Soli, Mallus, Issus, Myriandrus, Amanus passes. **People:** Darius, Alexander.
- Alexander campaigned around Soli and Mallus before advancing along the coast. [ARRIAN 2.5]
- He passed through Issus and moved toward Myriandrus. [ARRIAN 2.6.1–2; 2.7.1]
- Darius crossed the Amanus by another passage and arrived at Issus behind Alexander. [ARRIAN 2.7.1; POLYBIUS 12.17.2–3]
- Arrian reports that Darius’ troops killed the sick Macedonians left there; Curtius describes mutilation as well. Keep the versions separate. [ARRIAN 2.7.1; CURTIUS 3.8.14]
- Alexander confirmed the Persian presence by sending men by sea and turned north. [ARRIAN 2.7.2–3]
- Darius had placed himself across Alexander’s communications, but the coastal terrain limited deployment. [ARRIAN 2.7–8; INFERENCE]

### 16. Issus: deployment — November 333 BC (conventional month)
**Map:** Pinarus candidate river, Gulf of Issus, Amanus foothills. **People:** Darius, Alexander, Parmenion, Craterus.
- The armies faced one another across the Pinarus in Arrian’s account. [ARRIAN 2.8]
- Persian cavalry was concentrated toward the sea; Greek mercenaries stood in the centre, with troops called Cardaces beside them. [ARRIAN 2.8.5–9]
- The exact equipment and identity of the Cardaces are debated; do not give them a uniform Greek hoplite appearance as established fact. [ARRIAN 2.8.6; DISPUTED]
- Alexander placed Companion cavalry and associated troops on his right, phalanx infantry centrally, and Parmenion’s command on the left. [ARRIAN 2.8.3–4; 2.9.1–3]
- Thessalian cavalry was transferred to the left to counter Persian cavalry. [ARRIAN 2.9.1]
- Persian troops on the foothills threatened Alexander’s flank; he assigned troops to contain them. [ARRIAN 2.9.2–4]

### 17. Issus: attack and hard fighting
**Map:** river crossing, centre and coastal wing. **People:** Alexander, Darius, Thessalians, Greek mercenaries.
- Alexander’s right wing advanced and charged across the river. [ARRIAN 2.10.1–3]
- The Macedonian centre lost alignment on difficult ground and faced strong resistance from the Greek mercenaries. [ARRIAN 2.10.4–6]
- Persian cavalry fought hard against the Thessalians on the coastal wing. [ARRIAN 2.11.2]
- Darius withdrew when his position became threatened; Arrian describes flight first in a chariot, then on horseback. [ARRIAN 2.11.4–5]
- Diodorus gives a dramatic account of combat near the royal chariot; do not depict a verified personal duel between the kings. [DIODORUS 17.34; DISPUTED]
- Alexander was wounded in the thigh according to Plutarch, who cites Chares and Alexander’s letter. [PLUTARCH Alex. 20.8–9]

### 18. Issus: retreat and captives
**Map:** northern escape routes, Persian camp. **People:** Darius, Sisygambis, Stateira, Alexander.
- Persian forces retreated; pursuit and crowding contributed to casualties according to the ancient narratives. [ARRIAN 2.11.3–8; DIODORUS 17.35]
- Darius escaped; the royal camp and family fell into Macedonian hands. [ARRIAN 2.11.9–2.12.1]
- His mother, wife, daughters and young son were among the captives. [ARRIAN 2.12.1; CURTIUS 3.11–12]
- Alexander promised protection and continued royal status according to the Greek and Roman literary accounts; they remained captives. [ARRIAN 2.12.3–5; DIODORUS 17.37–38]
- The story of Sisygambis mistaking Hephaestion for Alexander appears in Diodorus and Curtius; Arrian reports it as an alternative tradition and expresses uncertainty. [DIODORUS 17.37.5–6; CURTIUS 3.12.15–17; ARRIAN 2.12.6–8]

### 19. Damascus, survivors and diplomacy — late 333 BC
**Map:** Damascus, escape routes toward Syria and Egypt. **People:** Parmenion, Darius, Amyntas.
- Parmenion seized the Persian baggage and wealth at Damascus. [ARRIAN 2.11.10; CURTIUS 3.13]
- Greek mercenaries escaped with Amyntas and other commanders; Arrian describes a group of about 8,000. [ARRIAN 2.13.2–3; ESTIMATE]
- Darius sought the return of his family and proposed an agreement in the letter reported by Arrian. [ARRIAN 2.14.1–3]
- Alexander’s reported answer demanded recognition of his claim to rule Asia. [ARRIAN 2.14.4–9]
- These are literary versions of correspondence, not surviving authenticated originals; later offers must not be inserted into this first exchange. [DIODORUS 17.39; CURTIUS 4.1; DISPUTED]

### 20. Toward Phoenicia — winter 333–332 BC
**Map:** Marathus, Aradus, Byblos, Sidon, Tyre. **People:** Alexander, Phoenician rulers.
- Alexander proceeded south along the coast rather than immediately pursuing Darius into the interior. [ARRIAN 2.13–15]
- Aradus, Byblos and Sidon submitted in the course of the advance. [ARRIAN 2.13.7–8; 2.15.6]
- Coastal control threatened the bases of the Persian fleet. [ARRIAN 1.20.1; 2.17.1–4; INFERENCE]
- Tyrian envoys met Alexander, but the dispute over entry to the island city led to siege. [ARRIAN 2.15.6–2.16]
- Handover: Darius remained at large and the Persian Empire remained capable of raising another army. [ARRIAN 3.8; INFERENCE]

### Granicus
- Modern Biga Çayı / Kocabaş Çayı identification is conventional; exact battlefield remains a research question. [ARRIAN 1.13–16; MODERN: Körpe research reported 2024, S7]
- Körpe’s team reported a location farther south than earlier proposals near Çınarköprü, arguing the earlier lowland was marshy in antiquity. This is a reported research finding, not independently verified excavation proof. [S7; DISPUTED]
- Arrian describes difficult banks and a crossing under attack; Diodorus describes a crossing before battle. No measured ancient depth or width can be recovered from these passages. [ARRIAN 1.13–15; DIODORUS 17.19]
- **Provisional 6 × 4.5 km scouting frame**, centred on modern Biga (not approved battlefield): NW 40.2502, 27.2047; SE 40.2098, 27.2753. [INFERENCE; calculation using local degree lengths]
- Do not commission final battle art until the proposed Körpe site is georeferenced from a scholarly map. Modern river meanders, embankments and drainage may differ. Keep water, banks, slopes and troop layers separate.

### Issus
- Candidate rivers include Payas, Deli Çay and Kuru Çay; the naming and correspondence of streams must be checked on specialist maps. Payas is a conventional candidate, not a proven site. [DISPUTED; S8–S10 reading list]
- Callisthenes, quoted by Polybius, gives at most 14 stades between sea and hills, about 2.6 km using 185 m per stade. Polybius questions the formation arithmetic and bank-crossing account. [POLYBIUS 12.17.4–5; 12.18–22; ESTIMATE]
- **Provisional 10 × 7.5 km Payas scouting frame**, centre 36.75, 36.20: NW 36.7837, 36.1439; SE 36.7163, 36.2561. [INFERENCE] This is not a verified ancient coastline or battlefield polygon.
- Modern industrial installations, steelworks, ports, highways and engineered shorelines must not appear. The ancient shoreline requires geomorphological reconstruction; do not assume the modern shoreline is exact. [INFERENCE]
- Show Persian army facing south, Macedonian army north, sea on Macedonian left and mountains on right for the Arrian-based scenario. [ARRIAN 2.8–10; RECONSTRUCTION]
- Keep Darius’ route via the Amanus separate from Alexander’s route along the coastal corridor. Do not silently equate the Pillar of Jonah, Syrian Gates and Belen pass.

- Veronese, Family of Darius: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:The_Family_of_Darius_before_Alexander — later interpretation.
- Coins: search Commons for Alexander III tetradrachms and Achaemenid darics; royal archer on a daric is not an authenticated portrait of Darius III. Verify photographer license.
- Modern landscape photos: Biga Çayı, Payas and Amanus; use only after exact file and photographer license verification. Modern photographs are reference material, not evidence that ancient terrain was identical.

## E. Open questions and next research pass

1. Granicus timing: Arrian/Plutarch versus Diodorus. Do not harmonize by inventing two attacks. Check Bosworth and Badian alongside fieldwork.
2. Granicus site: obtain Körpe’s actual coordinates and peer-reviewed geomorphological publication, if available. The 2024 press report is not enough for a georeferenced final map.
3. Pinarus: obtain Hammond and Devine’s maps and arguments before choosing a river; check ancient coastal evolution and the nomenclature of each modern stream.
4. Army sizes: use Polybius’ critique and modern logistics; do not invent a replacement precise Persian strength.
5. Persian plan: cutting communications is a plausible inference, not an authenticated statement of Darius’ intention. Greek accounts of cowardice or flattery are literary framing.
6. Persian-side evidence: Achaemenid inscriptions, coins and Babylonian documentation provide imperial context, but no Persian eyewitness narrative of either battle has been established in this pass. Consult Briant and Iranica. Avoid assuming absence of evidence means absence of planning.
7. Ancient source relationships: Arrian wrote in the second century AD, roughly 450 years later, drawing especially on Ptolemy and Aristobulus; Diodorus wrote in the first century BC, Curtius probably first century AD, Plutarch around the turn of the second century AD, Justin’s epitome is later and date debated. Polybius preserves and criticizes the lost contemporary Callisthenes. [ARRIAN preface; S1–S5, S11] Agreement is not necessarily independent corroboration.
8. Art: obtain exact file-level licenses and author attribution for all remaining images; no portraits should be fabricated as archival evidence.
