## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we follow Alexander the Great into Asia, to his first two great battles against the Persian Empire: at the river Granicus in 334 BCE, and at Issus a year and a half later. Alexander was twenty-two years old when he crossed into Asia. His opponent at Issus was Darius the Third, the Great King of Persia, who ruled the largest empire the world had yet seen. Almost everything we know comes from writers who lived centuries later, and most of what they used came from Alexander's side. We will tell you what they say, where they disagree, and what is still not known.

## Introduction

### I1. GRANICUS AND ISSUS
- In the spring of 334 BCE, Alexander, king of Macedon, crossed from Europe into Asia with an army of Macedonians and Greek allies. *(established)*
- Near the river Granicus, the Persian governors of the region met him with their cavalry and with Greek soldiers who served Persia. Alexander won. *(established)*
- In the autumn of 333 BCE, at Issus, on the coast where Anatolia meets Syria, he met the Great King himself, Darius the Third, and won again. Darius escaped. His family was captured. *(established)*
- Neither battle ended the Persian Empire. It took two more years, and another great battle, at Gaugamela. *(established)*

### I2. Two kingdoms
*Map: Campaign · 336 BCE*
- Macedon was a kingdom in the north of Greece. Alexander's father, Philip the Second, made it the strongest power in the Greek world. *(established)*
- The Persian Empire, ruled by the Achaemenid kings, stretched from the Aegean Sea to India and Egypt. It was made of many peoples, governed by provincial governors called satraps. *(established)*
- Its king was called the Great King. In 336 BCE, Darius the Third came to the throne. *(established)*
- Many Greeks lived inside the Persian Empire, in the cities of the Anatolian coast. Many Greek soldiers served the Great King for pay. *(established)*

### I3. Why the war?
*Map: Campaign · 338 to 336 BCE*
- After defeating a Greek coalition at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, Philip formed an alliance of Greek states under Macedonian leadership, and prepared a war against Persia. *(diodorus)*
- The war was presented as revenge for the Persian invasions of Greece more than a century before. That was its public justification. Not every Greek shared it. *(diodorus; scholarship)*
- Philip sent an advance force into Asia under his general Parmenion. Then, in 336 BCE, Philip was murdered. Who else may have been behind it is still debated. *(diodorus; plutarch; disputed)*
- Alexander, aged twenty, became king. *(established)*

### I4. The writers
- Our main source is Arrian, who wrote in Greek about four hundred and fifty years later. He used the lost histories of two men who served with Alexander: Ptolemy and Aristobulus. *(established)*
- We also have Diodorus, Plutarch, Curtius and Justin, and the criticisms of Polybius. They often disagree, about what happened, in what order, and above all about numbers. *(established)*
- Most surviving narratives were written in Greek or Latin after the campaign, and draw on accounts from Alexander's side. No equivalent Persian history of these battles survives. *(established)*
- Earlier does not always mean more accurate, and later does not always mean worthless. *(editorial)*

### I5. The armies
- Alexander's army was built around the phalanx: rows of infantry armed with a pike about five or six metres long, the sarissa. Beside them fought the hypaspists, the king's guard infantry. *(established)*
- His striking force was the cavalry: the Companions, Macedonian nobles, and on the other wing the horsemen of Thessaly. Light troops, like the Agrianian javelin-men, guarded the flanks. *(established)*
- The Persian army was the army of an empire: Persian and Iranian noble cavalry, infantry and archers from many peoples, and many thousands of Greek mercenaries. *(established)*
- Ancient writers give widely different army and casualty figures. The enormous Persian totals cannot be treated as reliable headcounts, and the actual strengths and losses remain uncertain. *(estimate; editorial)*

### I6. How this page tells it
- After each line, a tag tells you who says it. Where the writers disagree, we say so. *(editorial)*
- The maps are paintings made for this page over real elevation data. Where a battlefield's exact place is uncertain, the map says so. *(editorial)*
- The positions and movements of the soldiers are our own reconstruction, following Arrian. *(reconstruction)*

## Part 1: The road to Asia, 335 to 334 BCE

### 1. Thebes
*Map: Campaign · 335 BCE*
- Before leaving for Asia, Alexander campaigned north of Macedon, among the Triballians and other peoples. *(arrian)*
- A false report that he had been killed set off a revolt at Thebes. Alexander marched south at speed. *(arrian)*
- Thebes was taken and destroyed, and many of its people were enslaved. Athens was spared. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- The Greek cities had been warned. Alexander left his general Antipater in charge of Europe. *(arrian)*

### 2. Into Asia
*Map: Campaign · spring 334 BCE*
- In the spring of 334 BCE, Parmenion took the main army across the Hellespont, the narrow strait between Europe and Asia, from Sestos to Abydos. *(arrian)*
- Alexander crossed separately, toward the coast of Troy. Diodorus says he threw a spear into the soil of Asia as he landed, a claim to the land. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- At Troy, Alexander honoured the tomb of Achilles, the hero of the Iliad, and visited the sanctuary of Athena. *(arrian; plutarch)*
- His army numbered some tens of thousands. The ancient writers' counts, on the screen, do not agree exactly. *(estimate)*

### 3. The council at Zeleia
*Map: Campaign · spring 334 BCE*
- The Persian governors of the region gathered their forces at Zeleia. *(arrian)*
- Memnon of Rhodes, a Greek commander in Persian service, advised them not to fight: to burn the crops and farms, so that Alexander's army would run out of food. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- Arsites, the satrap of the region, refused to burn the land of the people he governed. *(arrian)*
- Arrian says the Persian commanders also distrusted Memnon. That is Arrian's explanation; no Persian record of the council survives. They decided to fight at a river. *(arrian; disputed)*

## Part 2: The Granicus, about May 334 BCE

### 4. The river
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- The Persians took up position on the far bank of the river Granicus, today usually identified as the Biga Çayı in north-western Turkey. *(established; disputed)*
- In Arrian's account, the Persian cavalry lined the bank, with the Greek mercenary infantry behind them. *(arrian)*
- Exactly where they stood is uncertain. A survey published in 2025 proposes a crossing place north of the modern town of Biga, as a proposal, not proof. *(scholarship; disputed)*

### 5. Two stories
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- Here the ancient writers tell two different stories, and they cannot both be true. *(disputed)*
- In Arrian and Plutarch, Parmenion advised waiting until morning, and Alexander refused, and attacked across the river at once. *(arrian; plutarch)*
- In Diodorus, the army crossed at dawn, and formed up before the Persians could stop it. *(diodorus)*
- This page follows Arrian, as most modern historians do, among them Badian and Devine. *(scholarship; reconstruction)*

### 6. The first attack
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- In Arrian's account, Alexander sent a first group across ahead of his main charge: horsemen and light infantry, with a squadron of Companions led by Ptolemy, son of Philip. *(arrian)*
- They met fierce resistance at the bank, and were driven back. *(arrian)*
- Then Alexander himself led the right wing into the river. *(arrian)*

### 7. The fight at the bank
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- Arrian describes a fight of horsemen at the water's edge, at close quarters, with lances and swords. *(arrian)*
- Alexander struck down Mithridates, a son-in-law of Darius. A Persian noble, Rhoesaces, struck Alexander's helmet. *(arrian)*
- As Spithridates, a satrap, raised his sword against the king, Alexander's officer Cleitus, called the Black, cut him down. *(arrian)*
- Plutarch and Diodorus tell this fight differently, with other names and other blows. We follow Arrian, and do not combine them. *(disputed)*

### 8. The mercenaries
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- The Persian cavalry gave way, and withdrew. Behind them, the Greek mercenaries still stood, on higher ground. *(arrian)*
- Plutarch says the mercenaries asked for terms, but Alexander attacked them. *(plutarch)*
- Alexander surrounded them with infantry and cavalry. Arrian says most were killed, and about two thousand were taken prisoner. *(arrian; estimate)*
- The prisoners were sent to Macedonia to work the land as forced labourers. Arrian's explanation: the Greek alliance had forbidden Greeks to fight for Persia. *(arrian)*

### 9. After the Granicus
*Map: Granicus · about May 334 BCE*
- Several Persian commanders were killed. Arsites escaped, and Arrian says he later killed himself. *(arrian)*
- Alexander sent three hundred Persian suits of armour to the goddess Athena at Athens, says Arrian; Plutarch speaks of shields. Both say the dedication left out the Spartans, who had not joined the war. *(arrian; plutarch)*
- The ancient figures for the losses, on screen, differ widely. *(estimate; disputed)*
- The victory opened western Anatolia to Alexander. It did not destroy the Persian Empire. *(established)*

## Part 3: A year in Anatolia, 334 to 333 BCE

### 10. Sardis and Ephesus
*Map: Campaign · summer 334 BCE*
- The people of Sardis, the old capital of Lydia, submitted, and its Persian commander, Mithrenes, surrendered the citadel. *(arrian)*
- At Ephesus, Alexander brought back exiles and replaced the ruling few with a democracy. Arrian says he stopped the killing of political opponents there. *(arrian)*
- The Greek cities were divided. Some citizens supported Persia, others Alexander. *(arrian; scholarship)*

### 11. Miletus and the fleet
*Map: Campaign · summer 334 BCE*
- Miletus resisted. Alexander's fleet reached the harbour before the larger Persian fleet could. *(arrian)*
- Parmenion urged a sea battle; Alexander refused. His siege engines broke the walls, and the city fell. *(arrian)*
- Then Alexander sent most of his fleet home. Arrian says it was too costly, and that he planned to defeat the Persian navy by taking its harbours on land. Diodorus gives cost as one reason, and also reports that some said it was to leave his army no way of escape. *(arrian; diodorus)*

### 12. Halicarnassus and Ada
*Map: Campaign · autumn 334 BCE*
- At Halicarnassus, Memnon and the Persian commander Orontobates organised a strong defence, with sorties against the siege engines. *(arrian)*
- The defenders set fires and withdrew into the city's strongholds. Then, according to Arrian, Alexander ordered the city demolished. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- Ada, a princess of the ruling family of Caria who had been pushed out of power, came to Alexander. She adopted him as her son, and he gave her the rule of Caria. *(arrian; plutarch)*

### 13. The winter march
*Map: Campaign · winter 334 to 333 BCE*
- Alexander sent the soldiers who had recently married home to Macedonia for the winter. *(arrian)*
- He marched along the southern coast, through Lycia and Pamphylia, where cities submitted. *(arrian)*
- At Mount Climax the road ran along the shore. Later stories said the sea itself drew back for him. Plutarch already doubted them. *(plutarch; disputed)*
- In the spring, the army gathered again at Gordium, in the heart of Anatolia. *(arrian)*

### 14. The knot and the fleet
*Map: Campaign · spring 333 BCE*
- At Gordium stood an ancient wagon, its yoke tied with a famous knot. A prophecy said that whoever undid it would rule Asia. *(arrian; plutarch)*
- One story says Alexander cut it with his sword. Aristobulus says he pulled out the pin that held the yoke. Arrian says he cannot say for certain how it was done. *(arrian; disputed)*
- Meanwhile, Memnon led the Persian fleet into the Aegean. He took Chios and most of Lesbos, and then died of illness while besieging Mytilene. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- The Persian navy fought on under other commanders. The war was not only a war on land. *(arrian; established)*

### 15. Tarsus
*Map: Campaign · summer 333 BCE*
- Alexander crossed the Taurus mountains by the narrow pass called the Cilician Gates, and hurried to Tarsus before its Persian governor could burn it. *(arrian)*
- At Tarsus he fell dangerously ill. Aristobulus blamed exhaustion; other writers blamed a swim in the icy river Cydnus. *(arrian; disputed)*
- His doctor, Philip of Acarnania, treated him. The writers tell of a letter accusing the doctor of a plot. It is an episode reported by the ancient writers; its details cannot be checked. *(arrian; plutarch; curtius)*

## Part 4: Issus, about November 333 BCE

### 16. The Great King comes
*Map: Campaign · autumn 333 BCE*
- Darius gathered a royal army and marched west. With him were Greek mercenaries, among them troops brought from the fleet. *(arrian; diodorus; curtius)*
- He waited at Sochoi, on open ground east of the Amanus mountains, where his cavalry could fight best. *(arrian)*
- Arrian says a Macedonian exile in his service, Amyntas, advised him to stay there. Darius moved. The ancient writers blame flattering courtiers; that is their explanation, not a Persian record. *(arrian; disputed)*

### 17. The armies pass
*Map: Campaign · autumn 333 BCE*
- Alexander marched along the coast, through Issus, toward Myriandrus. Darius crossed the mountains by another pass, and came down to the sea at Issus, behind him. *(arrian; polybius)*
- Darius was now across Alexander's road home. Arrian says his troops killed the sick Macedonians who had been left at Issus. *(arrian)*
- Alexander sent men by sea to see for themselves. Then he turned his army round, and marched back north. *(arrian)*

### 18. The Pinarus
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- The armies met at a river the writers call the Pinarus, on a narrow plain between the mountains and the sea. *(arrian)*
- Which river it was is uncertain: the Payas, the Deli Çay or the Kuru Çay have all been proposed. This map shows the Payas, as a hypothesis. *(scholarship; disputed)*
- Callisthenes, through Polybius, gives the width of the plain as at most fourteen stades: about two and a half kilometres. *(polybius; estimate)*

### 19. The lines
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- In Arrian's account, Darius placed his best cavalry toward the sea, his Greek mercenaries in the centre, facing the phalanx, with troops called Kardakes beside them. Who the Kardakes were, and how they were armed, is debated. *(arrian; disputed)*
- Darius himself stood in the centre, in his chariot. Troops on the foothills threatened Alexander's right. *(arrian)*
- Alexander put the Companions and the guard on his right, the phalanx in the centre, and Parmenion on the left by the sea. Seeing the Persian cavalry there, he sent the Thessalian horsemen across to the left. *(arrian)*

### 20. The charge
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- Alexander's right wing advanced, then charged across the river. The Persian left gave way. *(arrian)*
- But the phalanx in the centre lost its line on the steep river banks, and the Greek mercenaries fought hard. Arrian names Ptolemy, son of Seleucus, and about a hundred and twenty other Macedonians of distinction killed there. *(arrian)*
- By the sea, the Persian cavalry attacked across the river, and fought the Thessalians hard. *(arrian)*

### 21. Darius leaves the field
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- Alexander's wing turned toward the centre, where Darius stood. *(arrian)*
- Arrian says Darius fled, first in his chariot, then on horseback when the ground grew rough. Diodorus tells of fierce fighting around the royal chariot. A personal duel between the two kings is not established. *(arrian; diodorus; disputed)*
- Plutarch says Alexander was wounded in the thigh. *(plutarch)*
- Some modern historians see Darius's escape as a choice to save his rule and fight on, rather than only flight. That is a modern interpretation. *(scholarship)*

### 22. The rout
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- With the king gone, the Persian army broke. Many died in the pursuit and the crush in the narrow passes. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- The ancient figures for the dead, on screen, are very large and very different. They cannot be treated as counts. *(estimate; disputed)*

## Part 5: After Issus, 333 to 332 BCE

### 23. The royal family
*Map: Issus · about November 333 BCE*
- The Persian camp was taken. Among the captives were Darius's mother, Sisygambis, his wife, Stateira, his daughters and his young son. *(arrian; curtius)*
- The writers say Alexander promised them protection and their royal rank. They remained captives. *(arrian; diodorus)*
- A famous story says Sisygambis bowed first to Alexander's friend Hephaestion, mistaking him for the king. Arrian tells it only as a story he cannot vouch for. *(diodorus; curtius; arrian; disputed)*

### 24. Damascus and the survivors
*Map: Campaign · winter 333 to 332 BCE*
- Parmenion seized the Persian baggage and treasure at Damascus. *(arrian; curtius)*
- About eight thousand Greek mercenaries escaped with their commanders, among them Amyntas and Thymondas, says Arrian. *(arrian; estimate)*

### 25. The letters
*Map: Campaign · winter 333 to 332 BCE*
- Darius wrote to Alexander asking for his family back, and offering friendship. In Arrian's version, he said Alexander had invaded without any wrong done to him. *(arrian)*
- Alexander answered that he came to avenge Persia's wrongs against the Greeks, and demanded to be recognised as lord of Asia. *(arrian)*
- These letters come to us in the writers' own versions, which differ. They are not surviving originals. *(disputed)*

### 26. The road to Tyre
*Map: Campaign · winter 333 to 332 BCE*
- Alexander did not chase Darius inland. He marched south along the coast, taking the harbours of the Persian fleet. *(arrian)*
- Aradus, Byblos and Sidon submitted. The island city of Tyre would not let him in, and a siege of seven months began. Our page on the Siege of Tyre tells that story. *(arrian)*
- Darius was still free, and the empire could still raise another army. The two kings would meet again at Gaugamela, in 331 BCE. *(established)*

## Debrief

### What happened
- At the Granicus in 334 BCE, Alexander defeated the Persian satraps' cavalry and their Greek mercenaries, and opened western Anatolia. *(established)*
- At Issus in 333 BCE, he defeated Darius the Third. Darius escaped; his family was captured. *(established)*
- Neither battle ended the Persian Empire. *(established)*

### What the writers disagree about
- How the Granicus began: an attack at once, in Arrian; a dawn crossing, in Diodorus. *(disputed)*
- Who struck whom in the fight at the bank. *(disputed)*
- The sizes of the armies and the numbers killed: the Persian totals are far too large to be counts. *(estimate; disputed)*
- Where exactly the two battles were fought. *(disputed)*

### Voices we do not have
- No Persian history of these battles survives. We see Darius almost only through Greek and Roman eyes. *(established)*
