This is the script for the narrators to read, and the slide list for the projector. **Draft 1** (19 November 2026) is written
from the research report `research/RESEARCH-REPORT.md` (v3, after ChatGPT's source check and re-check). Report sections

**Format** (the same as the Ukraine page, `PRODUCTION-PLAN.md` there):
- An old carousel projector shows 35mm slides, with the click-clunk and fan whirr at each change.
- The slides advance **timed by the narration**.
- **Pictures:**
  - Wikimedia Commons photos with credit, each with its real date;
  - painted maps and scenes made by ChatGPT, with no real people in the paintings;
  - typed date cards.
- **No graphic violence.** Deaths are stated plainly, without detail.

**The approach:**
- **Four parts:**
  - After Tito (chapters 1–4)
  - Milošević (5–8)
  - Coming apart (9–12)
  - Toward war (13–16)
  - Introduction cards I1–I4 and a debrief.
- **No side is the villain, and no nation is described as thinking one thing.** Claims found only in one government's, party's
  or community's statements are attributed to it by name and date.
- **Kept apart:** what records show; what each side said; what courts found.
- **"Ancient hatreds" is not the explanation,** and the debrief says why historians reject it.
- **No sides, no red and blue.** The republics are drawn in neutral colours. (To confirm with the owner.)
- **Spoken forms:** numbers and dates as the narrator says them.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. This is not a battle. It is the background to the battles that follow. In 1980, Yugoslavia was one country of six republics, many nations and two alphabets. By the summer of 1991, two of its republics had declared independence. We will show you, slide by slide, how the federation came apart: what was decided, what was said, and what is still argued about. Some chapters describe deaths. We tell them plainly and without detail.

## Introduction

### I1. YUGOSLAVIA, 1980 TO 1991
▸ **CARD:** "Yugoslavia: Escalation, 1980–1991" over the title art (`art-title`).
- On the fourth of May 1980, Josip Broz Tito died. He had led Yugoslavia since 1945. *(established)* §2
- Eleven years later, on the twenty-fifth of June 1991, Slovenia and Croatia declared independence. *(established)* §10
- This page tells what happened in between. The wars that followed are told on the next pages. *(page)*

### I2. Where it is
▸ **MAP:** `map-europe` (the Ukraine page's Europe painting), slow zoom to the Balkans.
- Yugoslavia lay in south-eastern Europe, on the Adriatic Sea, between Italy and Austria to the north-west and Greece to the south. *(established)* §13
- It belonged to neither the Western alliance nor the Soviet bloc. It had broken with Stalin in 1948, and in 1961 helped found the Non-Aligned Movement. *(established)* §1

### I3. Six republics, two provinces
▸ **MAP:** `map-yugoslavia`, the republics and provinces outlined.
- Yugoslavia was a federation of six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro and Macedonia. *(established)* §1
- Inside Serbia were two autonomous provinces: Vojvodina in the north, and Kosovo in the south. *(established)* §1
- One party ruled: the League of Communists of Yugoslavia. *(established)* §1

### I4. How this page tells it
▸ **CARD:** the tags, with one example each.
- Each line is tagged with where it comes from. Some things are in laws and election results. Some are only what one side said. Some were found by a court. We tell you which. *(page)*
- Many of these events are remembered very differently by Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Albanians and others. Where they are, we give each account, and say whose it is. *(page)*

## Part 1: After Tito

### 1. Many nations
▸ **CARD:** "1981 federal census: 22,424,711 people". Serbs 36.3% · Croats 19.7% · Muslims 8.9% · Slovenes 7.8% · Albanians 7.7% · Macedonians 6.0% · Yugoslavs 5.4% · Montenegrins 2.6% · Hungarians 1.9%.
- The census of 1981 counted more than twenty-two million people. The largest nations were the Serbs and the Croats. Many others lived alongside them, and some people simply called themselves Yugoslavs. *(record)* §1
- "Muslims" was the census name for a nation then. Today, most of the people it counted call themselves Bosniaks. *(record)* §1

▸ **CARD:** "The 1974 constitution".
- The constitution of 1974 gave the republics, and the two provinces, wide powers, and each its own seat on the federal presidency. *(established)* §1
- There was a federal army, the Yugoslav People's Army. Each republic also had its own reserve force, the Territorial Defence. *(established)* §1

### 2. After Tito, Tito
▸ **PHOTO:** *Sahrana Josipa Broza Tita* (unknown author, Museum of Yugoslavia, CC BY-SA 3.0 Serbia). Caption: "Tito's funeral, Belgrade, 8 May 1980".
- Tito died in Ljubljana on the fourth of May 1980. His funeral in Belgrade was attended by many of the world's leaders. *(established)* §2

▸ **CARD:** "The collective Presidency: eight members · a new president each year".
- No one replaced him. A collective presidency of eight members, one from each republic and province, took over, and its president changed every year. *(established)* §2
- "After Tito, Tito" was the slogan. *(established)* §2

### 3. Debt
▸ **ART:** `art-banknotes`, worn banknotes and empty shelves (illustration).
- By 1982, Yugoslavia owed foreign lenders more than twenty billion dollars. *(record)* §3
- Austerity followed: shortages, rationed fuel, falling wages, and more people out of work, most of all in the poorer south. *(established)* §3

▸ **CARD:** "Rich and poor: Slovenia and Croatia · Kosovo".
- Slovenia and Croatia were the richest republics. Kosovo was the poorest region. Arguments about who should pay for whom grew sharper. *(established)* §3

### 4. Kosovo, 1981
▸ **MAP:** `map-kosovo`, Pristina marked.
- In March 1981, students at the University of Pristina began to protest. The protests spread. Many demonstrators demanded that Kosovo become a republic of its own. *(established)* §4
- A state of emergency was declared. *(established)* §4

▸ **CARD:** "April 1981: official reports: 8 demonstrators and 1 police officer killed".
- According to official reports cited by the International Commission of Jurists, eight demonstrators and one police officer were killed. Other estimates circulated. *(estimate)* §4
- Kosovo Albanian activists saw the response as repression. *(kosovo albanian account)* §4

## Part 2: Milošević

### 5. The Memorandum
▸ **CARD:** "September 1986: the draft Memorandum of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts".
- In the 1980s, Kosovo's Albanian majority grew. Many Serbs and Montenegrins left. Serb petitions complained of pressure. Albanians complained of arrests and trials. Why people left varied by place and time, and is still argued about. *(established; disputed)* §4
- In September 1986, a newspaper published a leaked draft by members of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts. It argued that Serbs were discriminated against in Yugoslavia, especially in Kosovo and Croatia. *(established)* §4
- Serbia's communist leaders at first condemned it. Whether it shaped what came later is a matter of interpretation. *(established; scholarship)* §4

### 6. Kosovo Polje
▸ **MAP:** `map-kosovo`, zoom to Kosovo Polje.
- On the twenty-fourth of April 1987, at Kosovo Polje, Slobodan Milošević, the head of Serbia's communist party, met local Serbs who said police had beaten them. *(established)* §5

▸ **CARD:** "Kosovo Polje, 24 April 1987": "Niko ne sme da vas bije." · "No one is allowed to beat you."
- He told them: "No one is allowed to beat you." The words were broadcast, and made him famous across Serbia. *(established)* §5

▸ **CARD:** "September 1987: the Eighth Session".
- That September, at a meeting of Serbia's party leaders, Milošević's side defeated Serbia's president, Ivan Stambolić, who had been his mentor. *(established)* §5

### 7. The rallies
▸ **CARD:** "October 1988: Vojvodina · January 1989: Montenegro".
- In 1988 and 1989, huge rallies brought down the leaders of Vojvodina, and then of Montenegro. Leaders loyal to Milošević replaced them. How spontaneous the rallies were is disputed. *(established; disputed)* §5

▸ **ART:** `art-mine`, the entrance to a mine in winter (illustration).
- In February 1989, Albanian miners at Trepča went on hunger strike against the removal of Kosovo's leaders. *(established)* §4
- In March, the assemblies voted for changes to Serbia's constitution that greatly reduced the provinces' self-government. *(established)* §4

▸ **CARD:** "March 1989: official toll: 25 killed, including 2 police officers".
- Unrest followed. The official toll was twenty-five dead, including two police officers. Other estimates circulated. *(estimate)* §4
- Kosovo Albanian activists said the vote had been forced. Federal authorities later said their response had answered violence against the police. *(kosovo albanian account; federal account)* §4
- On the eighth of May 1989, Serbia's assembly elected Milošević president of Serbia. *(established)* §5

### 8. Gazimestan
▸ **PHOTO:** *Gazimestan Kosovo* (FitimSelimi, CC BY-SA 3.0). Caption: "The Gazimestan monument, photographed in 2012".
- On the twenty-eighth of June 1989, six hundred years after the Battle of Kosovo, a very large crowd gathered at the Gazimestan monument. *(established; estimate)* §5

▸ **CARD:** "Gazimestan, 28 June 1989": the quotation, in Serbian and in English.
- Milošević said: "Six centuries later, today, we are again fighting battles and facing battles. They are not armed, although even such battles are not yet ruled out." *(established)* §5
- The same speech also spoke of economic, political and cultural prosperity. *(established)* §5
- His supporters said Serbia was restoring its unity, after years in which its provinces had been almost separate. *(serbian account)* §1

## Part 3: Coming apart

### 9. Slovenia
▸ **MAP:** `map-yugoslavia`, zoom to Slovenia.
- In 1988, four Slovenes, including the journalist Janez Janša, were arrested over a military document. *(established)* §6
- Their arrests sparked a defence committee and protests. In July they were tried by a military court in Ljubljana, in Serbo-Croatian, not in Slovene. *(established)* §6

▸ **CARD:** "September 1989: Slovenia's constitutional amendments · 1 December 1989: a rally banned".
- In September 1989, Slovenia's assembly declared its right to self-determination. *(record)* §6
- That December, Slovenia banned a rally of Kosovo Serbs planned in Ljubljana. A boycott of Slovenian goods followed in Serbia. *(established)* §6

### 10. A new dinar
▸ **PHOTO:** *Visit of Ante Marković, Yugoslav Prime Minister, to the CEC (cropped)* (European Commission, CC BY 4.0). Caption: "Ante Marković, Yugoslav prime minister, 1990".
- By 1989, prices were rising so fast that money lost its value from week to week. Over that year, prices rose by about two thousand seven hundred per cent. *(estimate)* §3
- In March 1989, Ante Marković became the federal prime minister. *(established)* §3

▸ **ART:** `art-banknotes` again, with a stamp: "1 January 1990: 1 new dinar = 10,000 old · 7 dinars = 1 Deutschmark".
- On the first of January 1990, he brought in a new dinar, worth ten thousand old ones, and tied it to the German mark. Within months, inflation had almost stopped. *(record)* §3
- But his reforms lost support during 1990. *(established)* §3

### 11. The last congress
▸ **ART:** `art-congress`, an empty congress hall (illustration).
- In January 1990, the League of Communists of Yugoslavia met in Belgrade. *(established)* §7
- The Slovenian delegation walked out. The Croatian delegation refused to continue without it. The party that had ruled Yugoslavia since 1945 never recovered. *(established)* §7

### 12. The elections of 1990
▸ **ART:** `art-ballot-1990`, a ballot box and posters with no readable words (illustration).
- In 1990, each republic held its own elections, the first with many parties since the Second World War. There were no federal elections. *(established)* §8
- Not all of them were equally free. *(established)* §8

▸ **CARD:** the table of six republics, with dates and results.
- In April and May, Slovenia and Croatia voted. In Slovenia, the DEMOS coalition won, and Milan Kučan was elected president. In Croatia, Franjo Tuđman's Croatian Democratic Union won a clear majority, and the parliament elected him president. *(record)* §8
- In November and December, Macedonia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia and Montenegro voted. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, three national parties won most seats. In Serbia, Milošević was elected president with about sixty-five per cent. *(record)* §8

▸ **CARD:** "May 1990: Territorial Defence weapons · July 1990: Kosovo".
- In May 1990, an order sought to move the republics' Territorial Defence weapons into the army's depots. Some Slovenian units kept their weapons, and Slovenia secretly organised its own defence. *(established)* §8
- In July, Albanian members of Kosovo's assembly, shut out of the building, declared Kosovo a republic within Yugoslavia. No federal authority recognised it. Days later, Serbia dissolved Kosovo's assembly. *(established)* §4

## Part 4: Toward war

### 13. Knin
▸ **MAP:** `map-croatia`, zoom to Knin and Srb.
- About one in eight people in Croatia were Serbs, the majority in some areas, among them the region around Knin. *(record)* §9
- In July 1990, a Serb assembly at Srb declared the sovereignty and autonomy of the Serbs in Croatia. *(established)* §9

▸ **ART:** `art-logs`, logs across an empty country road (illustration).
- On the seventeenth of August 1990, roads around Knin were blocked with logs. Croatian Serb leaders held a vote on Serb autonomy, and reported overwhelming support. It became known as the Log Revolution. *(established)* §9
- In December, they proclaimed a Serb Autonomous Region of Krajina. *(established)* §9

▸ **CARD:** "22 December 1990: the Constitution of the Republic of Croatia": the preamble, quoted.
- Croatia's new constitution, in force from the twenty-second of December 1990, described Croatia as the national state of the Croatian nation, and the state of members of other nations and minorities, naming the Serbs first among them. Its fourteenth article guaranteed equality. *(record)* §9
- The Croatian government said Serbs kept all their rights. Krajina Serb leaders said Serbs had lost their status as a nation of the republic. *(croatian account; croatian serb account)* §9

### 14. The army and the presidency
▸ **CARD:** "25 January 1991: a film on Belgrade television".
- In January 1991, the federal presidency ordered illegal armed groups to disarm. Belgrade television showed an army film about Croatian arms imports, with Croatia's defence minister, Martin Špegelj. How the film was edited is disputed. *(established; disputed)* §9

▸ **ART:** `art-street`, an armoured vehicle on an empty city street at night (illustration).
- On the ninth of March 1991, a large opposition protest filled central Belgrade. Armoured vehicles were sent into the streets. A police officer, Nedeljko Kosović, and a demonstrator, Branivoje Milinović, were killed. *(established)* §10

▸ **CARD:** "12–15 March 1991: the Presidency votes".
- Days later, the army's leaders asked the federal presidency for emergency measures. The proposal failed to win a majority. *(established)* §10
- The army said such measures would prevent violence between nations and protect the federation. Croatian authorities said the army was blocking Croatian police and protecting Serb-held areas. *(federal account; croatian account)* §10
- On the sixteenth of March, Milošević said Serbia no longer recognised the presidency's authority. *(account)* §10

▸ **CARD:** "25 March 1991: Karađorđevo".
- On the twenty-fifth of March, Milošević and Tuđman met at Karađorđevo. A Croatian politician later testified that Tuđman told him they had agreed in principle to divide Bosnia. He was not at the meeting. Others dispute that any such deal was made, and no agreed text has been verified. *(account; disputed)* §10

### 15. Spring 1991
▸ **MAP:** `map-croatia`, Pakrac, Plitvice, Borovo Selo and Split marked.
- In March 1991, Croatian police and local Serb forces faced each other at Pakrac. The army intervened. No deaths were confirmed. *(established)* §9

▸ **PHOTO:** *Plitvice Lakes- National Park 4* (Logica olgica, CC BY-SA 4.0). Caption: "The Plitvice Lakes, photographed in 2012".
- On the thirty-first of March, at the Plitvice Lakes, Croatian police retook the national park. A Croatian policeman, Josip Jović, and a Serb, Rajko Vukadinović, were killed. *(established)* §9

▸ **PHOTO:** *Memorial in Borovo Selo* (Modzzak, public domain). Caption: "The memorial in Borovo Selo, photographed in 2007".
- On the second of May, at Borovo Selo, near Vukovar, twelve Croatian policemen were killed. The tribunal's Mrkšić judgment, in its background, also reports three Serb civilians killed. *(established; court)* §9
- On the sixth of May, at a protest in Split, a Yugoslav army soldier, Saško Gešovski, was killed. *(established)* §9

### 16. The twenty-fifth of June
▸ **CARD:** "Slovenia, 23 December 1990 · Croatia, 19 May 1991": the referendum results.
- In December 1990, eighty-eight and a half per cent of Slovenia's voters voted for independence. *(record)* §10
- In May 1991, Croatia voted. Of those who took part, about ninety-three per cent said yes to a sovereign and independent Croatia that could join a union of sovereign republics. Many Serbs boycotted the vote. Krajina's leaders had held their own vote on joining Serbia. *(record)* §9

▸ **CARD:** "March–June 1991: the presidents of the six republics meet" · Split · Belgrade · Brdo · Ohrid · Cetinje · Stojčevo.
- Through the spring, the presidents of the six republics met again and again. Slovenia and Croatia wanted a looser union of states. Serbia and Montenegro wanted a federation. Bosnia and Herzegovina and Macedonia proposed a compromise. They found no agreement. *(established)* §10
- In May, Croatia's Stjepan Mesić was blocked from taking his turn as head of the federal presidency. *(established)* §10

▸ **CARD:** "Belgrade, 21 June 1991": "We worry, frankly, about history repeating itself." James Baker, US Secretary of State.
- On the twenty-first of June, the American Secretary of State, James Baker, came to Belgrade and called for unity and democracy. He said: "We worry, frankly, about history repeating itself." *(account)* §10

▸ **ART:** `art-assembly`, an empty assembly hall at dawn (illustration).
- On the twenty-fifth of June 1991, the assemblies of Slovenia and Croatia declared independence. Two days later, fighting began in Slovenia. That war, and those that followed, are told on the next pages. *(established; page)* §10

## Debrief

### What happened
▸ **MAP:** `map-yugoslavia`.
- After Tito's death, the federation faced debt, austerity and arguments over money and power. *(established)*
- In Serbia, Milošević rose on the Kosovo question, and Serbia's provinces lost their self-government. *(established)*
- The party that held Yugoslavia together split in 1990. Each republic elected its own leaders. *(established)*
- In Croatia, Serb leaders around Knin rejected the new government. Armed incidents began. In June 1991, Slovenia and Croatia declared independence. *(established)*

### How historians read it
▸ **CARD:** "Not ancient hatreds".
- Most historians reject the idea that old hatreds made the war inevitable. For decades, Yugoslavs had lived together, worked together and married each other. *(scholarship)* §11
- Susan Woodward stresses debt, austerity and a weakened federal state. V. P. Gagnon stresses leaders who used conflict to hold on to power. Dejan Jović stresses how federal authority wore away. These readings overlap. *(scholarship)* §11

### The courts
▸ **CARD:** "The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia".
- Later, an international tribunal tried some of the people in this story. Milošević died in 2006, before his trial ended. Milan Babić, a Krajina Serb leader, pleaded guilty to persecution. *(court)* §11
- Those trials mostly concerned the wars after June 1991, which are told on the following pages. *(page)*
