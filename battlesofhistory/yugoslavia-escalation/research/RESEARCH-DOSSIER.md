## Evidence labels
- **ESTABLISHED:** documented in official records, laws, election results, or the standard scholarship.
- **ACCOUNT:** a claim by one person, one report or one newspaper; attributed.
- **ESTIMATE:** an uncertain number, with what it counts.
- **DISPUTED:** credible sources disagree; both are stated.
- **COURT:** a finding of the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) or another court, with
  what it did and did not decide.
- **Accounts of one side only:** SERBIAN ACCOUNT, CROATIAN ACCOUNT, SLOVENIAN ACCOUNT, KOSOVO ALBANIAN ACCOUNT,
  FEDERAL / JNA ACCOUNT. Each is used for a claim found only in that government's, party's or community's statements,
  with who said it and when.
- **SCHOLARSHIP:** historians' interpretation, named.

## 1. Tito's Yugoslavia (1945–1980): the starting point
- **The federation:**
  - six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro and Macedonia;
  - two autonomous provinces inside Serbia: Vojvodina and Kosovo;
  - ruled by one party, the League of Communists of Yugoslavia (LCY), and led by Josip Broz Tito from 1945.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **The 1974 constitution** gave the republics, and the two provinces, wide powers, including their own seats on the federal
  presidency. The provinces were nearly equal to republics, but still part of Serbia. Many Serbian politicians later
  resented this. [ESTABLISHED; the resentment SERBIAN ACCOUNT and SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Self-management and non-alignment.** Yugoslavia had its own "self-managing" socialism, independent of Moscow after the
  break with Stalin in 1948. It was a founder of the Non-Aligned Movement (Belgrade, 1961). Many Yugoslavs worked in western
  Europe, and the country was open to tourists. [ESTABLISHED]
- **Defence:**
  - The **Yugoslav People's Army (JNA)** was federal.
  - After 1968, each republic also had its own **Territorial Defence (TO)**, a reserve force for "total national defence".
    These weapons mattered in 1990–1991.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **The Croatian Spring** of 1971, a reform and national movement in Croatia, was suppressed. Its leaders were removed, and
  Franjo Tuđman was among those imprisoned. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The 1981 census** (the last before the break-up; verify every figure):
  - about 22.4 million people;
  - Serbs about 36%, Croats about 20%, Muslims (by nationality) about 9%, Slovenes about 8%, Albanians about 8%,
    Macedonians about 6%, "Yugoslavs" about 5%, Montenegrins about 3%, Hungarians about 2%.
  - In 1981 the census category was "Muslims"; "Bosniaks" is the later name.
  - [ESTABLISHED; figures to verify]

## 2. Tito's death and the collective presidency (1980)
- **4 May 1980:** Tito died in Ljubljana, aged 87. His funeral in Belgrade on 8 May was attended by many heads of state and
  government. [ESTABLISHED]
- **After him:**
  - A collective **Presidency** of eight members (one from each republic and province) took over.
  - Its president changed each year, by rotation.
  - The party's leadership also rotated.
  - The slogan "After Tito, Tito" ("I posle Tita – Tito") expressed continuity.
  - [ESTABLISHED]

## 3. The economic crisis (1979–1990)
- **Foreign debt:** about US$20 billion by the early 1980s (verify the figure and year). IMF stabilisation programmes
  followed. [ESTIMATE]
- **Austerity:**
  - shortages of fuel, coffee and other goods;
  - rationing (for example, odd–even driving days);
  - falling real wages and rising unemployment, highest in the poorer south.
  - [ESTABLISHED in outline; details to verify]
- **Inflation:** it rose through the 1980s and reached hyperinflation in 1989, more than 1,000% a year (verify the annual
  figure). [ESTIMATE]
- **Ante Marković,** federal prime minister from March 1989. His reform programme of December 1989:
  - a new "convertible" dinar (10,000 old dinars to 1), pegged to the Deutschmark;
  - inflation fell sharply in early 1990;
  - his reforms lost support during 1990;
  - his Alliance of Reform Forces did poorly in the 1990 elections.
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify the dates and figures]
- **Rich and poor republics:** Slovenia and Croatia were the richest, Kosovo by far the poorest. Disputes over federal
  taxes and funds for the poorer regions sharpened in the 1980s. [ESTABLISHED]

## 4. Kosovo (1981–1989)
- **March–April 1981:** protests began at the University of Pristina on 11 March 1981, over student conditions, and spread
  in March and April. Many demonstrators demanded that Kosovo become a republic. A state of emergency was declared, and
  troops and federal police were sent. [ESTABLISHED]
  - **The deaths:** the official toll was 9 or 11 (verify); others claimed far more. [ESTIMATE; DISPUTED]
- **Through the 1980s:**
  - Kosovo's Albanian majority grew.
  - Many Serbs and Montenegrins left Kosovo. Serb petitions and protests complained of pressure and discrimination.
  - Albanians complained of repression, arrests and trials.
  - [ESTABLISHED; the causes of emigration DISPUTED: economic, and pressure; both stated]
- **The SANU Memorandum (September 1986):**
  - A draft memorandum by members of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts was leaked to the Belgrade paper Večernje
    novosti (24–25 September 1986).
  - It argued that Serbs were discriminated against in Yugoslavia, especially in Kosovo and Croatia.
  - Serbia's communist leadership at first condemned it. [ESTABLISHED]
  - **How it is read:** whether it was a programme for what followed, or a document later used as a symbol. [DISPUTED;
    SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Slovenia's answer:** Nova revija's issue 57 (February 1987), "Contributions to a Slovenian National Programme".
  [ESTABLISHED; verify]

## 5. Milošević (1987–1989)
- **24 April 1987, Kosovo Polje:**
  - Slobodan Milošević, head of the League of Communists of Serbia, met local Serbs who were complaining of police beatings.
  - He told them: "No one should dare to beat you!" ("Niko ne sme da vas bije!").
  - It was broadcast on television and made him a national figure in Serbia.
  - [ESTABLISHED; the wording to verify]
- **September 1987, the Eighth Session** of the Serbian party's Central Committee:
  - Milošević's side defeated the reformers around Ivan Stambolić, Serbia's president and his former mentor.
  - Stambolić was removed in December 1987.
  - (He was murdered in 2000. A Serbian court convicted members of Serbian State Security's special unit; outside this
    page's years.)
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **The "anti-bureaucratic revolution" (1988–1989):** mass rallies, many organised, brought down the leaderships of:
  - Vojvodina (October 1988, the "yoghurt revolution");
  - Montenegro (January 1989).
  - New leaders loyal to Milošević replaced them.
  - [ESTABLISHED; how spontaneous the rallies were DISPUTED]
- **Kosovo, 1989:**
  - Albanian miners at Trepča went on hunger strike in February 1989, against the removal of Kosovo's leaders.
  - In March 1989 the Kosovo and Serbian assemblies approved amendments to Serbia's constitution that greatly reduced the
    provinces' autonomy.
  - Protests and riots followed; people were killed (verify the number).
  - [ESTABLISHED; the figure ESTIMATE]
- **Milošević became president of Serbia's Presidency in May 1989.** [ESTABLISHED; verify the date]
- **28 June 1989, Gazimestan:**
  - On the 600th anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo, Milošević spoke to a very large crowd (ESTIMATE: hundreds of thousands
    to a million).
  - He said: "Six centuries later, now, we are being again engaged in battles and are facing battles. They are not armed
    battles, although such things cannot yet be excluded." [ESTABLISHED; the exact translation to verify]
- **The two readings:**
  - Serbian supporters saw a restoration of Serbia's rights over its own provinces. [SERBIAN ACCOUNT]
  - Slovenes, Croats and Kosovo Albanians saw a threat to the federation's balance. [SLOVENIAN, CROATIAN and KOSOVO ALBANIAN
    ACCOUNTS]
  - After the changes, Serbia and its allies controlled four of the eight votes on the federal Presidency (Serbia, Vojvodina,
    Kosovo, Montenegro). [ESTABLISHED; SCHOLARSHIP]

## 6. Slovenia (1988–1990)
- **The JBTZ trial, 1988:**
  - A military court in Ljubljana tried four men: Janez Janša, Ivan Borštner, David Tasić and the editor Franci Zavrl.
  - The charge concerned leaked military documents.
  - The trial was held in Serbo-Croatian, not Slovene.
  - Mass protests followed, and the Committee for the Defence of Human Rights was formed.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **September 1989:** Slovenia's assembly amended its constitution, asserting the right to self-determination including
  secession, and control over the use of emergency measures. [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **December 1989:**
  - A planned "rally of truth" by Kosovo Serbs in Ljubljana was banned by Slovenia.
  - Serbia's organisations called a boycott of Slovenian goods. [ESTABLISHED; verify]

## 7. The end of the League of Communists (January 1990)
- **20–22 January 1990, Belgrade:** the 14th Extraordinary Congress of the LCY.
  - The Slovenian delegation's proposals were voted down, and it walked out.
  - The Croatian delegation followed.
  - The congress was not resumed, and the party ceased to function as a federal body.
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify the dates]

## 9. Croatia and its Serbs (1990–1991)
- **The 1991 census:** Serbs were about 12% of Croatia's population, the majority in parts of Lika, Kordun, Banija, northern
  Dalmatia (around Knin), and parts of Slavonia. [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **The Serb Democratic Party (SDS)**, founded in Knin in February 1990: Jovan Rašković, then Milan Babić. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The constitution:**
  - Croatia's amendments of July 1990 changed state symbols (the chequered coat of arms).
  - The constitution of 22 December 1990 ("the Christmas Constitution") described Croatia as the national state of the
    Croatian nation and a state of members of other nations and minorities, Serbs among them.
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify the wording]
  - **Two readings:**
    - Croatian authorities said Serbs kept full civic rights. [CROATIAN ACCOUNT]
    - Serb leaders said Serbs had been reduced from a constituent nation to a minority. [SERBIAN ACCOUNT]
  - Dismissals of Serbs from police and public jobs are also cited. [ACCOUNT; ESTIMATE]
- **25 July 1990, Srb:** a Serb assembly declared the sovereignty and autonomy of the Serb people in Croatia. [ESTABLISHED]
- **August 1990, the "Log Revolution":**
  - Serbs around Knin blocked roads with logs and organised a referendum on autonomy (19 August – 2 September; the result
    reported as nearly unanimous).
  - Croatian police helicopters sent toward Knin were turned back by JNA fighter jets (17 August).
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify the dates]
- **21 December 1990:** the "Serb Autonomous Region (SAO) of Krajina" was proclaimed. [ESTABLISHED]
- **January 1991:**
  - The federal Presidency ordered all "illegal" armed formations to disarm.
  - On 25 January, Belgrade television broadcast a secretly filmed JNA counter-intelligence film about Croatian arms
    imports. It showed Croatia's defence minister, Martin Špegelj.
  - [ESTABLISHED; how the film was edited DISPUTED]
- **1–2 March 1991, Pakrac:** a confrontation between Croatian police and local Serb forces. The JNA intervened. (Verify:
  no deaths reported.)
- **31 March 1991, Plitvice (Easter Sunday):**
  - Croatian police retook the national park's headquarters from Krajina forces.
  - Two men were killed: Croatian policeman Josip Jović and Serb Rajko Vukadinović.
  - They are often called the first deaths of the war in Croatia.
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **2 May 1991, Borovo Selo:**
  - **12 Croatian policemen** were killed in an ambush and fighting in the village. (Vukovar's page has the checked wording.)
  - Serb casualties also occurred (verify).
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **6 May 1991, Split:** a protest at the Yugoslav navy's headquarters. A JNA soldier, Saško Gešovski, was killed.
  [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **12 May 1991:** a referendum in SAO Krajina on joining Serbia. [ESTABLISHED]
- **19 May 1991:** Croatia's referendum. About 94% voted for a sovereign and independent Croatia, which could enter an
  alliance of sovereign states (verify the figures and wording); about 83% turnout; many Serbs boycotted it. [ESTABLISHED;
  figures to verify]

## 10. The federation's last months (December 1990 – June 1991)
- **23 December 1990, Slovenia's plebiscite:** 88.5% of all voters (not only those who voted) for independence. The turnout
  was about 93%. [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **March 1991:**
  - **9 March, Belgrade:** a large opposition protest (Vuk Drašković's SPO) against state television. Tanks were sent into
    the streets; two people were killed. [ESTABLISHED; verify]
  - **12–15 March:** the JNA's leadership asked the Presidency to declare a state of emergency, and was refused.
    - Serbia's member, Borisav Jović, resigned, then returned.
    - Milošević said Serbia would no longer recognise the Presidency's decisions (verify).
    - [ESTABLISHED; verify]
  - **25 March, Karađorđevo:** Milošević and Tuđman met. Whether they discussed dividing Bosnia and Herzegovina is disputed.
    Later witnesses claimed they did; others deny it. [DISPUTED: no record]
- **The "summits of the six presidents"** of the republics (March–June 1991), in Split, Belgrade, Brdo, Ohrid, Cetinje and
  Sarajevo, found no agreement:
  - Slovenia and Croatia wanted a confederation.
  - Serbia and Montenegro wanted a federation.
  - On 3 June, Izetbegović and Gligorov proposed a compromise "asymmetric" federation.
  - [ESTABLISHED; verify]
- **15 May 1991:** Serbia, Montenegro, Vojvodina and Kosovo's members blocked Croatia's Stjepan Mesić from becoming
  president of the Presidency by rotation. The federation had no head of state until July (Brioni). [ESTABLISHED]
- **Abroad:** the European Community and the United States said they wanted a united, democratic Yugoslavia. US Secretary
  of State James Baker visited Belgrade on 21 June 1991. [ESTABLISHED; Baker's words to verify]
- **25 June 1991:** the Slovenian and Croatian assemblies declared independence. **The page ends here; the Ten-Day War
  page begins.** [ESTABLISHED]

## 11. Causes: how historians read them (for the debrief)
- **"Ancient hatreds":** the idea that old ethnic hatreds made war inevitable is rejected by most historians. Yugoslavs
  had lived together, and intermarried, for decades. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Economy and the state** (Susan Woodward, *Balkan Tragedy*): debt and austerity, and a federation that could not share
  the costs. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Leaders and nationalist politics** (Laura Silber and Allan Little, *The Death of Yugoslavia*; V. P. Gagnon): politicians
  who used nationalism to win and hold power. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **The weakening of the federation and its constitution** (Dejan Jović, *Yugoslavia: A State that Withered Away*).
  [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **The courts:**
  - The ICTY's trial of Milošević ended without a verdict when he died in 2006.
  - Milan Babić pleaded guilty in 2004 to persecution of Croats and other non-Serbs in Croatia in 1991–1992.
  - Milan Martić was convicted (2007; appeal 2008).
  - These cases mostly concern events after June 1991; they are named only in the debrief, and carefully.
  - [COURT; verify]
