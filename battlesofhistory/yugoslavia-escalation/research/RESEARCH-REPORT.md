## 1. Tito's Yugoslavia: the starting point
- **The federation:**
  - six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Macedonia;
  - two autonomous provinces in Serbia: Vojvodina and Kosovo;
  - one party, the League of Communists of Yugoslavia (LCY);
  - Josip Broz Tito, leader from 1945.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **The 1974 constitution** gave the republics and the provinces wide powers, and each its own seat on the federal
  Presidency. [ESTABLISHED] Serbian politicians later argued that provincial autonomy had divided Serbia's authority.
  [SERBIAN ACCOUNT; Milošević, Gazimestan speech exhibit]
- **Self-management and non-alignment.** Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948, and was a founder of the Non-Aligned Movement
  (Belgrade, 1961). [ESTABLISHED]
- **Defence:** the federal **Yugoslav People's Army (JNA)**; and, after 1968, each republic's own **Territorial Defence (TO)**.
  [ESTABLISHED]
- **The Croatian Spring** of 1971 was suppressed. Franjo Tuđman was among those imprisoned. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The 1981 federal census.** There was also a 1991 census round, with uneven coverage.
  - Population: **22,424,711**.
  - Serbs 36.3%, Croats 19.7%, Muslims (by nationality) 8.9%, Slovenes 7.8%, Albanians 7.7%, Macedonians 6.0%,
    "Yugoslavs" 5.4%, Montenegrins 2.6%, Hungarians 1.9%.
  - "Muslims" was the census category. "Bosniaks" is the later name, and the two are not assumed to be identical
    populations.
  - [ESTABLISHED; Archives of Yugoslavia]

## 2. Tito's death and the collective Presidency (1980)
- **4 May 1980:** Tito died in Ljubljana. His funeral in Belgrade on 8 May was attended by many heads of state and
  government. [ESTABLISHED]
- **After him:** the collective Presidency, which already existed, became the head of state: a representative from every
  republic and province, plus the president of the League of Communists by virtue of office (1974 constitution, art. 321),
  with a chair that rotated each year (draft 1 review). The party leadership also rotated. "After Tito, Tito" expressed continuity. [ESTABLISHED]

## 3. The economic crisis (1979–1990)
- **Foreign debt:** **US$20.3 billion in 1982** (World Bank retrospective). IMF programmes and austerity followed. [ESTABLISHED;
  World Bank, former Yugoslavia's debt apportionment]
- **Austerity:** shortages, rationing of fuel, falling real wages, rising unemployment, highest in the poorer south.
  [ESTABLISHED in outline]
- **Inflation:** about **2,700% during 1989** (OECD). This is not the annual-average consumer price rate. [ESTIMATE; OECD
  Economic Survey 1990]
- **Ante Marković,** federal prime minister from March 1989. His programme:
  - announced **19 December 1989**, in force **1 January 1990**;
  - a new dinar, worth **10,000 old dinars** (the Act on the Change of the Value of the Dinar, Official Gazette of the SFRY
    83/89, as cited), pegged at **seven to the Deutschmark**;
  - monthly inflation fell from **64% in December 1989** to near zero in April–June 1990 (World Bank).
  - His reforms lost support during 1990.
  - [ESTABLISHED; OECD; World Bank]
- **Rich and poor:** Slovenia and Croatia were the richest, Kosovo the poorest. Disputes over federal funds for the poorer
  regions sharpened. [ESTABLISHED]

## 4. Kosovo (1981–1990)
- **March–April 1981:** protests began at the University of Pristina on 11 March and spread. Many demonstrators demanded that
  Kosovo become a republic. A state of emergency followed. [ESTABLISHED]
  - **The deaths:** official reports cited by the International Commission of Jurists gave **eight demonstrators and one
    police officer** killed in the April unrest. Other estimates circulated. [ESTIMATE; attributed; ICJ, *States of
    Emergency*, p. 85]
  - The authorities declared emergency measures. Kosovo Albanian activists saw repression. [KOSOVO ALBANIAN ACCOUNT]
- **Through the 1980s:**
  - Kosovo's Albanian majority grew.
  - Many Serbs and Montenegrins left. Serb petitions complained of pressure.
  - Albanians complained of arrests and trials.
  - **Why people left** varied by place and period. The page does not give one cause. [DISPUTED]
- **The SANU Memorandum (September 1986):**
  - A leaked draft by members of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts argued that Serbs were discriminated against,
    especially in Kosovo and Croatia.
  - Serbia's communist leadership at first condemned it.
  - The page treats it as evidence that these arguments circulated. Claims that it directed later policy are an
    interpretation. [ESTABLISHED; the reading SCHOLARSHIP]
- **1989:**
  - the Trepča miners' hunger strike (February);
  - the March votes on Serbia's constitutional amendments, which greatly reduced the provinces' autonomy;
  - unrest followed. The **official toll was 25, including two police officers** (as recorded in the US State Department's
    1989 human-rights report). Other estimates circulated. [ESTIMATE; attributed]
  - Kosovo Albanian activists regarded the vote as coerced; the loss of autonomy itself is documented. [KOSOVO ALBANIAN
    ACCOUNT; ESTABLISHED]
  - Federal authorities, replying to the UN rapporteur, described the suppression of the 1989 demonstrations as a response to
    violence against police. [FEDERAL ACCOUNT; UN E/CN.4/1991/36]
- **July 1990:**
  - On 2 July 1990, Kosovo Albanian assembly members, denied access to the assembly building, declared Kosovo a republic
    within Yugoslavia. The federal authorities did not recognise it.
  - On 5 July, Serbia dissolved the provincial assembly.
  - [ESTABLISHED; ICTY Milutinović et al. trial judgment, vol. I, notes 405–407]

## 5. Milošević (1987–1989)
- **24 April 1987, Kosovo Polje:**
  - Slobodan Milošević, head of the League of Communists of Serbia, told local Serbs: **"Niko ne sme da vas bije"** — "No one
    is allowed to beat you."
  - It was broadcast, and made him a national figure in Serbia.
  - [ESTABLISHED; the words are recorded in the Kosovo Polje footage reproduced in the ICTY proceedings (9 February 2005)]
- **September 1987, the Eighth Session:** Milošević's side defeated Serbia's president, Ivan Stambolić, his former mentor, who
  was removed in December 1987. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The "anti-bureaucratic revolution" (1988–1989):** mass rallies brought down the leaderships of Vojvodina (October 1988)
  and Montenegro (January 1989). [ESTABLISHED; how spontaneous they were DISPUTED]
- **8 May 1989:** Milošević was elected president of Serbia's Presidency, by Serbia's assembly. This is distinct from the
  popular presidential election of December 1990. [ESTABLISHED; ICTY indictment chronology]
- **Votes on the federal Presidency:** the page does not say that Serbia "controlled four votes" throughout 1989. Particular
  votes are dated and documented when used, as in March 1991 (§10). [Source check]
- **28 June 1989, Gazimestan,** on the 600th anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo. Milošević said:
  - **"Šest vekova kasnije, danas, mi ponovo bijemo bitke i suočeni smo sa bitkama. One nisu oružane, mada ni takve još nisu
    isključene."**
  - "Six centuries later, today, we are again fighting battles and facing battles. They are not armed, although even such
    battles are not yet ruled out."
  - The wider passage also speaks of economic, political and cultural prosperity.
  - [ESTABLISHED; the recorded speech, ICTY proceedings]
  - The crowd: very large. [ESTIMATE: no single figure is used]

## 6. Slovenia (1988–1990)
- **The JBTZ trial, 1988:**
  - Janez Janša, Ivan Borštner, David Tasić and Franci Zavrl were tried by a military court in Ljubljana, over a military
    document.
  - Their arrests sparked a defence committee (the Committee for the Defence of Human Rights) and protests, before the
    military trial of 18–27 July 1988.
  - The trial was held in Serbo-Croatian, not Slovene.
  - [ESTABLISHED]
- **September 1989:** constitutional amendments asserted Slovenia's right to self-determination. [ESTABLISHED]
- **December 1989:** a "rally of truth" planned for 1 December 1989 in Ljubljana, organised by the Božur association of
  Kosovo Serbs, was banned. A boycott of Slovenian goods followed in Serbia (no exact start date, and not universal).
  [ESTABLISHED; Slovenian government]
- **Slovenia kept part of its Territorial Defence** and a defence structure in 1990–1991. [ESTABLISHED; Slovenian government
  chronology]

## 7. The last congress (January 1990)
- **20–22 January 1990,** the 14th Extraordinary Congress of the LCY:
  - The Slovenian delegation walked out.
  - The Croatian delegation refused to continue without it.
  - A rump session met in May, and the federal party did not recover.
  - [ESTABLISHED; Helsinki Committee, citing Borba and Novosti]

## 9. Croatia and its Serbs (1990–1991)
- **Serbs in Croatia:** **581,663, 12.16%** of the population (1991 census, State Bureau of Statistics table). They were the majority
  in parts of Lika, Kordun, Banija, northern Dalmatia around Knin, and parts of Slavonia.
- **The Serb Democratic Party (SDS),** founded in Knin in February 1990: Jovan Rašković, then Milan Babić. [ESTABLISHED]
- **The constitution:**
  - adopted 21 December 1990, proclaimed and in force 22 December ("the Christmas Constitution");
  - the preamble: "the national state of the Croatian nation and the state of members of other nations and minorities who
    are its citizens" ("nacionalna država hrvatskoga naroda i država pripadnika inih naroda i manjina, koji su njezini
    državljani"), naming **Serbs first** in the list;
  - Article 14 guarantees equality.
  - [ESTABLISHED; Narodne novine 56/1990]
  - **Two readings:**
    - the Croatian government said Serbs kept full civic rights. [CROATIAN ACCOUNT]
    - Krajina Serb leaders treated the change as a loss of status as a constituent nation. [CROATIAN SERB ACCOUNT; Martić
      judgment]
- **25 July 1990, Srb:** a Serb assembly declared the sovereignty and autonomy of the Serb people in Croatia. [ESTABLISHED]
- **August 1990, the "Log Revolution":**
  - **17 August:** barricades of logs on the roads around Knin.
  - **19 August – 2 September:** a referendum on the sovereignty and autonomy of the Serbs in Croatia, organised by Croatian
    Serb leaders, who reported overwhelming support. No total is given.
  - The helicopter episode is left out: it is not independently settled.
  - [ESTABLISHED; ICTY Martić indictment and judgment]
- **21 December 1990:** the "Serb Autonomous Region (SAO) of Krajina" was proclaimed. [ESTABLISHED]
- **January 1991:**
  - the Presidency's order to disarm "illegal" armed formations;
  - the JNA counter-intelligence film about Croatian arms imports, shown on Belgrade television (25 January), with Defence
    Minister Martin Špegelj.
  - [ESTABLISHED; how the film was edited DISPUTED]
- **1–2 March 1991, Pakrac:** a confrontation between Croatian police and local Serb forces. The JNA intervened. **No
  confirmed deaths;** contemporary reporting said three Croatian policemen were wounded. [ESTABLISHED; Los Angeles Times,
  5 March 1991]
- **31 March 1991, Plitvice:** Croatian policeman Josip Jović and Serb Rajko Vukadinović were killed. They are not called
  "the first deaths of the war" without qualification. [ESTABLISHED]
- **2 May 1991, Borovo Selo:**
  - 12 Croatian policemen were killed.
  - Three Serb civilians killed: a count reported in the background section of the ICTY's Mrkšić judgment, not a
    comprehensive casualty finding.
  - [ESTABLISHED; COURT background]
- **6 May 1991, Split:** a JNA soldier, Saško Gešovski, was killed during a protest at the navy's headquarters. No cause or
  perpetrator is stated. [ESTABLISHED]
- **12 May 1991:** Krajina Serb authorities held a vote on joining Serbia and remaining in Yugoslavia. [ESTABLISHED; Martić
  judgment]
- **19 May 1991, Croatia's referendum** (Sabor figures):
  - **83.56%** turnout, and **93.24%** of participants in favour on the blue ballot.
  - The blue question: whether a sovereign and independent Croatia, guaranteeing cultural autonomy and all civic rights to
    Serbs and other nationalities in Croatia, could enter a union of sovereign states with the other republics.
  - The red ballot asked about remaining in Yugoslavia as a single federal state.
  - Many Serbs boycotted the vote.
  - [ESTABLISHED; Sabor; referendum decree]
  - **Two accounts:**
    - the Croatian government proposed sovereignty with explicit guarantees for Serbs. [CROATIAN ACCOUNT; decree]
    - Krajina Serb leaders asserted their own sovereignty and autonomy. [CROATIAN SERB ACCOUNT]

## 10. The federation's last months
- **23 December 1990, Slovenia's plebiscite:** **88.5% of eligible voters** voted yes; the turnout was **93.2%**.
  [ESTABLISHED; Slovenian government]
  - The Slovenian government presented independence as carrying out a democratic mandate, after negotiations failed.
    [SLOVENIAN ACCOUNT]
- **9 March 1991, Belgrade:** a large opposition protest; armour deployed in the streets. Two people were killed: police
  officer Nedeljko Kosović and demonstrator Branivoje Milinović. [ESTABLISHED; Vreme, contemporary report]
- **12–15 March 1991, the Presidency crisis:**
  - The JNA's leadership proposed emergency measures to the Presidency, and the proposal failed to win a majority.
  - Serbia's member, Borisav Jović, resigned, then returned.
  - On 16 March, Milošević said Serbia no longer recognised the authority of the federal Presidency (paraphrase, as reported
    at the time; no direct quotation). [Washington Post, 17 March 1991]
  - [ESTABLISHED]
  - **Two accounts:**
    - Army leaders presented emergency measures as preventing interethnic violence and protecting the federation.
      [FEDERAL / JNA ACCOUNT]
    - Croatian accounts describe JNA interventions as obstructing Croatian police and increasingly protecting Serb-held
      areas. [CROATIAN ACCOUNT: Croatian authorities and witnesses, as cited in the Martić judgment]
- **25 March 1991, Karađorđevo:**
  - Milošević and Tuđman met.
  - Josip Manolić later testified to what he said Tuđman told him: an agreement in principle on Bosnia. Manolić was not at
    the meeting.
  - The defence in the ICTY's Prlić trial disputed the inference that a partition deal had been reached, citing Tuđman's
    later support for Bosnia's referendum.
  - No agreed text has been verified.
  - [DISPUTED; Prlić testimony and defence brief]
- **The summits of the six presidents** found no agreement: Split (from 27 March), Belgrade (4 April), Brdo near Kranj
  (11 April), Ohrid (18 April), Cetinje (29 April) and Stojčevo in Bosnia and Herzegovina (6 June). Slovenia and Croatia
  proposed a confederation; Serbia and Montenegro a federation. Izetbegović and Gligorov proposed a compromise (the exact
  date is not used). [ESTABLISHED; Helsinki Committee history]
- **15 May 1991:** Stjepan Mesić of Croatia was blocked from becoming president of the Presidency by rotation. [ESTABLISHED]
- **21 June 1991, James Baker in Belgrade:**
  - The US Secretary of State appealed for unity, democracy, human rights and territorial integrity.
  - He said: "We worry, frankly, about history repeating itself." [ESTABLISHED; Washington Post, 22 June 1991, reported
    quotation]
- **25 June 1991:** the Slovenian and Croatian assemblies declared independence. **The page ends here.** [ESTABLISHED]

## 11. Causes: how historians read them (for the debrief)
- **Not "ancient hatreds":** most historians reject the idea that old hatreds made war inevitable. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Susan Woodward** (*Balkan Tragedy*): debt, austerity and a weakened federal state. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **V. P. Gagnon** (*The Myth of Ethnic War*): elites used conflict to demobilise challengers at home. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Dejan Jović** (*Yugoslavia: A State that Withered Away*): the ideological and constitutional weakening of federal
  authority. [SCHOLARSHIP]
- These readings overlap.
- **The courts** (named separately, in the debrief only):
  - Milošević died in 2006, before judgment.
  - Milan Babić pleaded guilty to persecution in 2004; his 13-year sentence was upheld in 2005.
  - Milan Martić was convicted in 2007; his 35-year sentence stood on appeal in 2008.
  - [COURT]
