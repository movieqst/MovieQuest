## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Vimy Ridge, fought from the 9th to the 12th of
April 1917, between the Canadian Corps and the German Sixth Army.

## Introduction

### 1. BATTLE OF VIMY RIDGE
- On Easter Monday, the 9th of April 1917, the four divisions of the Canadian Corps attacked together for the first
  time, against a ridge in northern France that the Germans had held since 1914. *(established)*
- This introduction separates what the records show from what we have reconstructed for the map. Watch the colour of
  each tag. *(reconstruction)*

### 2. The ridge
- Vimy Ridge rises gently from the west and falls steeply on its east side to the Douai plain. Its highest point,
  Hill 145, looks out over the coalfields of Lens and the German rear. *(established)*
- The French lost tens of thousands of men attacking it in 1915. The British took over in 1916 and fought a war
  underground, of mines and craters. In October 1916 the Canadians arrived. *(established)*
- Whoever held the crest could see everything that moved for miles. That is why it mattered. *(established)*

### 3. The plan
- Vimy was the northern part of a larger British offensive at Arras. The Canadians' task was to take the ridge and
  guard the left flank of the Third Army's attack to the south. *(established)*
- Their commander, Lieutenant-General Sir Julian Byng, planned the attack in four stages, each marked on the map by a
  coloured line: Black, Red, Blue and Brown. The whole ridge was to be taken in about six hours. *(established)*
- Every man rehearsed his part on a full-size copy of the battlefield, laid out with tape and flags behind the lines.
  By the day of the attack, all ranks knew just where they had to go. *(established)*

### 4. The guns
- Nine hundred and eighty-three guns and mortars supported the Canadians: one heavy gun for every twenty yards of front.
  *(established)*
- The bombardment began on the 20th of March. From the 2nd of April it rose to a fury the Germans called "the week of
  suffering". More than a million shells fell on the ridge. *(established; the phrase is the Germans' own)*
- A new fuze, the 106, burst shells on contact, cutting the barbed wire that had stopped attacks before. *(established)*

### 5. Underground
- Under the Canadian lines, tunnellers dug eleven subways, almost four miles in all, so that the attackers could reach
  the front unseen and unshelled. Later guides count twelve or thirteen. *(disputed: the count)*
- The Grange Subway, dug by 172 Tunnelling Company of the Royal Engineers, still survives. *(established)*
- Mines were dug under the German line too, but most were never fired: commanders feared new craters would block their
  own men. *(established, Boire)*

### 6. The defenders
- Three German divisions held the ridge: Bavarians at Thélus, Prussians of the 79th Reserve Division at Vimy, and the
  16th Bavarian Division opposite Souchez. *(established)*
- Their front line was a maze of trenches and deep dug-outs. Behind it ran a second line along the crest, and a third on
  the plain. *(established)*
- But their reserve divisions waited fifteen miles back, too far to help in the first hours. *(established)*

## The reenactment (9–12 April)

1. **The night before** · 8 April, night
   Easter Sunday night. Fifteen thousand men of the first wave file out into no man's land, into shell-holes and ditches,
   some within a hundred yards of the German outposts. By four o'clock every man is in place, with a hot meal and a rum
   ration. *(established)*

2. **Zero hour** · 9 April, 5:30
   At half past five the guns open all at once. Snow and sleet blow from behind the Canadians into the Germans' faces.
   Twenty-one battalions rise and walk forward behind a curtain of shells that moves a hundred yards every three minutes.
   *(established)*

3. **The Black Line** · 6:15
   Many defenders are still in their dug-outs when the Canadians reach the first trench. By a quarter past six the 1st and
   2nd Divisions hold the Black Line. The 2nd Canadian Mounted Rifles pull a hundred and fifty surprised Germans out of a
   single tunnel. *(established)*

4. **Milne and Sifton** · morning
   Private William Milne of the 16th Battalion crawls up to a machine-gun that is holding up his company and puts it out
   of action. Lance-Sergeant Ellis Sifton of the 18th leaps into a German trench, takes a hidden gun, and holds off a
   counter-attack alone. Both win the Victoria Cross. Both are killed that day. *(established)*

5. **The Red Line and La Folie Farm** · 7:00 to 8:00
   The 3rd Division crosses the crest and finds La Folie Farm a heap of rubble. In the south, the 21st Battalion finds two
   German headquarters in a cave under Les Tilleuls. By eight o'clock the right of the corps is on the Red Line.
   *(established)*

6. **Hill 145** · 9 April, morning
   In the north, the 4th Division meets disaster. A stretch of German trench left untouched by the guns cuts down half
   the 87th Battalion's first wave. Germans emerge from tunnels behind the attackers, and machine-guns on The Pimple rake
   the slope. *(established)*
   Who asked for that trench to be spared is disputed: the official history blames the 87th's commander; other
   historians point to Brigadier-General Odlum's own notes. *(disputed)*

7. **Thélus** · late morning
   The reserve brigades pass through to the Blue Line. Thélus falls exactly as the men had rehearsed it. As the snow
   stops, the sun breaks through. In the official historian's words: "the Germans saw that the Ridge was lost, the
   Canadians knew that it was won." *(established; the quotation is the historian's)*

8. **The Brown Line** · early afternoon
   The last stage goes down the steep east face into Farbus Wood and the Bois de la Ville, with a downhill bayonet charge
   against guns firing point-blank. A Bavarian regimental commander and his staff are captured. By early afternoon three
   divisions hold their final objectives, on schedule. *(established)*

9. **The cavalry** · 4:20 p.m.
   Byng sends patrols of the Canadian Light Horse, about twenty men, toward Willerval on the plain. Machine-guns cut them
   down, but German reports of a strong force of cavalry breaking through cause alarm all along the line. *(established)*

10. **MacDowell** · 9 April
    On Hill 145, Captain Thain MacDowell of the 38th Battalion and two runners, Kobus and Hay, find a deep German dug-out.
    MacDowell convinces the men inside that he has a large force behind him. Two officers and seventy-five men surrender
    to three Canadians. He wins the Victoria Cross. *(established; the details are MacDowell's account)*

11. **Hill 145 falls** · 10 April, 3:15 p.m.
    The 10th Brigade, held back for The Pimple, is sent in instead. The 44th and 50th Battalions charge down the eastern
    slope and take the summit. Private John Pattison of the 50th destroys a machine-gun post and wins the Victoria Cross.
    *(established)*

12. **The Pimple** · 12 April, 5:00
    In a gale of snow and sleet, the 44th, 50th and 46th Battalions attack The Pimple, held by fresh Prussian Guards. By
    six o'clock it is taken. At daybreak the sky clears, and the Canadians look out over the Douai plain and the suburbs
    of Lens. *(established)*

13. **The cost** · 9–14 April
    The whole ridge is in Canadian hands. More than four thousand prisoners and fifty-four guns are taken. The Canadian
    Corps loses 10,602 men, 3,598 of them killed. *(established)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- The Germans fell back to a new line on the plain. The ridge stayed in Allied hands for the rest of the war.
  *(established)*
- Vimy was a local victory inside a larger battle at Arras that did not break through. Four months later the Canadians
  were still fighting near Lens. *(established)*
- For the first time, all four Canadian divisions had fought together. Arthur Currie, who led the 1st Division, soon
  commanded the whole Corps. *(established)*
- In Canada, Vimy became a symbol of the nation's coming of age. Historians debate how much of that was the battle, and
  how much the meaning given to it later. *(disputed)*
- On Hill 145 today stands the Canadian National Vimy Memorial, with the names of more than eleven thousand Canadians
  killed in France who have no known grave. *(established)*
