# Vimy Ridge — development of trench and underground works, 1914–1917

## October–December 1914: improvised positions
- After the front stabilised in Artois, soldiers began connecting individual firing positions into defensive trenches.
- Representative features: short shallow earthworks, parapets, firing steps, improvised shelters, early wire obstacles.
- Visual state: much farmland still recognisable; limited cratering. The precise early Vimy trench footprint requires period maps.

## January–September 1915: increasingly continuous defences
- Front, support and communication trenches expanded; wire, traverses, revetments, shelters and machine-gun positions were improved.
- Major French attacks in Artois in May and September 1915 reinforced the strategic importance of the ridge.
- Visual state: connected zigzag lines, damaged farms and roads, widening shell-damaged ground.

## Late 1915–mid-1916: mine warfare and crater landscape
- French, British and German tunnellers excavated offensive and defensive galleries, listening posts and countermine works.
- Underground charges damaged surface positions and produced large craters. These must be dated and located from plans, not guessed.
- In May 1916, a German attack seized significant British positions in the Vimy sector.
- Visual state: mine craters, disrupted trenches, crater-lip defences and rebuilt communications.

## Autumn–December 1916: Canadian sector and subway construction
- Canadian formations took over the Vimy sector in autumn 1916.
- Royal Engineers tunnelling companies developed communication subways and other underground facilities in preparation for the offensive.
- Veterans Affairs Canada's tunnel brochure describes 13 major subways; secondary sources sometimes give different totals, so keep the definition of 'subway' explicit.
- Visual state: older mining network distinct from newer protected troop-communication subways.

## January–March 1917: assault preparations
- Canadian engineers and British tunnellers improved trenches, underground assembly routes, cable systems, roads, tramways, supply points, artillery positions and medical facilities.
- Training included rehearsals on representations of German positions.
- Visual state: increased logistics density behind the Canadian front; tunnels leading toward assembly positions.

## Late March–9 April 1917: bombardment and attack
- Preparatory artillery damaged wire, parapets, routes and some surface positions, while some deep shelters and strongpoints survived.
- The Canadian attack began at 5:30 a.m. on 9 April 1917 under a creeping barrage.
- Visual state: broken wire, shell holes, smoke, collapsed sections, troops emerging from trenches and subway exits.

## Design cautions
- The timeline is not a simple one-way accumulation: trenches were destroyed, recut, moved and repaired repeatedly.
- German defences on the ridge and Allied assault infrastructure had different purposes.
- Underground mine galleries, countermines, dugouts and communication subways are different engineering categories.
- Do not portray all 1917 trenches as the modern concrete-preserved visitor trenches.
