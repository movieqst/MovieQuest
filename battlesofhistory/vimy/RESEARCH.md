# Vimy Ridge, 9-14 April 1917: research brief

Built only from two texts:

- **Nicholson**: G. W. L. Nicholson, *Canadian Expeditionary Force 1914-1919* (Official History of the Canadian Army in the First World War, Ottawa, 1962). Chapter VIII, "The Battle of Vimy Ridge, 9-14 April 1917", pp. 233-268, plus short passages on pp. 50, 93, 103, 120, 225, 269-270, 501-502. Page numbers are the book's running heads. The OCR text prints p. 264 as a second "262". Here it is cited as p. 264.
- **Boire**: Michael Boire, "The Underground War: Military Mining Operations in Support of the Attack on Vimy Ridge, 9 April 1917", *Canadian Military History* 1, 1 (1992), pp. 15-23 (journal page numbers), with notes on p. 23 and on the following unnumbered page, cited as "p. 24 (notes)".

**Tags.** **[est]** = established: stated as fact by the source. **[acct]** = account: rests on one participant or one side's record, and the source names it. **[estim]** = estimate: the source says "about", "some", "approximately" or "estimated". **[disp]** = disputed: the two sources disagree, or one source contradicts itself. Spellings follow the texts. Nicholson prints "Thelus" and "Zwolfer-Graben" without accents. "Odium" and "Home" are OCR errors for Odlum and Horne.

## 1. The ground

### The ridge as a whole
- The high ground between the Scarpe and the smaller Souchez River, with the Ridge as its dominant feature, "formed a nine-mile barrier across the western edge of the Douai plain". It overlooks Lens to the north, Douai to the east and Arras to the south. Nicholson calls it "tactically one of the most important features on the entire Western Front". (Nicholson p. 244) [est]
- On the 1915 French offensive Nicholson describes Vimy Ridge differently: it "formed a barrier five miles long across the salient" that the German Sixth Army held between Lens and Arras. That salient was "seven miles wide and four deep". (Nicholson p. 93) [est]. The 9-mile figure covers the whole Scarpe-Souchez high ground. The 5-mile figure is the ridge across the 1915 salient.
- The Ridge was "the keystone of the defences linking the new Hindenburg system to the main German lines leading north from Hill 70 to the Belgian coast." (Nicholson p. 244) [est]
- From the Lorette spur, north of the Souchez, "more of the war could be seen than from any other place in France". (Nicholson p. 244) [acct]. Endnote 37 credits the remark to Lt.-Col. D. E. Macintyre, formerly Brigade Major of the 4th Infantry Brigade.
- Canadian frontage: **7000 yards**, the centre opposite the village of Vimy, "which lay on the east side of the Ridge" (Nicholson pp. 245-246). Boire also gives "the 7,000 yards of Corps frontage" (Boire p. 20). [est]
- The ground taken on 9-10 April was "the whole of the main part of Vimy Ridge on a length of 7000 yards and to a maximum depth of 4000 yards". (Nicholson p. 261) [est]

### Heights
- The crest was formed by two heights. **Hill 135** ("measured in metres") lies "immediately north of the village of Thelus". **Hill 145** lies "two miles farther north-west". (Nicholson p. 245) [est]
- **The Pimple**: "an independent height of 120 metres at the north end of the Ridge" (Nicholson p. 245). Beyond Hill 145 the Ridge extended to "The Pimple", west of Givenchy, "whence the ground fell quickly to the valley of the Souchez" (Nicholson p. 245). The Pimple had "complete command of the Souchez defile" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]
- Hill 145 was "the highest and most important feature of the whole of Vimy Ridge" (Nicholson p. 258). Boire: "Of all the features making up the ridge, Hill 145 was the highest, tactically the most important and certainly the most heavily fortified" (Boire p. 22). [est]
- Opposite the Canadian right there was "a gradual descent from Hill 135 to the headwaters of the Scarpe, northwest of Arras, with only a few villages and copses breaking the wide expanse of open fields". (Nicholson p. 245) [est]
- Hill 65 lies north of the Souchez. It is "a commanding height ... overlooking the western part of Lens" (Nicholson p. 269). It is outside the April battle area. [est]
- The source map (Map 7) is layer-tinted at 80/100/120/140 m. (sources/README.md, not a text source)

### West slope and east face
- "The western slopes facing the Allied lines rose gradually over open ground which afforded excellent fields of fire for small arms and artillery." German histories complain that "their positions on the narrow forward slope of the Ridge were fully visible to the Canadians". (Nicholson p. 245) [est; the German complaint is one side's view]
- "The reverse slope dropped sharply into the Douai plain, its thick woods providing adequate cover for the enemy's guns." (Nicholson p. 245) [est]
- The opposing front lines "ran almost due north and south, while the Ridge angled to the south-east". (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- 4th Division sector: the assault was "never more than 800 yards deep in any battalion sector, it was uphill all the way". (Boire p. 22) [est]
- The Grange Tunnel was dug into "the compacted flint and chalk of an Artois hill". Four crater groups had been "blown in clay and as such were always full of water" (Boire pp. 15, 20-21). The first Pimple mine tunnel ran "through a layer of blue clay" (Boire p. 22). [est]
- Several deep caverns were "left from chalk-quarrying operations of an earlier day" (Nicholson p. 250). The Pimple was "built into the chalk quarries of Givenchy-en-Gohelle" (Boire p. 22). [est]

### Villages, woods, farms
- **Vimy**: east side of the Ridge (Nicholson p. 246). **Farbus**: on the east side of the Ridge, "2000 yards south of Vimy" (Nicholson p. 246). **Thelus**: on the western slope, a fortified position (Nicholson p. 246). **Givenchy-en-Gohelle**: below the Pimple, which lies "west of Givenchy" (Nicholson p. 245). Not to be confused with Givenchy-lez-la-Bassee (Nicholson p. 245n). [est]
- **La Folie Farm**: "midway between Hills 135 and 145", with the adjacent **Ecole Commune** (Nicholson p. 246). It lies south-east of Hill 145 (Nicholson p. 245). [est]
- **Les Tilleuls**: "battered hamlets ... immediately west of Thelus", just east of the Lens road (Nicholson p. 255). [est]
- Woods: **Bois de la Folie** (3rd Division objective); **Bois de Bonval** and **Count's Wood** "overlooking the village of Vimy"; **Bois de la Ville**; **Farbus Wood**; **Bois du Goulot** (also printed "Bois de Goulot"); **Bois de Givenchy**; **Bois en Hache** (across the Souchez on the Lorette ridge); **Bois de l'Hirondelle** (south bank of the Souchez). (Nicholson pp. 245, 247, 254-257, 262-263, 265) [est]
- Bombardment: "The villages of Thelus, Farbus and Givenchy were systematically destroyed so as to deny them to the enemy." (Nicholson p. 251) [est]

### Roads and railways
- **Arras-Lens road** (also "Lens road"). The Zwischen-Stellung paralleled it. By 9 April it was "now unrecognizable". (Nicholson pp. 246, 254) [est]
- **Lens-Arras railway embankment**: patrols were to push "a further 500 yards or more" beyond the Brown Line to reach it (Nicholson p. 248). The 1st and 2nd Divisions advanced to it on the evening of 13 April (Nicholson p. 264). [est]
- **Willerval-Vimy road**: 1st Brigade machine-gunners above Farbus Wood engaged enemy transport on it (Nicholson p. 257). **Givenchy-Souchez road**: a German pocket held out astride it on 12 April (Nicholson p. 263). **Vimy-Lens railway** (Nicholson p. 269). **Lens-Bethune railway** (Nicholson p. 233). [est]
- Engineers' communications: "more than 25 miles of road" repaired and maintained, including three miles of new plank road. "A system of twenty miles of tramway" was reconditioned and extended. It carried "800 tons or more" a day, with about 300 push trucks for wounded. (Nicholson p. 249) [est]
- Canadian railway units "laid steel up to the forward trenches". As the infantry advanced, tramways "were pushed forward over the newly consolidated ground." (Nicholson p. 489) [est]

### Zouave Valley
- Zouave Valley was the "southern offshoot" of the valley of the Souchez, "which ran behind the 4th Division's front". German watchers on Hill 145 could observe all movement in it, and "any activity in Zouave Valley invited a heavy barrage" (Nicholson pp. 258-259). The 16th Bavarian Division planned, then postponed, a gas-shell attack meant to gain "complete command of Zouave Valley" (Nicholson p. 259). On 12 April the 10th Brigade moved up "from their rest area behind Zouave Valley" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]

### State of the ground and weather
- After the bombardment the area was "a pock-marked wilderness of mud-filled craters" (Nicholson p. 251). Ground on 9 April: "deep shell-holes ... a maze of shattered trenches and the torn remnants of wire entanglements ... great mine craters from former operations presented insuperable obstacles that had to be bypassed ... the continuous shelling had pulverized the earth into vast puddles of clammy mud" (Nicholson p. 253). [est]
- The 38th Battalion's ground "was so badly cut up that the men could not keep up with the barrage; some of the wounded fell into water-filled shell-holes and were drowned." (Nicholson p. 260) [est]
- A German communication trench was "knee-deep in mud" (Nicholson p. 255, from a German account) [acct]. A German battalion lost men "abandoning their short field boots in the mud" on the night of 9-10 April (Nicholson p. 260). [est]
- Tanks "could not negotiate the heavily shelled ground"; the mud was "gripless" (Nicholson pp. 248, 254). [est]
- **Weather, 8 April (Easter Sunday)**: "A sharp frost on the previous evening had hardened the mud. The day had been fine and clear, but now, as night fell, the barometer was dropping and the air was turning much colder." (Nicholson p. 252) [est]
- **Night of 8-9 April**: "a moon just past the full, but the sky clouded later" (Nicholson p. 252). "Shortly before dawn ... a driving north-west wind swept the countryside with snow and sleet." The RFC bombing programme was abandoned. The attackers had the wind at their backs and in the defenders' faces, and darkness lasted beyond zero hour (Nicholson pp. 252-253). [est]
- **9 April, later**: after the first phase "the wind had turned, driving snow across the front and carrying smoke southward from Thelus" (Nicholson p. 255). Near the Blue Line there was "an unexpected break in the stormclouds and spring sunshine flooded the air". Then "early in the afternoon the sleet began again, and the day closed with intermittent snow flurries driven before a gusty westerly breeze" (Nicholson pp. 256-257). [est]
- **11-12 April**: "On 11 April the weather deteriorated rapidly". Early on the 12th "a strong westerly gale was raging, driving sleet and snow towards the enemy's positions". "The sky cleared at daybreak." (Nicholson pp. 262-263) [est]
- Water: 50,000 horses were concentrated "where very little water existed". This needed reservoirs, pumping and "45 miles of pipe lines" for a daily requirement of 600,000 gallons (Nicholson pp. 249-250). [est]

## 2. Trenches over time

### Germans take the ridge, 1914
- "Together with the Lorette spur, north of the Souchez, the Ridge had fallen to the Germans in October 1914." (Nicholson p. 244) [est]

### The French, 1915
- The 1915 Allied plan: the French Tenth Army would strike "eastward between Arras and Lens to capture the heights of Vimy and dominate the whole Douai region". (Nicholson p. 50) [est]
- **May 1915**: after a five-day bombardment, eight French assaulting divisions attacked at mid-morning on 9 May. Bad weather had postponed the attack two days. "In the centre a corps of three divisions under General Henri-Philippe Petain ... broke through the German defences to a depth of two and a half miles, and almost gained the crest of the Ridge." Reserves "held seven miles to the rear" arrived too late. The fighting continued until 15 May and resumed a month later for four days. French casualties were "more than 100,000, the Germans having lost about three quarters of that number". (Nicholson p. 93) [est; German loss is an estimate]
- In June Foch renewed his offensive against Vimy Ridge (Nicholson p. 103). [est]
- **September 1915**: on 25 September seventeen divisions of the French Tenth Army attacked on a front "which extended twelve miles northward from Arras to include the area of the unsuccessful May offensive against Souchez and Vimy Ridge". The Tenth Army had 35 heavy guns per mile. "One division reached the crest of Vimy Ridge on 28 September, but was driven back." (Nicholson p. 120) [est]
- Summary: in 1915 French forces "recaptured most of the Lorette feature and gained a small, temporary footing on Vimy Ridge". The enemy held and improved his position, "and from it his sappers extensively undermined the French positions at the foot of the western slopes." (Nicholson pp. 244-245) [est]
- The French attacks of 1915 "had failed partly because of the speed with which the Germans emerged from such shelters [deep dug-outs] after the opening bombardment". (Nicholson p. 252) [est]
- Boire: the first battle of Artois in 1915 saw "the French 10th Army begin a slow, stubborn year-long advance up the ridge" (Boire p. 15). [est]

### The British, 1916
- **Takeover date [disp]**: Nicholson says "British forces took over the Vimy sector in March 1916" (Nicholson p. 245). Boire says the French advance "ended in February 1916 when, urgently needed to stem the German onslaught at Verdun, it was relieved by the 17th Corps of the British Third Army, commanded by Lieut.-General Sir Julian Byng" (Boire p. 15).
- **Mine war [disp in emphasis]**: Nicholson says "tunnelling companies of the Royal Engineers forced the Germans to abandon their mining operations. But above ground the enemy still held the upper hand" (Nicholson p. 245). Boire says the newly arrived British "faced a hurricane of mine explosions which were carefully, violently and successfully orchestrated by the German defenders to throw the newly arrived force back down the slopes of the ridge" (Boire p. 15). Boire also says that in 1916 "the Germans were losing the initiative, and were not to regain it" (Boire p. 19).
- **German attack, May 1916**: "in the latter part of May he seized 1500 yards of British front-line and support trenches." (Nicholson p. 245) [est]. Neither source gives the date 21 May (see section 8).
- In the Arras sector, which included Vimy Ridge, during 1916 "ten Royal Engineer tunnelling companies dug thousands of yards of shafts, exploding well over 100 large mines underneath or close to German positions. The majority of the craters still visible today near the Grange Tunnel were fired during 1916." (Boire p. 19) [est/estim: "thousands of yards", "well over 100"]
- Across the whole BEF front in 1916 the British blew 750 mines and the Germans 696. In 1917 the figures were 117 British and 106 German. (Nicholson p. 502; Boire p. 19) [est]

### The Canadian takeover, October 1916
- After the Somme the Corps moved into Artois "to take over from the 4th British Corps the right sector of the First Army's front. By the morning of 28 October General Byng was holding a ten-mile sector which extended from two miles north of Arras to the same distance north-west of Lens." The 4th Division stayed at the Somme, so Byng had to keep all three remaining divisions in the line. (Nicholson p. 233) [est]
- Until their own artillery returned "towards the end of November", the Canadians had the heavy batteries of the 17th British Corps and the field artillery of the Lahore, 24th and 60th Divisions. (Nicholson p. 233) [est]
- **Arrival date [disp]**: Boire says the British at the base of the ridge were relieved by the Canadian Corps "during the month of October 1916" (Boire p. 15). Later he refers to "the Corps' arrival in December 1916" (Boire p. 21). Nicholson gives 28 October (p. 233).
- Opposing divisions in autumn 1916: the 6th Bavarian Reserve and the 12th Reserve Divisions. (Nicholson p. 233) [est]
- "Early in March [1917] the 24th Division of the 1st Corps took over [Byng's] left from Givenchy-en-Gohelle to Loos, thus setting free the 1st and 2nd Canadian Divisions." (Nicholson p. 246) [est]

### Winter raids, 1916-17
- **20 December 1916**: parties of the 1st Canadian Mounted Rifles, "in all more than 400 strong", raided near the Corps right boundary. They assaulted "from specially constructed galleries leading to craters in no man's land", destroyed 26 dug-outs and a machine-gun emplacement, and took "almost 60 prisoners". (Nicholson p. 233) [est]
- **17 January 1917**: the 20th and 21st Battalions (4th Brigade) raided with "some 860 specially trained all ranks" plus sappers of the 4th Field Company. The target was "three miles east of Lens in the area of the Lens-Bethune railway". The raid went in at 7:45 a.m., when the enemy usually relaxed after "stand down". On an 850-yard front in one hour they blew up more than 40 dug-outs and three ammunition dumps and took 100 prisoners of the 11th Reserve Division. Canadian losses were about 40 killed and 135 wounded. German record: "an extraordinarily forceful undertaking", with losses of 18 dead, 51 wounded, 61 missing. (Nicholson pp. 233-234) [est; German figures are one side's record]. "East of Lens" is as printed and looks geographically odd for the Lens-Bethune railway.
- **13 February 1917**: the 10th Brigade (Brig.-Gen. E. Hilliam) raided the 5th Bavarian Reserve Division. The 44th, 46th, 47th and 50th Battalions each sent a company 200 strong, plus about 70 men of the 10th Field Company and 67th Pioneer Battalion. The raiders inflicted "an estimated 160 casualties (including the capture of more than 50 prisoners), and destroyed dug-outs, mine shafts and barbed wire". Their own losses were "approximately 150". (Nicholson p. 234) [estim]
- **Gas raid, night of 28 February-1 March 1917**: "some 1700 all ranks of the 4th Division (representing from left to right the 73rd, 72nd, 75th and 54th Battalions)" raided Hill 145 to reconnoitre and do damage.
  - There was no preliminary bombardment or wire-cutting.
  - Cylinders of tear gas and chlorine were installed along the whole divisional front. Releasing the tear gas first "served only to alert the enemy". A changing wind prevented use of the chlorine, and the attackers suffered casualties "when German shells breached some of the cylinders".
  - The 12th Brigade parties on the left reached most objectives. The 11th Brigade parties were discovered "before they were well clear of their own wire".
  - Result: 37 prisoners taken. Canadian casualties were 687, "including two battalion commanders killed". "During the next two days the Germans permitted and even helped our troops to recover their dead."
  - The defenders were the 16th Bavarian Infantry Division and the 79th Reserve Division. (Nicholson p. 234) [est]
- **From 20 March**: the Canadians "raided the enemy lines every night", at a cost of "some 1400 casualties in two weeks" (Nicholson p. 234) [estim]. Raids ranged "from small patrols to the 600 all ranks sent out by the 10th Canadian Infantry Brigade on 31 March to investigate the defences in front of the Pimple" (Nicholson p. 251). [est]
- Boire: "By the month of March 1917 every assaulting battalion had sent out many patrols into and across the wire ... losses were sometimes high, especially in 4 Division." (Boire p. 21) [est]

### The German defence system, April 1917
- The defences were built "in accordance with the old doctrine of a rigid defence". Little had been done by April 1917 to provide the new defence in depth. (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Forward zone (First Line)**: "The advanced field works, five to seven hundred yards deep, consisted of three lines of trench, with deep dug-outs for the foremost garrisons." It was interspersed with "a network of concrete machine-gun emplacements woven about with barbed wire", linked by communication trenches and "connecting tunnels". (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Second Line**: east of the Ridge, "a mile to the rear" on the Canadian left and two miles on the right, because the Ridge angled away. It was protected by deep belts of wire. "Its dug-outs were vast fortified underground chambers, some capable of sheltering entire battalions." (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Intermediate Line (Zwischen-Stellung)**: "a single-trench Intermediate Line ... reached southward from the village of Vimy through the western edge of Thelus, paralleling the Arras-Lens road." (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Vimy-Riegel** (British name: Vimy-Lens line): an extension running north from Vimy to cover the west side of Lens. (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Third Line**: "snaked its way southward from Lens over the plain, passing in front of the villages of Mericourt and Oppy to join the Monchy Riegel east of Arras." The First and Third Lines were "at their widest points of separation ... more than five miles apart". (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Wotan-Stellung (Drocourt-Queant Line)**: "Five miles to the east"; "not yet completed" (Nicholson p. 244). A fourth position, "partly constructed", meant to contain any Allied success in the Vimy-Lens area (Nicholson p. 246). It ran "southward through Drocourt (five miles south-east of Lens) to meet the Hindenburg Line at Queant (twelve miles south-east of Arras)" (Nicholson p. 269n). [est]
- **Fortified localities** between the First and Third Lines: Farbus, Thelus, La Folie Farm and the Ecole Commune. (Nicholson p. 246) [est]
- **Named trenches and works**:
  - **Zwolfer-Graben** (Zwölfer-Graben): "About 100 yards beyond the now unrecognizable Arras-Lens road", it "marked the southern end of the Black Line objective in the Canadian sector". (Nicholson p. 254)
  - **Turko-Graben**: "reached northward behind the road" in the 2nd Division's Red Line sector. (Nicholson p. 255)
  - **Thelus Trench**: north of Thelus village. (Nicholson p. 256)
  - **Hangstellung**: "on the reverse slope [of Hill 145] a system of deep dug-outs ... housed the reserve companies". (Nicholson p. 259)
  - **Givenchy Line**: the 12th Brigade's final objective. (Nicholson p. 261)
  - Hill 145's First Line "consisted of two trenches; the rounded summit of the hill itself was ringed with two more"; "the garrison had comparative immunity in deep mine workings". (Nicholson p. 259) [est]
- **Pimple defences**: "Its surface was a maze of trenches, and below ground German engineers had constructed deep dug-outs and tunnels" (Nicholson p. 262). "It bristled with dozens of machine guns whose fire dominated the northern, western and eastern slopes of 4th Division's objective, Hill 145." An "adjacent and mutually supporting enemy position" stood in the Bois de Givenchy, just north of the Pimple. (Boire p. 22) [est]
- **Distances between the lines**: in the 4th Division sector the subways let troops reach "assembly trenches within 150 yards of the German front line" (Nicholson p. 259). On the night of 8-9 April assembly was "completed, in some cases to within a hundred yards of the enemy's outposts" (Nicholson p. 252). Black Line: "average distance of 750 yards from the Canadian front trenches" (Nicholson p. 247). No general no man's land width is given.
- The new German doctrine (December 1916) called for an outpost zone in front of the Main Line of Resistance, then a battle zone "extending 1500 to 2500 yards back to a Second Line". The tactical unit was the eleven-man "group". (Nicholson pp. 239-240) [est]. It was largely not applied at Vimy (Nicholson p. 246).
- German infantry strength: the divisions "had among them only five regiments on the Ridge; and many of their rifle companies were left with only about 75 men each." Each regiment held the First and Second Lines with its forward battalions, a second battalion was in the Third Line or close support, and the third battalions were billeted "about two hours' march from the battlefield". (Nicholson p. 247) [est/estim]

### Craters and crater groups
- **Duffield, Grange and Birkin crater groups**: "the well-manicured remains" near the Grange Tunnel, "a stone's throw away" (Boire p. 15). "The majority of the craters still visible today near the Grange Tunnel were fired during 1916" (Boire p. 19). [est]
- BEF craters "took their name from either nearby trenches, the family names of tunnelling company officers or local infantry commanders." (Boire p. 23, note 1) [est]
- "19 distinct mine crater groups fired by the French, Germans, British and Canadians in the four miles of no man's land separating the combatants in the Vimy sector". (Boire p. 20) [est]
  - A Corps staff analysis concluded "that not one of these 19 groups constituted an insurmountable obstacle to movement".
  - Four groups were to be avoided "as they had been blown in clay and as such were always full of water".
  - The other 15 groups, "most containing several deep craters, were deemed to be 'no obstacle' and as such 'easily traversable by small parties of men'". (Boire pp. 20-21)
- **Kennedy crater group**: on the 12th Brigade's northern flank, where three mines were blown at zero hour (Boire p. 23). See section 3.
- **Broadmarsh crater**: "the older Broadmarsh crater on the boundary between the 3rd and 4th Divisions". Three mines lay under it and were not fired (Boire p. 23). [est]
- Older craters "in the German front line" just south of the Kennedy craters had enemy outposts "built along the lips". Five mines were planned under them and abandoned (Boire p. 23). [est]
- Neither text names any other crater, and neither says who blew the Duffield, Grange, Birkin, Kennedy or Broadmarsh craters or when.

## 3. Tunnels and mines

### Tunnelling organisation (background)
- Tunnelling companies were formed from the winter of 1914-15 from professional miners. They were centralised at Army level under a Royal Engineer colonel titled "Controller of Mines", with an "Inspector of Mines" of general rank at HQ BEF. (Boire pp. 17-18) [est]. Nicholson: from the beginning of 1916 all mining was coordinated by "a Controller of Mines at G.H.Q.", and tunnelling companies were "separated from their corps and employed as army troops" (Nicholson p. 501). [minor disp: Army-level Controller with GHQ Inspector (Boire) vs. Controller at GHQ (Nicholson)]
- 33 tunnelling companies were on the Western Front by summer/autumn 1916: one New Zealand, three Canadian and three Australian among them (Boire p. 18; Nicholson p. 501). The 33 companies "could field 24,000 miner-soldiers" (Boire p. 18). [est]
- A company at full strength could field "500-600 miners", often reinforced by infantry fatigue parties. "With attachments, company strengths were often well into four figures, as was the case with 172 Company while it dug the Grange Tunnel from November 1916 to March 1917." (Boire p. 18) [est]
- Tunnelling rate by 1916: "20 feet a day, soil conditions permitting". Listening devices could detect German tunnelling "up to 100 yards away". The explosive was ammonal, with French *cordeau détonant* ("det cord") for firing. (Boire pp. 18-19) [est]
- German mining declined: "the recall of 125,000 miners from the Army" at the end of 1916, and 50,000 more in June 1917 (Boire p. 19). Nicholson gives "50,000 more followed in May and June 1917" (Nicholson p. 502). [minor disp: June vs May-June]. "From November of 1916 onwards ... more miners could be employed digging infantry subways, which was the case in the Vimy sector" (Boire p. 19). [est]

### The subways
- **Count [disp]**: Nicholson says "Tunnelling companies excavated or extended **eleven subways** of a total length of **almost four miles**, leading to the Canadian front line." (Nicholson p. 250). Boire reports that tunnel guides describe the Grange Tunnel as "part of a **13-tunnel** underground labyrinth" (Boire p. 15). The 13 figure is attributed to today's guides.
- **What was inside** (Nicholson p. 250) [est]:
  - "electrically lit"
  - "25 feet or more underground"
  - telephone cables and water mains, protected from shelling
  - a covered approach for assault troops and reliefs; "safe and speedy evacuation of the wounded"
  - "Chambers cut into their walls housed brigade and battalion headquarters, ammunition stores, and dressing stations"
- **Zivy cave**: the largest of "several deep caverns, left from chalk-quarrying operations of an earlier day". It "had room for an entire battalion". (Nicholson p. 250) [est]. No location is given.
- **Grange Tunnel**: "an infantry subway dug during the winter and spring of 1916-1917 by the soldier-miners of **172 Tunnelling Company, Royal Engineers**". Work parties came "from all four veteran battalions of 3rd Canadian Infantry Division's 7th Brigade: The Black Watch (42 Battalion), The Royal Canadian Regiment, Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry and the Edmonton Regiment (49 Battalion)". It was dug "from November 1916 to March 1917". It is "cool, humid, subterranean ... hurriedly burrowed into the compacted flint and chalk". It protected assault battalions "as they made their final move from their reserve trenches in the rear, forward to their assembly trenches". (Boire pp. 15, 18) [est]. No length is given.
- **4th Division subways**: "six subways leading from communication trenches eight to ten feet deep were tunnelled into the eastern slopes of Zouave Valley. Through them troops could reach assembly trenches within 150 yards of the German front line." (Nicholson p. 259) [est]. They are not named, and no lengths or companies are given.
- On the night of 8-9 April "Some used the newly completed subways to reach the front lines; others, forced to cross open ground, suffered casualties". (Nicholson p. 252) [est]
- Nicholson calls the tunnelling "one of the great engineering achievements of the war" (Nicholson p. 250).
- **Not in either text**: the names of the other subways, their lengths, start and end points, assigning divisions and companies, and any metres-dug totals. Only 172 Tunnelling Company is named.

### Offensive mines for 9 April
- First plan: "26 German strongpoints, immediately in front of the Corps front line, to be attacked with mines". They were to be fired "at Z-hour, the very moment the attack was to be launched", not beforehand. The "Hawthorne" mine at Beaumont Hamel on 1 July 1916 had warned the Germans. (Boire p. 20) [est]
- Infantry commanding officers, backed by formation commanders, "balked at the possibility of the Corps' mining plan adding new and dangerous obstacles". Byng "heeded their concerns". (Boire p. 21) [est]
- **Outcome of the 26 large mines** (Boire p. 21) [est]:

  | outcome | number |
  |---|---|
  | abandoned for technical difficulties | 4 |
  | detonated before 9 April "for defensive purposes" | 2 |
  | "fired as planned" | 5 |
  | "prepared but not used" | 8 |
  | mentioned in early planning but absent from tunnellers' war diaries | 7 |

- **4th Division area**: "four major mining operations had been planned. The most important was abandoned due purely to technical reasons, a second was carried through to completion and achieved the desired results and two were abandoned without significant effect" (Boire p. 22). The detail Boire then gives (below) describes more than four schemes, so his count is unclear. [disp, internal]
  1. **Pimple mine (deep)**: to destroy the Pimple at Z-hour. The tunnel ran through blue clay, "deep under the target" but "dangerously shallow at the tunnel face". "During the first days of construction, a barrage of friendly fire from the Corps' heavy artillery, though aimed at German forward positions on the Pimple, fell far short and landed squarely on top of the tunnel face, collapsing it completely." Work halted. (Boire pp. 22-23)
  2. **Second Pimple mine**: aimed at the "adjacent and mutually supporting enemy position in le Bois de Givenchy, just north of the Pimple". It was halted because it would not be finished in time: "On the morning of the attack, that tunnel was only 100 feet from its target. So near yet so far." (Boire pp. 22-23). Boire says both were "inspired by the 19 great mines" then being laid under Messines Ridge. Boire's counterfactual is that had they been fired, the Pimple "could not have offered organized resistance" (Boire p. 23). That is his opinion.
  3. **Kennedy crater group, three mines, fired at zero hour**: "three mines were laid and blown in the Kennedy crater group on that formation's [12th Brigade's] northern flank. The mines went up as scheduled, burying and killing many German defenders in the front line and wrecking many yards of German mining galleries underneath. With debris from the explosions still falling, the 73rd Battalion (The Black Watch) dashed forward to seize the farthest lips of the newly formed craters which anchored the Brigade's strong defensive flank guard". (Boire p. 23)
  4. **Five mines south of the Kennedy craters**: to be fired under German outposts on the lips of older craters. They were abandoned "in deference to local commanders' misgivings". The 72nd and 38th Battalions charged over this ground. (Boire p. 23)
  5. **Three mines under the Broadmarsh crater** (3rd/4th Division boundary): "not detonated, again due to local commanders' objections". The 54th and 102nd Battalions and the 42nd Battalion "traversed this sector quickly". (Boire p. 23)
- **[disp] How many mines at zero hour opposite the 73rd**: Nicholson says "**Two** mines exploded at zero hour killed many of the garrison opposite the 73rd Battalion; the survivors fled as the Canadians approached." (Nicholson p. 260). Boire says **three** mines in the Kennedy group (Boire p. 23).
- Boire does not identify the other two of the "five fired as planned", or the two fired before 9 April "for defensive purposes" (Boire p. 21).
- The Pimple was instead "masked by an elaborate smoke and shrapnel barrage during the opening phase of the attack" (Boire p. 23, citing Nicholson p. 261). Nicholson: "an effective smoke barrage had combined with the snowstorm to conceal the operations on the left flank from observers on the Pimple" until the smoke cleared (Nicholson p. 261). [est]

### German mining
- The Germans "extensively undermined the French positions at the foot of the western slopes" (Nicholson p. 245). In spring 1916 they directed "a hurricane of mine explosions" at the British (Boire p. 15). The enemy had "mine shafts" in the 10th Brigade raid area, 13 February 1917 (Nicholson p. 234). On 9 April, defenders on Hill 145 "emerged from mine shafts and dug-outs after the attacking wave had passed" (Nicholson pp. 259-260). The Kennedy mines wrecked "many yards of German mining galleries" (Boire p. 23). [est]

### Other underground works on 9 April
- The 2nd C.M.R. captured "150 surprised Saxons" from "a tunnel behind the second trench line" (Nicholson p. 254). In "a large cave under the shell-torn ruins of" Les Tilleuls the 21st Battalion found two German battalion headquarters (Nicholson p. 255). [est]

## 4. The plan

### Planning chronology
- 21 November 1916: corps commanders' conference at First Army HQ. The Canadian Corps then drew up plans for a two-corps operation to recapture the whole position "from the Arras-Lens road to the Souchez River" (Nicholson p. 245). Boire: Corps staff "began planning ... on 21 November 1916" (Boire p. 20). [est]
- December 1916 assumption: the Canadians would assault on the left, "northward from Vimy village" (Nicholson p. 245). [est]
- 19 January 1917: First Army told Byng he would capture "the whole of the main crest". This excluded the Pimple, which "with the Bois en Hache across the Souchez would be assaulted later by another corps". (Nicholson p. 245) [est]
- Early January: a group of British officers, Currie among them, went to Verdun to study Nivelle's December 1916 counter-offensive. Currie then lectured "on the lessons of Verdun". (Nicholson p. 250) [est]
- 5 March: Byng sent Horne his "Scheme of Operations", which became the plan after amendments. (Nicholson p. 247) [est]
- Role: "primarily to form a strong defensive flank for the Third Army's effort" (Nicholson p. 244). Vimy was the northern part of the Battles of Arras. It was fought alongside the First Battle of the Scarpe, an eight-mile Third Army attack, on "the adjoining four miles of front" (Nicholson p. 244). [est]

### Corps organization and strength
- Corps Commander: Lieut.-General Sir Julian Byng. Army Commander: General Sir Henry Horne, First Army. (Nicholson pp. 247, 258)
- All four Canadian divisions plus the 5th British Division (1st Corps), with Canadian and British heavy artillery (Nicholson p. 245). With British attachments the Corps stood at "approximately 170,000 all ranks, of whom 97,184 were Canadians" (Nicholson p. 252n) [estim]. "Fifteen thousand Canadians, the first wave" (Nicholson p. 252). [est]
- "For the first time the four Canadian divisions had attacked together." (Nicholson p. 267) [est]
- Machine-guns: 358 Vickers under Corps. That is 16 Canadian MG Companies and four companies of the British 5th Division at 16 guns each, plus 38 in the 1st Canadian Motor Machine Gun Brigade, excluding Lewis guns (Nicholson p. 253n). Each assaulting battalion had two machine-guns attached under a machine-gun officer (Nicholson p. 248). [est]
- Tanks: eight, to support the 2nd Division against Thelus. "Much was not looked for." None got beyond the German support trenches (Nicholson p. 248). They were "left behind early in the battle" (Nicholson p. 254). [est]
- Air: No. 16 Squadron RFC was attached to the Canadian Corps (Nicholson p. 251). [est]

### Divisions, south (right) to north (left)
Divisions were "arranged in numerical order from right to left", each assaulting "with two brigades" (Nicholson p. 247).

- "the long line of twenty-one battalions pushed forward" (Nicholson p. 253). [est]
- The 85th Battalion "had arrived in France two months earlier to replace the 12th Brigade's 73rd Battalion" (Nicholson p. 259). The 85th was from Nova Scotia and replaced the Montreal 73rd, which "eventually disappeared" (Nicholson p. 225). [est]
- **British 13th Infantry Brigade** (5th British Division): brought in on the 2nd Division's left "because of the 2nd Division's widening front" for the Blue and Brown phases (Nicholson pp. 248, 256). [est]
- **Flanks**: on the right, the Third Army's 17th Corps, whose 51st Division adjoined the Canadians (Nicholson pp. 247, 257). On the left, the 1st British Corps (24th Division) (Nicholson pp. 246, 262). [est]

### Objective lines
- **Black Line**: "at an average distance of 750 yards from the Canadian front trenches, would mean the capture of the German forward defence zone". On the northern flank it should put the 4th Division "on the summit of Hill 145". (Nicholson p. 247). The Zwolfer-Graben marked its southern end (Nicholson p. 254). [est]
- **Red Line**: "ran north along the Zwischen-Stellung on the Canadian right, turning north-westward beyond the crest of the Ridge to include La Folie Farm and Hill 145." It was the final objective of the 3rd and 4th Divisions (Nicholson p. 247). In the 2nd Division's sector it "ran just east of the Lens road and included the battered hamlets of Les Tilleuls" (Nicholson p. 255). [est]
- **Blue Line**: "included Thelus, Hill 135 and the woods (Bois de Bonval and Count's Wood) overlooking the village of Vimy" (Nicholson p. 247). It was "some 1200 yards" beyond the Red Line, "securing the enemy's intermediate position and the village of Thelus and breaching his second-line defences" (Nicholson p. 248). [est]
- **Brown Line**: "covered the German Second Line, running through Farbus Wood, the Bois de la Ville and the southern part of the Bois de Bonval" (Nicholson p. 247). Because it ran generally south-east, the 1st Division "still had 3000 yards to cover on the right flank, though opposite the 13th Brigade on the left the distance tapered off to nothing" (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- Beyond: patrols to push 500 yards or more to the Lens-Arras railway embankment. The final defence would be an outpost line of machine-guns in the eastern woods, a support line just east of the crest, and a main line of resistance "100 yards down the western slope" with deep dug-outs (Nicholson p. 248). [est]

### Artillery
- Director: Brig.-Gen. E. W. B. Morrison, Corps G.O.C. Royal Artillery (Nicholson p. 248).
- Guns available to the Canadian Corps (Nicholson p. 248) [est]:
  - the 1st and 2nd Canadian Heavy Artillery Groups plus nine British heavy groups
  - "245 heavy guns and howitzers"
  - field artillery of seven divisional artilleries and eight independent field brigades: "480 eighteen-pounders and 138 4.5-inch howitzers"
  - with the 1st British Corps but also supporting: 132 more heavies and 102 field pieces
  - "a few" heavy guns under First Army
- Density: "one heavy gun for every 20 yards of frontage and one field gun for every ten yards". At the Somme it had been one heavy per 57 yards and one field gun per 20 (Nicholson p. 248 and note). [est]
- At zero: "the 983 guns and mortars supporting the Canadians" (Nicholson p. 253). [est]
- Ammunition: "more than 42,500 tons in 'bulk'" for each of the two corps, plus "a daily quota of nearly 2500 tons" (Nicholson p. 249). Bombardment: "More than a million rounds of heavy and field ammunition, with a total weight of 50,000 tons" (Nicholson p. 251). [est]
- **Preliminary bombardment**: planned to last two weeks (Nicholson p. 249). It began on **20 March**, with "about half the batteries" silent for the first thirteen days to conceal strength (Nicholson pp. 250-251). The **intensive phase began 2 April**, from guns "drawn up in a great arc extending 22,000 yards from Bully Grenay (north-west of Lens) to the outskirts of Arras" (Nicholson p. 251). The Germans called it **"the week of suffering"** (Nicholson p. 251). [est]
- Daily programme: observed fire on trenches, dug-outs, concrete MG emplacements, strongpoints, dumps and road junctions; harassing fire day and night on approaches; machine-guns firing on targets already shelled, to stop repair. Signal centres were special targets in the later phase. (Nicholson pp. 249, 252) [est]
- **No. 106 fuze**: "Particular attention was paid to cutting the German wire in the forward areas, in which process a new fuse (No. 106), specially designed for use with high explosive shell where splinter effect was required above ground, was to prove highly satisfactory." (Nicholson p. 249) [est]
- No final bombardment: "as zero hour drew near the artillery slackened its fire, and the customary final bombardment just before the assault was dispensed with". The aim was surprise as to the time of attack (Nicholson p. 252). Only the time of attack was kept secret (Nicholson p. 250). [est]
- **Creeping barrage**: "a rolling barrage in front of the infantry in average lifts of 100 yards", with standing barrages ahead of it on known defences (Nicholson p. 249). As fired: "one gun to every 25 yards of front ... fired for three minutes on the enemy's foremost trenches at three rounds a minute, and then lifted 100 yards every three minutes, slowing their rate of fire to two rounds per minute" (Nicholson p. 253). Barrage shrapnel showed as "whitish-grey puffs" (Nicholson p. 254). For the Brown Line only the advanced batteries were in range. They fired "in successive lifts from right to left" to give the right more time (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- Supplementary fire: 18-pounder standing barrages, 4.5-inch howitzer concentrations, heavy barrages, and "the continuous fire of 150 machine-guns, creating a bullet-swept zone 400 yards ahead". This was "on a scale unprecedented in military history". Mortars laid smoke in front of Thelus and Hill 135. Gas and HE fell on German batteries and dumps. (Nicholson p. 253) [est]
- **Counter-battery**: the concept came from the French and was developed on the Somme by the 5th British Corps. It drew on air observation and photographs, ground observers, survey and sound-ranging sections, liaison officers, wireless interception, documents and prisoners (Nicholson p. 249). "Of an estimated 212 guns opposite the Canadian Corps, 83 per cent were located" (Nicholson p. 251). [estim]
- Hill 145: at the CO's request a stretch of German trench in front of the 87th Battalion "had been left undestroyed by the heavy artillery ... who hoped to put it to good use when captured" (Nicholson p. 259). [est]

### Rehearsals and the model
- "a full-scale replica of the battle area was laid out in a field to the rear, constructed and kept up to date by air photographs supplemented by reports of aerial observers." "German trenches were represented by broad white tapes, and flags of various colours identified strongpoints". "A rolling barrage was simulated by mounted officers with flags moving forward at the appropriate pace." Troops rehearsed "from platoon to divisional level" carrying exactly their battle loads. "By the day of the attack all ranks knew just where they would have to go and what to do when they got there." (Nicholson p. 250) [est]
- The 28th and 31st Battalions cleared Thelus "in precisely the same manner as it had been worked out on the practice fields" (Nicholson p. 256, quoting the 28th Battalion narrative). [acct]
- Raids had trained the infantry to follow barrages: "platoons, companies and sometimes entire battalions had followed a barrage" (Boire p. 21). [est]
- **Maps**: each objective was "indicated by a coloured line on the map" (Nicholson p. 247). See Map 7 in `sources/`.

### Logistics and signals
- Signals: 21 miles of cable buried seven feet deep, and 66 miles of unburied wire. Most work was done at night because the area was "in full view of the enemy". (Nicholson p. 250) [est]
- Assembly route marking: "stakes marked with luminous paint" (Nicholson p. 252). [est]
- Cavalry: an order putting the 1st Cavalry Division under Horne was cancelled on 5 April. The rigid timetable "had resulted in the exclusion ... of any participation by the cavalry" (Nicholson p. 258). [est]

### The German side before the battle
- Sixth Army (General von Falkenhausen) had 20 divisions forward and "the equivalent of six to eight in reserve" (Nicholson p. 246). [est]
- Facing the Canadians, south to north (Nicholson pp. 246-247) [est]:
  - **1st Bavarian Reserve Division**: Thelus and Bailleul. In the Arras area since October 1914.
  - **79th Reserve Division**: the Vimy sector. Raised in Prussia, two years on the Russian front, in the Vimy sector from end of February 1917. It included the 263rd Reserve Regiment, whose men are called "Saxons" (p. 254).
  - **16th Bavarian Infantry Division**: opposite Souchez. Formed January 1917. "had so far opposed only Canadian troops".
- Falkenhausen's five reserve divisions were "from ten to twenty-five miles behind the front", "too far away" to counter-attack (Nicholson p. 247). Later Nicholson says "fifteen or more miles" (p. 267). Two near Douai "might have reached Vimy Ridge by rail in the first four hours" (p. 268). [est]
- In total seven German front-line divisions faced First and Third Armies' "fourteen divisions forward and five in close reserve" (Nicholson p. 247). [est]
- On 7 April Falkenhausen thought no British attack would come before the French offensive in Champagne, about 16 April (Nicholson p. 265). [acct]
- The bombardment cut German supply. Ration parties that "had formerly reached the front line in fifteen minutes, now often took six hours". Companies were without fresh food "for two or three days at a time". German accounts cite this as "a major cause of weakness" (Nicholson p. 251). [est; German view]

## 5. The battle, day by day

### 8-9 April night: assembly
- On the evening of 8 April, Easter Sunday, firing died down. Gunners stacked ammunition, fused shells and laid out barrels of water to cool overheated guns. (Nicholson p. 252) [est]
- Infantry moved up after dark. Forward companies filed through lanes in the Canadian wire "into no man's land to occupy the shell-holes and narrow ditches from which they would assault", in strict silence, "in some cases to within a hundred yards of the enemy's outposts". "All units were in position by 4:00 a.m., every man having received a rum ration and a hot meal." (Nicholson p. 252) [est]

### 9 April, Easter Monday
- **5:30 a.m.**: "Promptly at 5:30 on the morning of Easter Monday, 9 April, the attack on Vimy Ridge opened with the thunderous roar of the 983 guns and mortars". Snow and sleet blew from the north-west at the attackers' backs. German S.O.S. rockets drew a "weak and ineffective" response, and the "ill-directed counter-barrage" fell "well behind the attacking troops". (Nicholson p. 253) [est]
- Aircraft "swooped low sounding their klaxon horns" to mark progress in the snowstorm (Nicholson p. 254). [est]
- At zero hour mines were blown under the German front opposite the 73rd Battalion: two according to Nicholson p. 260, three in the Kennedy group according to Boire p. 23. [disp]

**1st Division (right)**
- The forward companies reached the first trench "while most of the surviving defenders were still in their deep dug-outs". They left parties to guard dug-out and tunnel exits for the moppers-up. "Even at the second trench some of the garrison were caught still underground." The main resistance came from snipers and machine-guns. At the **Zwolfer-Graben**, about 100 yards beyond the Arras-Lens road, both brigades met well-sited machine-guns and "losses were heavy". (Nicholson pp. 253-254) [est]
- **Black Line by 6:15**, "forty-five minutes after zero", for both the 1st and 2nd Divisions (Nicholson p. 254). [est]
- **6:45**: the advance resumed with the battalions' rear companies leading. The Intermediate Line was "700 yards ahead, along the far edge of a wide, saucer-like depression". Snow and smoke hid the attack. "Within the first five minutes, what appeared to be a full battalion was seen retreating over the sky-line towards Farbus Wood." (Nicholson p. 255) [est]
- "By shortly after seven o'clock" it held the left half of the Red Line (= Zwischen-Stellung). The exception was 500 yards on the right, where German trenches "turned sharply to the south-east". The 1st Brigade was brought up for these (Nicholson p. 255). [est]

**2nd Division (right centre)**
- Men went "walking, running and occasionally jumping across no man's land". "Opposition stiffened at the second German line." (Nicholson p. 254) [est]
- Red Line phase:
  - The **21st Battalion** passed through Les Tilleuls. In a cave there it found two German battalion headquarters and sent back 106 prisoners. Its own casualties at that stage were 215.
  - The **25th Battalion** seized the Turko-Graben, with two 77-mm guns and eight machine-guns. With 22nd Battalion mop-up parties it took "close to 400 prisoners", at a cost of more than 250 casualties.
  - (Nicholson p. 255) [est/estim]
- **By eight o'clock** both the 1st and 2nd Divisions were reported on the Red Line (Nicholson p. 255). [est]

**3rd Division (left centre)**
- "only light opposition in the first phase". The **2nd C.M.R.** took "150 surprised Saxons of a battalion of the 263rd Reserve Regiment" from a tunnel. Some troops failed to recognise landmarks and ran into their own barrage. **Black Line confirmed by 6:25** (Nicholson pp. 254-255). [est]
- **Crest at 7:30**: the division occupied the western edge of the Bois de la Folie (Nicholson p. 255). Two 8th Machine Gun Company detachments reached the Arras-Lens road ahead of the 1st C.M.R. and inflicted "an estimated 100 casualties". One caught a German battalion headquarters withdrawing toward the Bois de Bonval. According to a German account, "most of the staff and all the men were killed or wounded" (Nicholson p. 255). [estim / acct]
- The 2nd C.M.R. "found La Folie Farm a mass of rubble and overran it without opposition". In the orchards to the north the 7th Brigade met "ineffective local counter-attacks" against the R.C.R. and its junction with the 4th C.M.R. (Nicholson p. 256). [est]
- **About 9:00**: on learning Hill 145 was untaken, the 7th Brigade formed a defensive flank "from the Bois de la Folie, through the former German third trench, to the original front line". The P.P.C.L.I. took over part of the 42nd's front and the 42nd extended left. Both were "harassed by small-arms fire from the top of the hill" all day (Nicholson p. 256). [est]

**Blue Line (1st and 2nd Divisions, 13th British Brigade)**
- **By 9:30** reserve brigades were up on the Red Line (Nicholson p. 256). [est]
- On the extreme right the 1st Battalion met "spirited resistance at the Zwischen-Stellung" and took 125 prisoners. Elsewhere "the leading waves, advancing as if on parade, met remarkably little opposition", and casualties came mainly from shellfire. (Nicholson pp. 256-257) [est]
- **Blue Line**: reported by the 1st Division "shortly after eleven o'clock", and a little later by the 2nd (Nicholson p. 256). [est]
  - The 29th Battalion overran Thelus Trench and the south end of Hill 135.
  - The 28th and 31st Battalions cleared Thelus.
  - Two British battalions went round the northern slopes of Hill 135 to occupy Count's Wood, the Bois de Bonval and the Bois du Goulot.
- Sunshine broke through: "Thus for a fleeting moment was revealed the final issue of the day: the Germans saw that the Ridge was lost, the Canadians knew that it was won." (Nicholson pp. 256-257, quoting Duguid per note 85) [acct: historian's phrase]

**Brown Line**
- After a 90-minute halt the advance went on. The 1st Brigade kept its 3rd and 4th Battalions forward, and the 6th Brigade used the 27th Battalion and a company of the 29th. The 1st and 6th Brigades wheeled half-left. (Nicholson p. 257) [est]
- The 1st and 3rd Battalions' forward companies each carried 24 pairs of wire-cutters, plus wire-breakers fitted to rifles, and got through both belts "without losing the barrage". They overran German batteries whose crews had withdrawn. Patrols set up observation posts on the far edge of Farbus Wood (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- The 27th Battalion painted its unit designation "in green paint on the backs of their prisoners" (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- In the Bois de la Ville, machine-guns and field guns firing point-blank were overcome with rifle grenades "and a downhill bayonet charge". Among 250 captured were "the commander of the 3rd Bavarian Reserve Regiment and his staff" (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- The 13th British Brigade cleared the Bois du Goulot north to south and took nine guns and howitzers. That made **31 guns** taken by the 1st and 2nd Divisions that day (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- At mid-afternoon Griesbach threw back his right flank to keep touch with the 51st Division, "whose attack at the left of the Third Army front had only partly succeeded" (Nicholson p. 257). [est]
- "With the capture of the Brown Line the three divisions on the Canadian Corps right had taken their objectives on schedule." (Nicholson p. 258) [est]

**Cavalry episode**
- **2:40 p.m.**: Byng telephoned First Army suggesting a cavalry regiment exploit the success. GHQ permission was needed and the reply was slow. The British Official History: "Only by immediate exploitation of the situation on the ridge at 7:15 a.m. could such action on the part of the cavalry have been brought within the realms of possibility". (Nicholson pp. 257-258) [est]
- "Shortly after two o'clock" Byng ordered a Canadian squadron to "push on to Willerval". **At 4:20 p.m.** two mounted patrols of the Canadian Light Horse, "totalling some twenty men", set out from Farbus for Willerval, "a scant mile to the east". One patrol captured ten Germans but lost half its men and horses to a machine-gun. The other "was all but wiped out by rifle fire". Half the main body's horses were killed by shelling. German reports that "a strong force of English cavalry had broken through into Willerval" led to a three-battalion counter-attack order. Observers then saw "enemy advancing in three waves" towards Farbus, but no counter-attack developed (Nicholson p. 258). These were reserve battalions rushed from Lille; their regimental commanders used them to fill gaps instead (Nicholson p. 258n). [est/estim]
- In the late afternoon the 9th Cavalry Brigade was put at First Army's disposal, with one squadron to reinforce the Canadian Light Horse (Nicholson p. 258). [est]

**4th Division: Hill 145 (9-10 April)**
- Watson hoped to take the forward slope by surprise and to stop counter-attacks from the Hangstellung or Givenchy with barrages (Nicholson p. 259). [est]
- **11th Brigade**:
  - The **102nd** took its half of the forward slope, and the **54th** passed through to consolidate on the summit.
  - Farther north, the undestroyed German trench in front of the **87th** (spared at its CO's request) poured machine-gun fire that "cut down half the 87th's leading wave and pinned the right of the supporting 75th Battalion to their assembly trenches". Germans also "emerged from mine shafts and dug-outs after the attacking wave had passed", and "murderous fire from the second trench" followed.
  - "The entire left wing of the 11th Brigade's attack broke down." The **54th**, with its flank open, was "forced to withdraw". For a time only the 102nd held its objective, the south-western slope.
  - Because the 102nd was there, the German trench could not be shelled. **At one o'clock** bombing parties of the 87th, with Stokes mortars and machine-guns, took it.
  - **Before dark**, two companies of the **85th** overran "the two remaining trenches on the west side of the summit".
  - (Nicholson pp. 259-260) [est]
- **Why Hill 145 went wrong** (as the texts give it) [est]:
  1. The 87th's trench left unshelled at the CO's request (Nicholson p. 259).
  2. Germans emerging from mine shafts and dug-outs behind the first wave (Nicholson pp. 259-260).
  3. Enfilade from the Pimple once the smoke cleared (Nicholson p. 261). Boire: Hill 145 "was solidly supported from the north by the guns of the Pimple", and the abandoned Pimple mines "might have" changed this (Boire pp. 22-23).
  4. Surprise "more difficult to achieve here than anywhere else on the Vimy front" under German observation (Nicholson p. 259).
- **12th Brigade**:
  - Two (or three, see [disp]) mines at zero hour; the survivors opposite the **73rd** fled, and the 73rd consolidated on the first trench as the left flank.
  - The **72nd** bombed the second trench from a flank after frontal assault failed. The **38th**'s ground was so cut up the men could not keep up with the barrage; some wounded drowned in shell-holes.
  - After the smoke cleared, heavy machine-gun fire from the Pimple stopped further progress. The 72nd "could do no more than gain a footing in the third German trench".
  - The **78th** passed through the 38th, came under fire from Hill 145 and lost its barrage. **About 8:30 a.m.** its few survivors near the final objective "just outside Givenchy" were "counter-attacked and overpowered by a force of some 200 Germans". The German attempt to retake the second trench was beaten off.
  - Late afternoon: two companies of the **46th** took "a number of craters beyond the disputed second trench". "Night fell with the main objectives still untaken."
  - (Nicholson pp. 260-261) [est/estim]
- **6:00 p.m., 9 April**: Watson called on the 10th Brigade, which had been held for the Pimple, to take the 11th Brigade's final Red Line objective (Nicholson p. 261). [est]
- **German counter-attack**: Falkenhausen, "not realizing the extent of the Canadian Corps' success", held his reserve divisions back as reliefs. At **6:00 p.m.** a reserve battalion of the 16th Bavarian Division was ordered to retake the northern slope of Hill 145. It reached the Pimple only at midnight; men lost their way and boots in the mud, and those who got close "were dispersed by the fire of a single machine-gun". A pre-dawn German attempt on the first trench east of the summit was forestalled by the 75th and 85th (Nicholson p. 260). [est]

**Prisoners, 9 April**: "Up to midnight on 9-10 April, about 3400 German prisoners had been counted; and more were still coming in." (Nicholson p. 261) [estim]

### 10 April
- **3:15 p.m.**: the 10th Brigade's two uncommitted battalions were in position on Hill 145. The barrage fired on the Hangstellung the day before was repeated, and "the assaulting waves charged down the eastern slope, the 44th Battalion on the right and the 50th on the left." "The action was a brilliant success, though the 50th Battalion suffered almost 240 casualties." Mopping up yielded "150 unwounded prisoners and a number of machine-guns". Parties of the 44th linked with the 3rd Division's left through the northern end of the Bois de la Folie (Nicholson p. 261). [est/estim]
- The 12th Brigade improved its positions "next morning as German artillery fire dwindled". "At mid-afternoon, when Hill 145 was completely in Canadian hands", it occupied "with relative ease" its final objective, the "Givenchy Line" (Nicholson p. 261). [est]
- On the right the 51st Division reached its objectives and was in touch with the Canadians at the Brown Line (Nicholson p. 261). [est]
- **Two-day casualties**: "Canadian casualties in the two days' fighting totalled 7707, of which 2967 were fatal." 13th British Brigade on Easter Monday: "approximately 380" (Nicholson p. 261). [est/estim]
- Rupprecht's diary, 10 April: "No one could have foreseen that the expected offensive would gain ground so quickly." (Nicholson p. 263) [acct]
- The First Army ordered an advance to the Lens-Arras railway on the 10th; it was postponed to match the flanking corps (Nicholson p. 264). [est]

### 11 April
- The weather "deteriorated rapidly" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]
- The Sixth Army's Chief of Staff was removed as responsible for "this heavy defeat" and replaced by Colonel von Lossberg (Nicholson p. 266). [est]
- Rupprecht accepted the loss of the Ridge "as permanent" and ordered a general withdrawal to the Third Position. Opposite the Canadians it followed "the Oppy-Mericourt line and the Avion Switch, encircling the western suburbs of Lens" (Nicholson p. 263). [est]
- Falkenhausen "belatedly ordered regiments of his 'counterattack' divisions to take over the new German front line" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]

### 12 April: the Pimple and the Bois en Hache
- Plan: First Army split the "Northern" operation between the Canadian Corps (Pimple, 10th Brigade) and the 1st British Corps (24th Division against the Bois en Hache). It was to start within 24 hours of the "Southern" operation, but was postponed 48 hours because the 10th Brigade had been used at Hill 145 (Nicholson p. 262). [est]
- Defenders: "a fresh battalion of the 5th Guard Grenadier Regiment, hastily entrained from the north and thrust into position in front of Givenchy" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]
- Fire support (Nicholson p. 262) [est]:
  - No. 4 (Double) Heavy Artillery Group R.A.
  - five siege batteries of the 1st Corps
  - a "Reserve Divisional Artillery", formerly the Lahore Divisional Artillery, standing in for the "as yet non-existent 4th Canadian divisional artillery"
  - On the eve, a special R.E. company fired "more than forty gas drums" into Givenchy, "killing a number of Germans in the cellars". This was the first use of **Livens projectors** in a Canadian operation: "a 50-lb drum containing 30 lbs of mustard gas distances of from 500 to 1300 yards" (Nicholson p. 262 and note).
- Weather: "a strong westerly gale was raging, driving sleet and snow towards the enemy's positions" (Nicholson p. 262). [est]
- **5:00 a.m.**: "as nearly a hundred field guns opened fire on the German front trench, the infantry advanced". Frontage was a thousand yards: from right to left the 44th, 50th and 46th Battalions. Heavy mud kept the men from staying with the barrage. The 44th and 50th took the first two trenches against little opposition, "so unexpected was an attack in such weather". The 46th, on the extreme left, "suffered 108 casualties—about half the strength of the two companies involved". There was hand-to-hand fighting with Guards in shell-holes and dug-outs (Nicholson pp. 262-263). [est/estim]
- **By six o'clock** the right and centre were on their objectives (Nicholson p. 263) [est]:
  - The **44th** was on the Pimple; its Lewis gunners "had cut down 100 of the enemy trying to escape down the hillside into Givenchy".
  - "Five hundred yards to the north the **50th** Battalion held the shattered stumps of the Bois de Givenchy".
  - "In another two hours" the **46th** reported the left flank secure, with machine-guns on the south bank of the Souchez.
- "The sky cleared at daybreak, and the Canadians found themselves overlooking the Douai plain and the south-western suburbs of Lens." A small German pocket astride the Givenchy-Souchez road, between the 44th and the 73rd, withdrew within 24 hours (Nicholson p. 263). [est]
- The **73rd British Brigade** (24th Division) took the Bois en Hache, "driving the Germans from the edge of the Lorette spur" (Nicholson p. 263). [est]
- The Germans completed their withdrawal to the Third Position on the night of 12-13 April, leaving light rearguards and enough guns "to simulate normal activity" (Nicholson p. 263). [est]

### 13-14 April
- Evening of 13 April (Nicholson p. 264) [est]:
  - The 1st and 2nd Divisions advanced to the line of the Lens-Arras railway.
  - The 3rd Division reached "the line Vimy-Petit Vimy-La Chaudiere".
  - The 4th Division occupied Givenchy.
  - The 1st Corps reached Angres and the 17th Corps reached Bailleul.
- 14 April: First Army advanced "another thousand yards on a nine-mile front, opposed only by small rearguards". The enemy abandoned many guns and much ammunition. Canadian gunners trained on German guns over the winter, and by the evening of 14 April "nine captured pieces, ranging from 8-inch howitzers to 77-mm. guns" were in action, "bombarding the Germans in Avion and Mericourt with their own gas shells" (Nicholson pp. 264-265). [est]
- Roads: "Even with 5000 men working on them, these would not be passable for several days." (Nicholson p. 263) [estim]
- 14 April afternoon: Byng ordered a main line of resistance "running through Willerval and La Chaudiere to the Bois de l'Hirondelle (on the south bank of the Souchez)". It was held "virtually unchanged until the autumn of 1918" (Nicholson p. 265). [est]

### Totals
- "In six days the Canadian Corps had advanced some 4500 yards and seized 54 guns, 104 trench mortars and 124 machine-guns ... capturing more than 4000 prisoners." (Nicholson p. 265) [est/estim]
- **Canadian casualties, 9-14 April: 10,602 all ranks, 3598 of them fatal** (Nicholson p. 265; repeated p. 221 as "10,602 in six days"). Two-day figure: 7707, 2967 fatal (p. 261). [est]
- German casualties for Vimy alone are **not given**. Only raid figures and fragments appear (see section 2).
- Third Army, First Battle of the Scarpe (Nicholson pp. 265-266) [est]:
  - Easter Monday advances of 2000 to 6000 yards; Allenby reported 5600 prisoners and 36 guns that evening.
  - 8238 casualties in the first three days, "about 700 less than those suffered by the Canadian Corps over the same period".
  - "more than 7000 prisoners and 112 guns".
  - Monchy-le-Preux was taken on the 11th. Haig halted the offensive on 15 April.
- Ludendorff: the news "spoiled Ludendorff's 52nd birthday. 'I had looked forward to the expected offensive with confidence', he wrote, 'and was now deeply depressed.'" (Nicholson p. 265) [acct]

### The German view and the inquest
- German blame (Nicholson pp. 267-268) [est]:
  - The Sixth Army misjudged the strength and frontage of the attack.
  - Its artillery was "slow and inadequate"; heavy batteries in Douai were not used.
  - Communications and supply broke down.
  - Above all, it held its counter-attack divisions "fifteen or more miles from the battlefield to avoid their being shelled", against Ludendorff's doctrine.
- Falkenhausen had planned to bring forward counter-attack formations "on the evening of the second day of the battle" (Nicholson p. 267). Ludendorff and Rupprecht had urged moving reserves closer but gave no firm order (Nicholson p. 268). [est]
- Lossberg's report blamed the Army Group "for the initial fiasco at Vimy" (Nicholson p. 268). [acct]
- Within a fortnight Falkenhausen became Governor General of Belgium. General Otto von Below succeeded him (Nicholson p. 268). [est]
- Horne credited "soundness of plan, thoroughness of preparation, dash and determination in execution, and devotion to duty on the part of all concerned" (Nicholson pp. 266-267). [acct]
- Nicholson: Vimy "was and remains a classic example of the deliberate attack against strong prepared positions". The Corps proved it could pass "readily from swift and sustained assault to aggressive and concerted defence" (Nicholson p. 267, the latter quoting Duguid). [est / historian's judgement]

- The **date 21 May 1916** for the German attack. Nicholson says only "in the latter part of May" and "1500 yards" (p. 245). Also absent: the attack's name, units and the British units involved.
- **Named subways other than the Grange**. Names that other works may give (unverified here): Goodman, Cavalier, Tottenham, Coburg, Gobron, Blue Bull, Vincent, Lichfield, Douai, Bentata, Souchez, Clarence, Hunter, Gordon, etc. None of these names appears. Also absent: per-subway length, start and end points, and the division/brigade/company for each.
- **Tunnelling companies** other than 172 RE. Absent: 176, 182, 185 RE; 1st/3rd Canadian Tunnelling Companies at Vimy; Australian electrical and mechanical units. Also absent: men employed and metres or yards dug at Vimy.
- Which of Boire's five mines "fired as planned" were outside the Kennedy group, and the two mines fired before 9 April "for defensive purposes". Also absent: charge weights for any Vimy mine.
- Crater names other than Duffield, Grange, Birkin, Kennedy and Broadmarsh (names to check elsewhere: Momber, Longfellow, Durand, Patricia, etc.). Robinson's *The Durand Mine* is cited by Boire (note 32) but its content is not reproduced. Also absent: who blew the named craters and when.
- **German casualties** for Vimy Ridge as a whole; German regimental names other than the 263rd Reserve, 3rd Bavarian Reserve and 5th Guard Grenadier Regiments; the 79th Reserve Division's other regiments; German commanders below Army level (e.g. the Group Vimy commander).
- **Any Vimy VC beyond Milne, Sifton and MacDowell**. A fourth (Pattison) is often cited elsewhere and should be verified from another source; neither text mentions him. Full VC citations are also absent.
- Exact heights of Hill 145 and Hill 135 beyond the names "(measured in metres)"; the length of the ridge proper in miles beyond the 5-mile (1915 salient) and 9-mile (Scarpe-Souchez high ground) figures.
- Sunrise time on 9 April, temperature figures, and casualties per division or battalion, except where given above (21st: 215; 25th: >250; 50th: ~240; 46th: 108).
- **Divisional frontages** for the 3rd and 4th Divisions and the exact divisional boundaries.
- The model's location; the names of training areas; maps issued to troops.
- Artillery details: German gun numbers beyond "an estimated 212" opposite the Corps; the 18-pounder/howitzer barrage table by phase; machine-gun barrage positions.

- Post-war crater preservation, the Canadian Memorial Park, and the reconstruction of the Grange trenches. Boire mentions only "well-manicured" craters and "concrete sandbags" (pp. 15, 22).

## Contradictions and points to watch

1. **Number of subways**: Nicholson: "eleven subways ... almost four miles" (p. 250). Boire: the Grange is part of a "13-tunnel underground labyrinth", per today's tunnel guides (p. 15).
2. **Mines blown at zero hour on the 4th Division's left**: Nicholson: "Two mines" (p. 260). Boire: "three mines ... in the Kennedy crater group" (p. 23).
3. **Canadian arrival**: Nicholson: front held by 28 October 1916 (p. 233). Boire: "October 1916" (p. 15) and also "the Corps' arrival in December 1916" (p. 21).
4. **British takeover from the French**: Nicholson: March 1916 (p. 245). Boire: February 1916, by Byng's 17th Corps (p. 15).
5. **1916 mine war**: Nicholson has British tunnellers forcing the Germans to abandon mining (p. 245). Boire has the British facing a German "hurricane of mine explosions" (p. 15), though he says the Germans were losing the initiative by year's end (p. 19).
6. **Boire's 4th Division count**: "four major mining operations" (p. 22). The detail describes two Pimple mines, the Kennedy mines, five mines south of Kennedy and three under Broadmarsh (pp. 22-23).
7. **Ridge length**: 5 miles (Nicholson p. 93, 1915 salient) vs. a 9-mile barrier (Nicholson p. 244, Scarpe-Souchez high ground).
8. **Barrage density**: Nicholson gives "one field gun for every ten yards" overall (p. 248) and "one gun to every 25 yards" for the main field barrage (p. 253). These are different measures, not a conflict.
9. **German reserve distance**: Nicholson gives both "ten to twenty-five miles" (p. 247) and "fifteen or more miles" (p. 267).
10. **Page numbering**: the OCR prints p. 264 as a second "262". This brief cites it as p. 264.
