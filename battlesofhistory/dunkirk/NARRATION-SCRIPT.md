Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about Dunkirk, where in nine days at the end of May 1940 more than three hundred thousand British and French soldiers were rescued from the beaches and harbour of Dunkirk in France.

## **intro-1**: DUNKIRK, 1940
- In nine days at the end of May 1940, more than 338,000 British and French soldiers were carried across the Channel from the harbour and beaches of Dunkirk.
- It was a defeat: the army left its guns, tanks and lorries behind. It was also a rescue that let Britain fight on.

## **intro-2**: The trap
- On 10 May the German Army invaded the Netherlands, Belgium and France. The British and the best French armies advanced into Belgium to meet it.
- The main German blow came further south, through the Ardennes. Its tanks crossed the Meuse at Sedan and raced west to the Channel coast, which they reached on 20 May.
- The armies in Belgium were cut off from the rest of France: the British Expeditionary Force, the French First Army and the Belgian Army, with their backs to the sea.

## **intro-3**: The armies
- The British Expeditionary Force, under Lord Gort, had about 390,000 men in France, of whom around 250,000 were in the fighting area in the north.
- Beside them stood the French First Army and the Belgian Army. Against them came two German army groups, with most of the tank divisions in the west.

## **intro-4**: The ships
- From his headquarters in the cliffs at Dover, Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay planned the evacuation. Its code name was Operation Dynamo.
- The Admiralty expected to lift perhaps 45,000 men in two days before the Germans closed in.
- Destroyers, minesweepers, ferries and paddle steamers would do most of the work, joined later by hundreds of small boats: the little ships.

## **event-1**: The tanks reach the sea
20 May. German tank divisions that broke through at Sedan reach the Channel coast near Abbeville. The Allied armies in Belgium are cut off from the rest of France.

**explain-1**: The Allied plan had sent the best British and French troops forward into Belgium. The German main effort went through the wooded Ardennes, which the French thought too difficult for tanks, and cut in behind them.

## **event-2**: Counter-attack at Arras
21 May. British tanks and infantry strike south of Arras into Rommel’s 7th Panzer Division. The attack is beaten off, but it shakes the German command.

**explain-2**: Only two tank battalions took part, many of their tanks slow Matildas armed with machine guns. Rommel reported being attacked by hundreds of tanks. The scare fed the German caution of the following days.

## **event-3**: The halt order
24 May. With Boulogne taken and Calais surrounded, the German tanks stop on the Aa canal, about 25 km from Dunkirk. They wait there for three days.

**explain-3**: Rundstedt, commanding Army Group A, halted the tanks to rest and repair them, and Hitler confirmed the order. Göring promised the Luftwaffe could finish the trapped armies alone. Why the order was given is still argued about.

## **event-4**: Back to the coast
25–28 May. Gort gives up the planned attack south and orders his army back to Dunkirk. A corridor is held open while the divisions march north, with rearguards at Cassel and Wormhout.

**explain-4**: Gort decided on the evening of 25 May without waiting for London or the French command: he sent the two divisions meant for the attack south to fill a gap opening on the Belgian front. Many historians count it the decision that saved the army.

## **event-5**: Operation Dynamo begins
26 May, 18:57. The Admiralty signals Ramsay to begin Operation Dynamo. The first ships sail for Dunkirk on the short route, route Z, along the French coast.

**explain-5**: Some men had already been brought home from Dunkirk over the previous days: wounded and rear-area troops. Dynamo was the order to bring off the fighting army. Nobody expected more than a fraction of it to be saved.

## **event-6**: Route Z under fire
27 May. German guns near Calais shell the ships on route Z, the shortest route. By daylight it can no longer be used, and ships must take routes much longer around the sandbanks.

**explain-6**: Route Z was 39 sea miles; the longer route Y, east around the banks, was 87, more than doubling the turnaround time. Minesweepers set to work clearing a middle route, X.

## **event-7**: The East Mole
27 May. The harbour is wrecked and burning. Captain William Tennant, sent to run the evacuation ashore, tries the long breakwater of the outer harbour, the East Mole, as a quay. It works.

**explain-7**: The mole was never built for ships to tie up to: it was a narrow concrete and timber walkway on piles. But destroyers could lie alongside it, and men could file along it four abreast. Most of the army would leave this way.

## **event-8**: The perimeter
28 May. King Leopold surrenders the Belgian Army. British troops race to close the gap at Nieuport. The perimeter is now a line of canals around Dunkirk: French in the west, British from Bergues to Nieuport.

**explain-8**: The French opened sluices to flood the low fields, making the ground hard for tanks. Inside the line the roads filled with abandoned lorries and guns, wrecked on purpose so the Germans could not use them.

## **event-9**: Air attack on the ships
29 May. The Luftwaffe attacks the harbour and the ships in strength. The destroyer Grenade is hit alongside the mole and sinks; others are lost or damaged. Still, 47,000 men are brought home.

**explain-9**: The smoke from the burning oil tanks hid the harbour on some days and marked it on others. RAF fighters flew patrols over Dunkirk from bases in England, but at the edge of their range they could not be there all the time, and the men on the beaches rarely saw them.

## **event-10**: Night on route Y
Night of 28–29 May. Off the Kwinte Buoy on route Y, a German E-boat torpedoes the destroyer Wakeful, crowded with troops. She breaks in two and sinks in seconds; nearly all the 640 soldiers aboard are lost.

**explain-10**: The long route Y passed close to the Belgian coast at its eastern end, within reach of German E-boats at night and aircraft by day. Losses like the Wakeful’s are why route X, through the minefields, was cleared and used from 29 May.

## **event-11**: The little ships
30 May. Small boats arrive off the beaches in numbers: yachts, fishing boats, lifeboats and pleasure launches, many crewed by their owners. They ferry men out from the shallows to the bigger ships.

**explain-11**: The Small Vessels Pool had been gathering boats for a week. Most were taken over and crewed by the Navy, but many civilians went too. Their great value was reaching the beaches, where the larger ships could not come in close.

## **event-12**: The biggest day
31 May. 68,014 men are brought home, the most of any day. Lord Gort hands over command to Major-General Harold Alexander and sails for England, as ordered.

**explain-12**: The beaches were hard to work from: heavy surf on 31 May overturned boats, and men waded out to their shoulders. The lorry jetties, lines of army lorries driven into the sea and decked over, helped boats come alongside.

## **event-13**: 1 June: the worst day at sea
1 June. In clear weather the Luftwaffe sinks the destroyers Keith, Basilisk and Havant and many other vessels. German troops cross the canal near Bergues. From now on ships may come only at night.

**explain-13**: The day’s losses decided Ramsay to stop sailings in daylight. Even so, more than 64,000 men were brought off on 1 June.

## **event-14**: The last British troops
2 June. The British rearguard falls back through the town to the mole. At 23:30 Captain Tennant signals Dover: “BEF evacuated.”

**explain-14**: About 198,000 British soldiers were brought home. Tens of thousands of British troops had already been captured or killed in the campaign, and the army left behind almost all its equipment: some 2,500 guns, 64,000 vehicles and 76,000 tons of ammunition.

## **event-15**: The French rearguard
3 June. French troops hold the shrinking line while their comrades are taken off from the mole. Ships return night after night until the end.

**explain-15**: The French had defended the western sector from the beginning and the whole line at the end. About 139,000 French soldiers were brought to England; most went back to France within days to fight on, until France asked for an armistice on 17 June.

## **event-16**: The last ship
4 June, 03:40. The destroyer Shikari leaves the mole, the last ship out. At 10:20 the Germans enter Dunkirk; about 35,000 French soldiers of the rearguard are taken prisoner.

**explain-16**: The Admiralty ended Operation Dynamo at 14:23 on 4 June. In nine days about 338,000 men had been landed in England, of them about 139,000 French and other Allied soldiers.

## **event-17**: Operation Dynamo ends
4 June. That afternoon Churchill tells the House of Commons that “we must be very careful not to assign to this deliverance the attributes of a victory. Wars are not won by evacuations.”

**explain-17**: The same speech ends with “we shall fight on the beaches”. The army that came home without its weapons became the core of the forces that held Britain in 1940 and fought on until 1945.

## **outcome**
- About 338,000 men were brought to England between 26 May and 4 June: about 198,000 British and 139,000 French and other Allied soldiers.
- Of the more than 900 vessels that took part, about 240 were lost, among them six British and three French destroyers; the totals vary between sources. The RAF lost about 145 aircraft over Dunkirk.
- The BEF left behind nearly all its heavy equipment: about 2,500 guns, 64,000 vehicles and 76,000 tons of ammunition.
- About 35,000 French soldiers of the rearguard were captured when the town fell on 4 June. France signed an armistice on 22 June.
- Britain fought on. The rescued soldiers became the core of the army that would return to France in 1944.
