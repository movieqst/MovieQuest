## Part 1 — ten sentences for voice cloning (about two minutes)

Read these in the voice you want for the narrator: unhurried, warm, like a documentary.
They cover every English speech sound plus the names above.

1. Three thousand years ago, two great kings marched their armies toward a walled city on the river Orontes.
2. Ramesses the Second, pharaoh of Egypt, believed the enemy was far away; Muwatalli, Great King of the Hittites, was waiting behind the walls of Kadesh.
3. Chariots thundered across the ford, wheels biting the shallow water, as the archers drew their bows.
4. The divisions of Amun, Re, Ptah and Seth were strung out along the road, each a day's march from the next.
5. Nobody knows exactly how many men fought that day; the Egyptian texts claim thirty-seven thousand, and most historians doubt it.
6. The judge quickly asked whether the young boy had ever seen a zebra, a giraffe or a vulture at the zoo.
7. Fresh troops, the Ne'arin, appeared from the west in the late afternoon, and the pendulum began to swing.
8. Joyful children splashed through the shallow brook while their mother measured the thick purple cloth.
9. Fifteen years later, the two kingdoms sealed a peace treaty, the oldest of its kind that survives from both sides.
10. This is a reconstruction. What the stones tell us is one thing; what historians have pieced together is another.

**Recording tips.** A quiet room, no music, phone or microphone about a hand's width from your mouth, the same distance for every take. Record as WAV or the highest-quality MP3 your app offers. Leave half a second of silence before and after each take. Don't whisper and don't shout: an even, relaxed reading clones best. If you stumble, pause and read the whole sentence again; it is easy to cut.

## Part 2 — the full script (47 clips, about 1630 words)

Read every entry as one take and save it with the file name shown, as MP3, in a folder named after you inside `audio/`, for example `audio/jb/intro-1.mp3`. Then add `{ id: 'jb', name: 'JB' }` to `voices` in `scenario.js` and the narrator picker will offer it.

### intro-1.mp3 — Intro screen 1: BATTLE OF KADESH

BATTLE OF KADESH. c. 1274 BCE , Orontes River, Syria. One of the earliest battles in history that we can follow move by move, thanks to the inscriptions Ramesses II carved on his temples. This introduction separates what the ancient sources actually say from what historians have reconstructed. Watch for the coloured tags.

### intro-2.mp3 — Intro screen 2: Two empires, one border town

Two empires, one border town. Ramesses II, pharaoh of Egypt, in the fifth year of his reign, marches north against Muwatalli II, Great King of the Hittites. The prize is Kadesh, a fortified city on the Orontes, and the land of Amurru beside it: the frontier where the two empires met in Syria. Amurru had recently switched its loyalty to Egypt. Muwatalli came south with a large coalition to take the frontier back. The exact year is debated. 1274 BCE is the most common modern dating, but other chronologies give 1275 or 1285.

### intro-3.mp3 — Intro screen 3: The Egyptian army

The Egyptian army. Four divisions, each named for a god: Amun, Re, Ptah and Seth. Roughly 5,000 men per division, about 20,000 in all, with perhaps 2,000 chariots. No source gives these numbers directly. The Egyptian chariot carried two men, a driver and an archer. It was light and fast, built for shooting on the move. A fifth force, the Ne’arin, marched separately. The texts never say where from; most historians think along the coast through Amurru.

### intro-4.mp3 — Intro screen 4: The Hittite army

The Hittite army. Egyptian texts claim 37,000 infantry and 2,500 chariots in the first charge, with 1,000 more held back. Most historians think these figures are inflated. Muwatalli led a coalition of vassals and allies. The Egyptian list names about nineteen peoples, including Aleppo, Carchemish, Arzawa and the Dardany. The Hittite chariot carried three men: a driver, a shield bearer and a spearman. It was heavier and built for shock. The whole army waited out of sight behind the city, on the far side of the river.

### intro-5.mp3 — Intro screen 5: The deception

The deception. South of Kadesh, two Shasu nomads tell Ramesses the Hittites are far away at Aleppo, 200 km to the north. They were sent by Muwatalli to say exactly that. Ramesses races ahead with the Amun division, crosses the ford at Shabtuna, and makes camp north-west of the city. His other divisions are strung out behind him over many kilometres. The spacing shown on this map is our reconstruction.

### intro-6.mp3 — Intro screen 6: The truth

The truth. Egyptian scouts capture two Hittite spies near the camp. Beaten, they confess: the Hittite army is standing right behind Kadesh. Ramesses sends his vizier racing south to hurry the Re and Ptah divisions forward. It is already too late.

### event-1.mp3 — Battle event 1: The march

Ramesses has camped north-west of Kadesh with the Amun division. Re is strung out on the road behind him, Ptah is still crossing at Shabtuna and Seth is far to the south. He believes the Hittites are 200 km away at Aleppo.

### explain-1.mp3 — Explain this moment 1: The march

Why rush ahead? Ramesses trusted the two Shasu who said the enemy was at Aleppo. Reaching Kadesh first would let him begin a siege before the Hittites arrived. The Egyptian account blames the deception, not the king, for the gap between his divisions.

### event-2.mp3 — Battle event 2: Captured scouts

Two Hittite scouts are captured and beaten until they confess: the Hittite army is standing behind the city. Ramesses sends his vizier racing south to hurry the Re and Ptah divisions.

### explain-2.mp3 — Explain this moment 2: Captured scouts

The Bulletin says the scouts were beaten before they talked. The Hittite army now slides south along the east bank, keeping the city between itself and the Egyptian camp.

### event-3.mp3 — Battle event 3: The chariots cross

Muwatalli sends his chariots across the Orontes south of Kadesh. The Egyptian account claims 2,500 chariots in this first wave.

### explain-3.mp3 — Explain this moment 3: The chariots cross

Why chariots alone? A chariot force could cross and strike fast while the infantry stayed safe behind the river. The target was not the camp but the Re division, caught in the open on the march.

### event-4.mp3 — Battle event 4: Into the flank

The Hittite chariots sweep west onto the marching column of the Re division. Re is in line of march, not in battle formation.

### explain-4.mp3 — Explain this moment 4: Into the flank

A column on the march cannot fight. Its men are spread along the road, its chariots are not harnessed for battle, and it has no flank guard. This is the moment the Hittite plan was built around.

### event-5.mp3 — Battle event 5: Re breaks

Taken in the flank, the Re division shatters. Survivors flee north toward the king’s camp with Hittite chariots on their heels.

### explain-5.mp3 — Explain this moment 5: Re breaks

The rout of Re is accepted by nearly all historians: the Egyptian texts had no reason to invent a disaster. How many died is unknown.

### event-6.mp3 — Battle event 6: Charge on the camp

The Hittite chariots crash into the Amun camp, driving the fleeing soldiers of Re before them.

### explain-6.mp3 — Explain this moment 6: Charge on the camp

The camp was fortified with a wall of shields, according to the reliefs, but it was full of men who were not expecting a fight. Fugitives from Re arrived with the enemy right behind them.

### event-7.mp3 — Battle event 7: Ramesses stands

The Egyptian account says the camp dissolved in panic and that Ramesses, with only his household troops, charged the Hittite chariots again and again, calling on Amun.

### explain-7.mp3 — Explain this moment 7: Ramesses stands

How much of this is true? The Poem is a royal text written to glorify the king, who claims he fought alone. But a counter-attack by whatever chariots were at hand is plausible, and something stopped the Hittite charge.

### event-8.mp3 — Battle event 8: Plunder

The Hittite crews stop to plunder the camp, according to the Egyptian account. That pause may have saved the pharaoh.

### explain-8.mp3 — Explain this moment 8: Plunder

Looting a captured camp was normal in ancient warfare, and it broke the momentum of the attack. Whether it happened here or is an excuse written by the Egyptians, we cannot tell.

### event-9.mp3 — Battle event 9: The Ne’arin arrive

A fresh Egyptian force, the Ne’arin, appears from the west. The texts never say where it came from; most historians think it marched along the coast through Amurru.

### explain-9.mp3 — Explain this moment 9: The Ne’arin arrive

Was this luck or planning? Some historians think Ramesses had arranged for the Ne’arin to meet him at Kadesh that day. The Egyptian account presents their arrival as a gift of the gods.

### event-10.mp3 — Battle event 10: The second wave

Muwatalli commits a second wave across the river north of the city: the Egyptian account says 1,000 chariots, including his own brothers and allied kings.

### explain-10.mp3 — Explain this moment 10: The second wave

Why not send the infantry? A river crossing under pressure is slow, and the Hittite infantry would have been exposed while fording. Chariots could cross and withdraw quickly.

### event-11.mp3 — Battle event 11: Counter-attack

Caught between the Ne’arin and the king’s chariots, the Hittite crews are driven back east toward the river.

### explain-11.mp3 — Explain this moment 11: Counter-attack

Heavy three-man chariots that have stopped to loot lose their main advantage: speed. Fresh infantry and lighter Egyptian chariots could now take them at a disadvantage.

### event-12.mp3 — Battle event 12: Ptah arrives

The Ptah division reaches the field from the south. The pendulum has swung.

### explain-12.mp3 — Explain this moment 12: Ptah arrives

The Egyptian account gives Ptah credit for the recovery. With fresh infantry arriving and the Ne’arin already engaged, the Hittite chariots were outnumbered on the west bank.

### event-13.mp3 — Battle event 13: Into the Orontes

Hittite chariots are pushed back into the Orontes. The reliefs show the prince of Aleppo being held upside down to drain the water from him after he was pulled from the river.

### explain-13.mp3 — Explain this moment 13: Into the Orontes

The reliefs delight in drowning Hittites and tumbling chariots. Some of this is genuine: a chariot force retreating across a river under attack is in real danger.

### event-14.mp3 — Battle event 14: Across the river

The survivors recross the river. Muwatalli’s infantry, the bulk of his army, never fought at all.

### explain-14.mp3 — Explain this moment 14: Across the river

Why did the infantry stay out? Perhaps Muwatalli judged the chariot raid had failed and saw no reason to risk his main body. Perhaps he never meant to fight a pitched battle at all.

### event-15.mp3 — Battle event 15: Nightfall

The first day ends. Egyptian stragglers drift back toward the camp. Both kings still have large forces in hand.

### explain-15.mp3 — Explain this moment 15: Nightfall

Neither side had been destroyed. Egypt had lost much of the Re division and its camp had been overrun; Hatti had lost many chariots and their crews, including senior nobles.

### event-16.mp3 — Battle event 16: Day two and after

The Egyptian account claims fighting resumed the next morning and that Muwatalli asked for a truce. Ramesses marched home. Kadesh stayed Hittite.

### explain-16.mp3 — Explain this moment 16: Day two and after

The second day is barely described in any source. What is certain is the result: Egypt did not take Kadesh, Amurru returned to Hittite control, and both kings claimed victory.

### outcome.mp3 — Historical outcome (debrief)

The Hittite chariot attack was stopped and its survivors driven back across the Orontes. Muwatalli’s infantry never fought. Fighting resumed on the second day and Muwatalli proposed a truce. Ramesses withdrew to Egypt. Kadesh stayed Hittite, and Amurru returned to Hittite control. Later Hittite texts claim Muwatalli defeated Ramesses and advanced south into the land of Upi, around Damascus. Most historians call it a tactical draw at best for Egypt and a strategic success for the Hittites. Ramesses’s temples call it a glorious victory. About fifteen years later, c. 1259 BCE, Ramesses II and Hattusili III sealed the earliest surviving peace treaty known from both sides. A copy hangs at the United Nations.
