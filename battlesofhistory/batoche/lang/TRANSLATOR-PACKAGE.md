# Batoche, 1885: package for Michif and Cree translators

**What to translate:** the 22 parts below, in order. Each becomes one recording.

**How to record:**
- one file per part, named as in the heading (welcome, intro-1 … outcome);
- any phone or computer is fine, in a quiet room;
- read at an easy pace.

**Names:** keep people's names as they are. For places, use the name your language uses, if there is one (Batoche, Duck Lake, Fish Creek, Saskatchewan River).

**Your judgment is welcome:** if a sentence is wrong, or something should be said differently in your language or from your community's view, please change it and tell us. We will fix the English to match.

## The script (22 parts)

### welcome

Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Batoche, fought from the 9th to the 12th of May 1885, on the South Saskatchewan River in what is now Saskatchewan, Canada, between a Canadian government army under Major-General Frederick Middleton and the Métis and First Nations fighters of Louis Riel and Gabriel Dumont.

### intro-1

Battle of Batoche. In the spring of 1885, a Canadian army marched north across the prairies to the Métis village of Batoche. For four days a few hundred men in rifle pits held it off. On the fourth day, the army broke through. These events were long called the North-West Rebellion. Today they are more often called the North-West Resistance, the name used by Parks Canada and the Métis Nation.

### intro-2

The Métis of the South Saskatchewan. The Métis are a people born of the fur trade, with First Nations and European roots, their own language, Michif, and their own way of life on the plains: buffalo hunting, freighting and farming. Many had moved west from Red River, in Manitoba, after 1870. At Batoche and the nearby settlements they farmed long, narrow river lots running back from the South Saskatchewan, as they had at Red River. The village took its name from its founder, the trader Xavier Letendre, known as Batoche. It had a ferry on the Carlton Trail, stores, houses and a new church, Saint-Antoine-de-Padoue.

### intro-3

Grievances. By the 1880s the buffalo were gone. The Cree and other First Nations who had signed treaties faced hunger, and felt that the government was not keeping its promises. The Métis feared for their land. Government surveyors used square lots, and title to the river lots was slow to come. Year after year they sent petitions to Ottawa, with little answer. In 1884 a group of Métis, Gabriel Dumont among them, rode to Montana to ask Louis Riel to come back and lead them. Riel had led the Red River Resistance of 1869 and 1870, which brought Manitoba into Canada, and had lived in exile since.

### intro-4

March 1885. In March 1885 Riel and his followers declared a provisional government at Batoche. Gabriel Dumont, a buffalo hunter famous on the plains, led its fighters. On the 26th of March, at Duck Lake, Métis and First Nations fighters defeated a force of North-West Mounted Police and volunteers. Further west, Cree fighters killed nine people at Frog Lake and took Fort Pitt, though the Cree leaders did not all agree on using force. Ottawa sent an army west on the new Canadian Pacific Railway, still unfinished in places north of Lake Superior. Its commander was a British officer, Major-General Frederick Middleton.

### intro-5

The road to Batoche. Middleton marched north from the railway at Qu'Appelle toward Batoche. On the 24th of April, at Fish Creek, Dumont's men ambushed his column from a wooded coulee and stopped it. That battle has its own page. Middleton waited about two weeks for supplies, more men, and a river steamer, the Northcote. Then, on the 7th of May, he marched on Batoche.

### intro-6

The armies and the ground. Middleton counted 886 men on land: militia from Winnipeg, Toronto and Ontario towns, mounted scouts, and gunners of A Battery. He had four nine-pounder guns, a Gatling gun, and about fifty more men on the steamer. The defenders were Métis, Cree and Dakota fighters, perhaps 250 to 300 at the start. Their numbers changed as the battle went on. They had hunting rifles, shotguns and old muzzle-loaders, and little ammunition. The village lay on the east bank of the river. To the south, the church and the rectory stood on higher ground, with the cemetery beyond. Along the wooded slopes and ravines, the defenders had dug rifle pits: shallow holes with a low wall of earth and logs, hard to see and hard to hit.

### event-1

Middleton plans a joint attack. His column will come up from the south-east by land, while the Northcote, with soldiers behind barricades on her decks, comes down the river behind the village.

### event-2

The Northcote arrives before the land column is ready. Fighters on both banks open fire on her. At the Batoche ferry, the ferryman Alex P. Fisher, helped by Pascal Montour, has lowered the ferry cable across the river. It catches the steamer and knocks down her smokestacks. Damaged, she drifts on downstream and never lands her soldiers.

### event-3

The column reaches the church and the rectory. The priests, the sisters, and Métis women and children are sheltering inside. A bullet through the rectory wall wounds Father Moulin in the leg. Witnesses say a white cloth was shown, and Middleton ordered the firing stopped.

### event-4

Beyond the church, the ground drops toward the river. The soldiers push to the edge of Mission Ridge and run into fire from pits they cannot see. The guns are pulled forward onto the ridge, and the defenders creep through the bush toward them. Captain Howard's Gatling gun opens fire and covers the infantry as they fall back.

### event-5

When the Northcote came by, Philippe Garnot took about twenty men from the pits to fire on her. That thinned the line facing Middleton's first advance. The defenders, scouts under Patrice Fleury and Ambroise Champagne watching both banks, also pressed the soldiers' flanks. Hildebrandt argues that these Métis attacks deserve more credit than the usual story gives them.

### event-6

Middleton pulls his army back into a camp walled with wagons and earth, a zareba, about a kilometre south-east of the church. Through the night, rifle fire from the bush keeps the camp awake.

### event-7

The soldiers move out again, cautiously. They dig shallow pits halfway toward the cemetery but cannot take the heights. The guns shell the rifle pits, the cemetery and the buildings across the river. The camp's walls are built higher.

### event-8

Middleton rides out with Boulton's mounted men and Howard's Gatling gun onto the open prairie to the north, the Jolie Prairie. The Gatling fires a few rounds into the rifle pits. Defenders hurry across to meet him, and he sees that the line in front of his infantry grows thin when they do.

### event-9

In the pits, cartridges are running out. Witnesses describe men searching the ground the soldiers have left for dropped cartridges, and melting down lead. Hildebrandt writes that some fired nails when the bullets made from scavenged lead ran out; how often that happened is uncertain.

### event-10

Middleton tries the trick again. He leads the mounted men, a nine-pounder and the Gatling out onto the Jolie Prairie, to draw the defenders north. The infantry are to attack when they hear his guns. A rider comes out under a white flag: John Astley, a surveyor held prisoner by Riel, with a letter warning that if the women and children are harmed, the prisoners will be too. Middleton answers that the families should gather in one house under a white flag.

### event-11

The infantry do not move: a strong east wind carries the sound of the guns away, and Colonel Van Straubenzie hears nothing. The planned attack never begins. Middleton rides back, furious: "I lost both my temper and my head," he wrote later. His men sit down to their lunch of hard biscuit. Across the lines, seeing the soldiers pull back, many Métis think peace has come and go back to their families.

### event-12

In the afternoon the Midland and the Royal Grenadiers go forward near the cemetery, with the 90th Winnipeg Rifles in support, and the advance becomes a charge. Who ordered it is still argued: Lieutenant-Colonel Williams, Colonel Van Straubenzie, or nobody, as the soldiers simply ran forward. The thin line in the pits breaks. A Métis witness remembered Riel kneeling, his arms crossed, praying with the people around him as the defence gave way.

### event-13

The soldiers pour down toward the village. Fighting goes on in the bush and around the houses. Captain John French of French's Scouts is shot at an upstairs window of Batoche's house. Prisoners held by Riel's government are set free. Among the defenders killed that day is Joseph Ouellette, an old man, said to be over ninety.

### event-14

Batoche has fallen. Families who had sheltered by the river come out to find their homes searched and their belongings taken. Later testimony said that soldiers took belongings, among them a woman's wedding ring.

### outcome

The army won. Hildebrandt counts twenty-five dead in the four days: soldiers, Métis and First Nations. By the last day, only 50 to 60 defenders were still fighting, with about 40 rifles between them; the rest had double-barrelled shotguns. On the 15th of May, Louis Riel gave himself up. Gabriel Dumont stayed near Batoche for some days; when he heard that Riel had surrendered, he escaped to the United States. Poundmaker surrendered at the end of May, and Big Bear in early July. Both were sent to prison. Riel was tried for high treason at Regina. The jury found him guilty but asked for mercy. He was hanged on the 16th of November 1885. Eleven days later, on the 27th of November, six Cree and two Nakoda (Assiniboine) men were hanged together at Battleford. Riel's death divided Canada for a generation, French against English, Catholic against Protestant. Dumont later toured the United States with Buffalo Bill's Wild West show. After an amnesty he came home, and he died near Batoche in 1906. Today Batoche is a National Historic Site. Riel, once hanged as a traitor, is remembered as the founder of Manitoba, and statues of Riel and Dumont stand in Winnipeg and Saskatoon.
