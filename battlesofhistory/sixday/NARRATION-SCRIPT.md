## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Six-Day War, fought from the 5th to the 10th of June 1967, between Israel and Egypt, Jordan and Syria, with Iraqi forces also taking part. We begin three weeks earlier, with a warning that was not true, and follow the crisis that led to war.

## Introduction

### 1. THE SIX-DAY WAR
- In June 1967 Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria on three fronts at once. It began on the morning of the 5th of June with an Israeli air strike on Egypt's airfields, and ended on the evening of the 10th with a ceasefire on the Golan Heights. *(established)*
- When it was over, Israel held land three times its own size, with about a million Arabs under its rule, and hundreds of thousands of people had fled or been driven from their homes. *(estimate)*
- Israelis call it the Six-Day War. In Arabic it is often called an-Naksa, "the setback", a word Nasser used on the 9th of June. *(established)*

### 2. The land and its peoples
- The war of 1948 ended in 1949 with armistice agreements between Israel and Egypt, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon. These were not peace treaties, and the armistice lines were not agreed borders. *(established)*
- Egypt ran the Gaza Strip. Jordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem with the Old City; Israel held West Jerusalem, and Mount Scopus stayed an Israeli island inside Jordanian ground. Syria held the Golan Heights, above demilitarised zones along the line. *(established)*
- The war of 1948 also made about seven hundred thousand Palestinians refugees, by the UN's count; Palestinians call it the Nakba, "the catastrophe". Many still lived in camps in 1967, and in this war many would be uprooted a second time. *(established)*

### 3. Suez, 1956
- In 1956 Israel invaded Sinai in coordination with Britain and France. Under pressure from the United States and the Soviet Union, it withdrew in 1957. *(established)*
- A United Nations force, UNEF, then took up posts on the Egyptian side only, along the line in Gaza and Sinai and at Sharm el-Sheikh, above the Straits of Tiran: Israel's sea road to its port of Eilat. *(established)*
- Israel declared that if the straits were ever closed to its ships again, it would treat that as an act of war. *(established)*

### 4. The great powers
- This was the Cold War. The Soviet Union armed Egypt and Syria with MiG jets and Soviet tanks, and gave them political backing. *(established)*
- Israel's air force flew mostly French jets, such as the Mirage. The United States was friendly to Israel, but also to Jordan, whose army had American Patton tanks and British Hunter jets. *(established)*

### 5. Water and the border, 1964 to 1967
- Israel's National Water Carrier drew water from the Sea of Galilee. In January 1964 the Arab League decided to divert two of the Jordan River's sources, the Hasbani and the Banias, away from it. Syria began digging in 1965, and Israeli tanks and artillery fired on the works that year. *(established)*
- In the demilitarised zones below the Golan, Israeli farming and Syrian fire from the heights led to clash after clash. In interviews published after his death, Moshe Dayan said Israel often sent tractors in knowing the Syrians would shoot. *(israeli)*
- From 1965 Fatah, a small Palestinian group backed by Syria, raided Israel. After a mine killed three Israeli policemen, a large Israeli force attacked the West Bank village of as-Samu on the 13th of November 1966, destroying many houses and ambushing a Jordanian force. The UN Security Council censured Israel. *(established)*
- On the 7th of April 1967 a fight in the zone near the Sea of Galilee grew into an air battle that reached Damascus. Israeli Mirages shot down six Syrian MiG-21s, two of them in sight of the Syrian capital. Israel claimed seven; Syria claimed a Mirage, which Israel denied. *(disputed)*

### 6. The armies
- Israel, with reserves called up, had about 264,000 men, about 800 tanks and between 200 and 300 combat aircraft. *(estimate)*
- Egypt sent about 100,000 men and 900 to 950 tanks into Sinai, with about 420 combat aircraft at home, while up to a third of its army was still fighting a war in Yemen. *(estimate)*
- Jordan had about 55,000 men, some 45,000 of them on the West Bank with about 270 tanks, and two dozen Hunter jets. Syria had perhaps 50,000 to 75,000 men and about 120 combat aircraft. Iraq readied about 100 tanks and an infantry division to send into Jordan. *(estimate)*
- On paper Israel was outnumbered. Most historians judge that its training, air force, reserve system and short inner lines, with Egyptian command failures, counted for more than numbers. *(established)*

### 7. How this page tells it
- This page draws on Israeli, Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian and Palestinian sources, and on American, Soviet and United Nations papers. *(established)*
- Each line carries a tag. "Established" means sources on all sides agree. A side's name means that side's own account. "Disputed" means sources disagree, and we say who says what. "Estimate" marks numbers that depend on who is counting. *(established)*
- Clock times are Israel time unless we say otherwise. Where no source gives a time, we say morning, afternoon or night. *(established)*

## Part 1: The crisis

### 1. The false warning
*Map: Region · 13 to 15 May 1967*
- On the 13th of May 1967 a Soviet intelligence report reached Egypt. It said Israel was massing about eleven brigades on the Syrian border. *(established)*
- The report was false. United Nations observers found no such troops on either side of the line. *(established)*
- It reached Cairo by at least three routes, the first through Anwar Sadat, Speaker of Egypt's National Assembly, then in Moscow. Whether President Podgorny or a deputy minister, Vladimir Semyonov, told him is unclear. *(disputed)*
- Why Moscow sent it is still argued. Leonid Brezhnev later said the Soviets hoped Nasser would move troops toward Israel and take pressure off Syria; others think Moscow wanted a crisis. *(disputed)*
- On the 14th of May Egypt put its forces on alert. Columns of troops and tanks paraded through Cairo on their way to Sinai, in full view. *(established)*
- The same day President Nasser sent his Chief of Staff, General Mohamed Fawzi, to Damascus. Fawzi later wrote that he found no Israeli build-up at all. Yet the troops kept moving into Sinai. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- One Egyptian account says Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer, Nasser's deputy and the real head of the armed forces, mobilised more than 100,000 men without telling Nasser first. *(disputed)*
- In Israel, the Chief of Staff, Lieutenant-General Yitzhak Rabin, asked to call up the reserves. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol, who was also Defence Minister, refused, and allowed only a higher state of alert. *(israeli)*
- After the war Rabin told the French paper *Le Monde* that the two divisions Egypt sent on the 14th of May could not have launched an attack, "and we knew it". *(israeli)*
- On the 15th of May, at a song festival in West Jerusalem, Shuli Natan sang Naomi Shemer's new song, "Jerusalem of Gold", for the first time. It longs for the Old City, then in Jordanian hands, and became the Israeli anthem of the war. *(established)*
- For the Palestinian families who lived in that Old City, it was simply home. Haifa Khalidi, then 19, had been born in a house beside the Mughrabi Quarter, next to the Western Wall. *(arab: Palestinian)*

### 2. The peacekeepers go
*Map: Region · 16 to 19 May 1967*
- At about ten at night on the 16th of May, in Gaza, the commander of the UN force, the Indian Major-General Indar Jit Rikhye, was handed a letter from General Fawzi. *(established)*
- It said Egyptian troops were already gathered in Sinai, and asked the UN to withdraw its soldiers from their observation posts along the border. Rikhye answered that he could not move without orders from the Secretary-General. *(established)*
- In New York, Secretary-General U Thant said such a request must come from Egypt's government to him directly, and that it could not be for a partial withdrawal: UNEF would stay whole or go. *(established)*
- On the 18th of May Egypt's Foreign Minister, Mahmoud Riad, formally ended Egypt's consent. Egyptian troops were already blocking UN soldiers from their posts, and India and Yugoslavia decided to pull their men out. U Thant agreed the same day. *(established)*
- The force stood on Egyptian soil only with Egypt's consent. U Thant asked whether Israel would take UNEF on its side of the line. Israel's UN ambassador, Gideon Rafael, said that was "entirely unacceptable". *(established)*
- U Thant's quick consent was criticised then and since. Critics say he should have taken it to the General Assembly or played for time. His defenders say he had no legal choice. *(disputed)*
- On the 19th of May Rikhye was ordered to begin the withdrawal, and the UN flag came down in Gaza. *(established)*
- UNEF did not vanish overnight. The Canadians were flown out by the 31st of May; Indian, Brazilian and Swedish soldiers were still leaving when war came. *(established)*

### 3. The straits close
*Map: Region · 22 to 23 May 1967*
- With the UN gone, Egyptian troops took over Sharm el-Sheikh, above the Straits of Tiran. *(established)*
- On the 22nd of May Rabin told the Israeli cabinet that the Egyptian forces in Sinai were deployed for defence, not for attack. *(israeli)*
- That night, speaking to pilots at the Bir Gifgafa air base in Sinai, Nasser announced that the Gulf of Aqaba was closed to Israeli ships and to any ship carrying strategic goods to Israel, from the next day. *(established)*
- His most quoted line: "Under no circumstances can we permit the Israeli flag to pass through the Gulf of Aqaba." The wording varies with the translation. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Egypt said the straits had been mined. Later accounts say they were not. *(disputed)*
- Eilat was Israel's only Red Sea port, and the way in for its oil from Iran. *(established)*
- For Israel this was the act of war it had warned of since 1957. President Johnson later said the closure was the step that, more than any other, made war likely. *(established)*
- Anwar Sadat later wrote that he had backed the closure. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 23rd of May President Lyndon Johnson publicly rejected Egypt's claim to control shipping in the Gulf. *(established)*
- That day the US Ambassador in Israel, Walworth Barbour, helped hold off an Israeli strike by passing on a British idea: an international naval group to open the straits. *(established)*

### 4. Waiting
*Map: Region · 23 May to 3 June 1967*
- Israelis call the next twelve days the hamtana, "the waiting". The army stood mobilised, the economy nearly stopped, and the cabinet chose to give diplomacy time. *(established)*
- On the 23rd of May the former Prime Minister, David Ben-Gurion, criticised Rabin for the mobilisation. Soon after, Rabin collapsed from strain and was sedated; he later said he slept about a day. The date and length are disputed. *(israeli)*
- Foreign Minister Abba Eban flew west. In Paris, on the 24th of May, President de Gaulle warned Israel not to fire first, telling him, by Eban's account: "1967 is not 1957." In London, Prime Minister Harold Wilson backed free passage through the straits. *(israeli)*
- The UN Security Council met from the 24th of May and decided nothing. U Thant went to Cairo, without result. *(established)*
- On the 25th of May, in Washington, Eban was told by a new cable to warn of an imminent Egyptian and Syrian attack. American intelligence did not agree. Israel agreed to wait up to two weeks while Washington tried other ways. *(established)*
- On the 26th of May Nasser told Arab trade unionists that if war came it would be a general battle, and that its aim would be to destroy Israel. The wording varies between versions. He also said Egypt would not fire first. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Historians still disagree on whether Nasser meant to fight, or hoped to win without war. *(disputed)*
- That evening in Washington, the small hours of the 27th in Israel, Johnson met Eban. Johnson said his advisers saw no attack coming and that Israel would win if attacked. "Israel will not be alone," he said, "unless it decides to go it alone." *(established)*
- That same night an Egyptian air strike plan, code-named "Dawn", was called off. One account says the Soviet ambassador woke Nasser at about half past two, Cairo time, with a warning from Moscow, as pilots sat in their planes. Others say Nasser never approved the plan. *(disputed)*
- In Moscow, the Soviet premier, Alexei Kosygin, told Egypt's War Minister, Shams Badran: "Politically, you prevailed." *(established)*
- On the 27th or 28th of May the Israeli cabinet split nine to nine on going to war at once. Eshkol sided with Eban, and diplomacy got a few more days. *(israeli)*
- On the 28th of May Eshkol spoke live on the radio from a text corrected by hand, and stumbled over the words. Many Israelis heard it as weakness. *(established)*
- In the Negev, a seventeen-year-old paratrooper, David Bahat, waited with his unit for three weeks. He later remembered hearing an Egyptian broadcast in broken Hebrew claiming that Tel Aviv had fallen. *(israeli)*
- In Cairo, eight-year-old Suraya Mahmoud heard the children in her street chanting that the army would march into Tel Aviv. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 5. Hussein in Cairo
*Map: Region · 30 May to 4 June 1967*
- On the 30th of May King Hussein of Jordan flew to Cairo, unannounced, with his prime minister and his army and air force chiefs. Nasser, who had lately called him an "imperialist lackey", said their differences had vanished "in one moment". *(established)*
- They signed a defence pact on live radio. An attack on one would count as an attack on both, and in war Jordan's forces would come under Egyptian command. An Egyptian general, Abdul Munim Riad, was sent to Amman to lead the Jordanian front. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts saw this as the moment the ring around Israel closed. Jordanian accounts show a king pulled into a war he neither wanted nor expected, because his people would not let him stay out. *(disputed)*
- On the 31st of May Iraqi troops and armour began moving into Jordan. The same day Hussein asked the United States to stay neutral. *(established)*
- On the 31st of May and the 1st of June the head of Mossad, Meir Amit, was in Washington. He told Defense Secretary Robert McNamara that Israel could not stay mobilised for long. The American record shows no decision; whether Amit came home believing Washington would not object is still argued. *(disputed)*
- On the 1st of June Eshkol gave up the Defence Ministry to Moshe Dayan, and the opposition leader, Menachem Begin, joined a government of national unity. *(established)*
- On the 2nd of June Israel's generals pressed for war; most ministers still hesitated. *(israeli)*
- The same day, General Fawzi wrote later, Nasser told his own generals to expect an Israeli attack on the 4th or 5th of June. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 3rd of June a letter from Johnson asked again that Israel not start the fighting. At the General Staff that day Rabin warned that "a military and diplomatic noose" was tightening, and that delay could let Jordan, Iraq and Syria join. *(israeli)*
- The same day two Egyptian commando battalions were flown to Amman. *(established)*
- At a Jordanian base near Samu, the army's radio chief, Mahmoud Muali, a Palestinian from Qarawat Bani Zaid, had decided by the 2nd of June that war was coming. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- On Sunday the 4th of June the Israeli cabinet met. The head of military intelligence, Aharon Yariv, reported Jordan's army under Egyptian command and Iraqi units entering Jordan. Even the cautious Haim-Moshe Shapira accepted that time had run out. Eshkol reminded them that "the Arabs will remain here" whatever the war's outcome. *(israeli)*
- That afternoon the cabinet voted for war. The exact count is not known. Eshkol and Dayan were left to fix the hour, and that evening Rabin set it for the next morning. *(israeli)*
- The same day Iraq formally joined Egypt, Syria and Jordan. In Cairo, the American journalist James Reston wrote that Egypt did not want war. Egypt's Vice-President was due in Washington for talks on the 7th of June. *(established)*

## Part 2: The air strike

### 6. Operation Focus
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, 07:10–12:00*
- On Monday the 5th of June, at a quarter to eight Israel time, Israeli jets struck about ten Egyptian airfields at once. In Cairo, an hour ahead, it was a quarter to nine. *(established)*
- Israel called it Operation Focus, in Hebrew *Moked*. The plan is credited to Ezer Weizman, the former air force commander. It was carried out by his successor, Mordechai Hod, known as Motti. *(israeli)*
- At ten past seven, sixteen Fouga Magister trainers flew the patrol path Egyptian radar saw every morning. Five minutes later the strike force took off: 183 aircraft by Israeli figures, with only twelve fighters left to guard Israel. *(israeli)*
- The jets flew in groups of four, west over the sea for about eighteen minutes, then south, about sixty feet above the waves and in radio silence. Hod later called the wait "the longest 45 minutes of my life." *(israeli)*
- In Egypt, that same morning, Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer was flying from Almaza, near Cairo, to Bir Thamada in Sinai with the air force commander, Sidki Mahmoud. Air defences had been told not to fire while his plane was in the air. *(established)*
- Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw large formations and sent a coded warning to Cairo. Egypt's foreign minister, Mahmoud Riad, later wrote that the cipher had just been changed, and the message was not read in time. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Over each base the jets climbed sharply, hit the runways first, then the aircraft parked in the open. None of Egypt's airfields had hardened shelters. *(established)*
- At El Arish, the MiG-17 pilot Fikry el-Gahramy remembered: "There was no warning at all." *(arab: Egyptian)*
- At Kabrit, Flight Lieutenant Mustafa Hafez, aged twenty-six, was in the mess when the bombs fell. He took off in whatever MiG-17 was ready, and found his guns would not fire because of a cockpit switch. "I was not actually due to fly that morning," he said. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- A second wave, from about half past nine, hit fourteen more fields. Bombers moved south to Luxor for safety were attacked there at midday. *(israeli)*
- All the numbers are claims. Israeli-based totals for the day run from about 290 to 340 Egyptian aircraft; an Arab chronology gives 209 of 340. Egypt's chief of staff, General Mohamed Fawzi, wrote that 85 percent was gone within four hours. *(estimate)*
- Israel lost about 17 to 19 aircraft that day, most to anti-aircraft fire. *(estimate)*
- In Cairo, Nasser's former deputy Abdul Latif al-Baghdadi wrote that Amer that evening first claimed 73 Israeli planes shot down, then showed a list: 47 Egyptian aircraft left, 35 able to fly. Radio Cairo was still announcing victories. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 7. Three air forces
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, 11:50–evening; 7 June*
- Jordan's radar had read the heavy traffic as Egyptian aircraft, and Cairo told Amman that Egypt's air force was striking Israel. Jordan, Syria and Iraq sent their own aircraft in. *(established)*
- At about ten to twelve, Jordanian Hawker Hunters from Mafraq attacked near Netanya. One account says they were aiming at an air base, missed, and hit a factory, killing one civilian and wounding seven. Whether three or four Hunters flew is disputed. *(disputed)*
- Syrian jets struck northern Israel. An Arab chronology names the Haifa refineries and Megiddo airfield as targets; Israeli accounts say three Syrian jets were shot down. *(disputed)*
- An Iraqi Tu-16 bomber dropped bombs on a main street of Netanya, then was shot down by anti-aircraft guns near Afula. It fell on an army camp, killing its crew and about a dozen Israeli reservists. Sources disagree whether this was the 5th or the 6th of June. *(disputed)*
- From about a quarter to one, Israel's third wave hit Mafraq and then Amman. By Israeli count, Jordan's air force was out of the war by a quarter to three. *(israeli)*
- Israel claimed all 21 of Jordan's Hunters, with transports and helicopters. An Arab chronology gives 22 fighters, 5 transports and 2 helicopters lost. *(estimate)*
- Accounts drawn from Jordanian sources say the squadron commander, Major Firas Ajlouni, was killed at Mafraq. A Pakistani instructor flying for Jordan, Saif-ul-Azam, claimed an Israeli Mystère, which Israel does not confirm. *(disputed)*
- Between two and four in the afternoon, Israeli jets struck Syrian bases around Damascus and beyond. Israel claimed about two-thirds of Syria's air force, 50 to 61 aircraft. Other accounts say Syria had spread its aircraft to distant bases and lost far fewer. *(disputed)*
- At about three, four Vautour bombers struck the Iraqi base H-3 in the western desert. Israel claimed about ten aircraft there; other counts give six. A single Lebanese Hunter was also shot down. *(israeli)*
- That night the chief of staff, Yitzhak Rabin, announced on the radio that 400 enemy planes had been destroyed. Earlier, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan had asked the press not to publish the number. *(israeli)*
- On the 7th of June, by Iraqi accounts, Iraqi, Jordanian and Pakistani pilots flying Hunters from H-3 shot down two Vautours and a Mirage. *(disputed)*
- For the whole war Israel claimed about 450 Arab aircraft; other counts run from 372 to 440. Israel lost between 26 and 48 aircraft, depending on what is counted. From the second day, the Arab armies fought with almost no aircraft of their own overhead. *(estimate)*

## Part 3: Sinai and Gaza

### 8. Through Rafah
*Map: North Sinai · 5 June, 07:50–night; El Arish 6 June morning*
- As the jets struck, Israel's Southern Command, under Brigadier-General Yeshayahu Gavish, sent the code word "Red Sheet". Three divisions moved into Sinai: Israel Tal's in the north, Avraham Yoffe's in the centre, Ariel Sharon's at Abu-Ageila. *(israeli)*
- Israel had about 45,000 to 70,000 men and 650 to 700 tanks in the south; Egypt about 100,000 men and some 900 tanks, in three lines of defence. *(estimate)*
- At about ten to eight, Brigadier-General Tal's division crossed at two points, opposite Nahal Oz and south of Khan Yunis. Colonel Shmuel Gonen's 7th Armoured Brigade led. *(israeli)*
- Patton tanks pushed slowly through the streets of Khan Yunis. The historian Michael Oren writes that the railway junction fell in just over four hours. *(israeli)*
- At about noon, Israeli tanks attacked the dug-in Egyptian brigade at Rafah junction, which had heavy Soviet-built IS-3 tanks. Egyptian losses in the Rafah gap are put at about 2,000 men and 40 tanks. *(estimate)*
- In the afternoon, at Sheikh Zuweid, Gonen's brigade was pinned, then broke through. A young company commander, Avigdor Kahalani, was caught in an ambush and badly burned when his Patton caught fire. *(israeli)*
- West of Sheikh Zuweid lay the Jiradi, a narrow road between high dunes. At about half past two, Centurions of the 82nd Battalion raced through it. Then the Egyptian brigade holding it closed the road behind them. *(israeli)*
- At half past four the Pattons met heavy fire from bunkers and anti-tank guns, and their commander, Ehud Elad, was killed. Tal sent in Colonel Rafael Eitan's paratroopers to help clear the pass. *(israeli)*
- The Jiradi was taken, lost and taken again. The Egyptian defenders fought on from their trenches after midnight, and Israel's supply trucks only got through at about two in the morning. One count gives Israel's losses there as 66 dead, 93 wounded and 28 tanks hit. *(estimate)*
- At about ten to eight on the 6th of June, Israeli troops took El Arish airfield, then entered the town, where snipers fired from the rooftops. One account says El Arish fell the evening before. *(disputed)*
- Further south, Brigadier-General Yoffe's reservists drove their Centurions through dunes Egypt thought impassable, some thirty-five miles in about nine hours, mostly in first gear. *(israeli)*
- At the Bir Lahfan crossroads they blocked an Egyptian armoured and a mechanised brigade sent north toward El Arish by the Sinai front commander, General Abd el-Mohsen Murtagi. The night tank battle there is dated to either of the first two nights. *(disputed)*

### 9. Gaza
*Map: North Sinai · 5–8 June*
- The Gaza Strip was a narrow coastal strip under Egyptian administration, crowded with refugees from the 1948 war and their children. *(established)*
- It was defended by the 20th Palestinian Division of the Palestine Liberation Army, under an Egyptian-appointed commander whose name is spelled Hasni or Husni, with Egyptian units beside it. *(established)*
- Defence Minister Dayan had ordered his troops to keep out of the Strip. After guns in Gaza shelled the kibbutzim of Nirim and Kissufim, Rabin overruled him and sent in Colonel Yehuda Reshef's 11th Mechanised Brigade. *(israeli)*
- A former Palestinian fighter at a border base near Khan Yunis later said Israeli tanks came on the first day and his unit was not ready. "I knew we had lost," he said. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- The last UN peacekeepers were still in their camps, waiting to leave. At half past two a shell hit the Indian battalion's main camp, killing an officer and a soldier. India blamed Israeli artillery; the UN report noted Egyptian positions close by. *(established)*
- The same day, by the UN's report, Israeli aircraft strafed a white-painted UN convoy south of Khan Yunis, killing three Indian soldiers; at the Rafah camp a Brazilian soldier was wounded. *(established)*
- How many peacekeepers died in all is disputed: India counted 8 by the 8th of June; UN papers are cited for 15, fourteen Indian and one Brazilian; another count gives 27. *(disputed)*
- By sunset Israeli troops held the Ali Muntar ridge above Gaza City, but were pushed back from the city itself. One count gives about 70 Israelis killed in the Strip that day, among them the Life photographer Paul Schutzer. *(estimate)*
- On the morning of the 6th of June, Eitan's paratroopers and the armour took Gaza City. In Khan Yunis, broken into on the first day, the town and camp held out until the 7th or the 8th of June. *(disputed)*
- In Beach camp, Sarah Ayoub, aged twenty-four, was baking bread when the explosions began. Next morning she saw soldiers across the camp and houses destroyed. Her husband, Munther, was among the men rounded up. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- In Shujaiya, Zuhair Ahmad, a young aircraft engineer just graduated in Cairo, was killed by shrapnel while checking on his brothers. His mother, Mariam Farajallah, said: "That war tore my heart apart." *(arab: Palestinian)*
- In al-Maghazi camp, Hajj Jumaa Abu Hadrous, a refugee from Jaffa, saw tanks gather at the camp's edge. They came in at night with aircraft overhead, and he fled with his wife and three children to the coast at Zawaida. "We did not think it was a war," he said. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- Ten-year-old Ghazai Misleh carried his little sister to Zawaida. Back home, he remembered, his father would not let his mother cook over a fire, because soldiers fired at smoke. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- The UN relief agency reported that civilian casualties in Gaza were higher than elsewhere, but gave no figure. *(established)*

### 10. Approach to Um Katef
*Map: Abu-Ageila · 5 June, 08:15–19:00*
- Abu-Ageila was a crossroads in eastern Sinai, where the road from the Israeli border meets the roads west into the peninsula. East of it rose the Um Katef ridge. *(established)*
- Here the Egyptian 2nd Infantry Division, under Major-General Sa'di Naguib, held three lines of trenches with concrete bunkers, minefields and wire facing east. The dunes to the north were thought impassable and had no mines. *(established)*
- Studies of the battle give about 8,000 Egyptian soldiers at Um Katef, with a tank regiment of 66 T-34s and 22 SU-100 assault guns, and a brigade of artillery in depth. *(estimate)*
- Western accounts say General Naguib was reluctant to leave Amer's side and was away from the front. No Egyptian account found in our research confirms this. *(disputed)*
- Brigadier-General Sharon had about 14,000 to 19,000 men and about 150 tanks. They included Colonel Mordechai Zippori's armour, Colonel Kuti Adam's reservist infantry, Danny Matt's paratroopers, six artillery battalions and Natke Nir's Centurions. *(estimate)*
- The first probes, in the morning and around midday, were stopped on minefields, with six or seven Centurions lost. *(israeli)*
- Sharon changed his plan: he would strike from the north, through the dunes. At one o'clock, civilian buses began carrying Adam's reservists toward the front. *(israeli)*
- North of the ridge, Position 181 was held by the Egyptian 38th Infantry Battalion with ten SU-100 guns. A second attack took it at about four in the afternoon. *(israeli)*
- Nir's Centurions drove west without stopping to refuel. At about seven they cut the road from Abu-Ageila to Bir Lahfan and destroyed eleven vehicles of the Egyptian tank regiment. Um Katef was now cut off from the west. *(israeli)*
- Most of what is known of this battle comes from Israeli and Western studies. Egypt's archives are closed, and no first-hand account by Abu-Ageila's Egyptian commanders was found in our research. *(established)*

### 11. The night assault
*Map: Abu-Ageila · 5 June 19:00 – 6 June 12:00*
- From about seven in the evening, helicopters began carrying Matt's paratroopers to a landing zone about five kilometres north of the Egyptian guns. Accounts give six to eighteen helicopters and 200 to 560 men; some lost their way, and at about nine Egyptian fire stopped the lift. *(disputed)*
- Adam's infantry left their buses and marched about a dozen miles through the sand to the Egyptian northern flank. *(israeli)*
- Then Israel's guns opened up, firing about 6,000 shells. Sources put the start between ten and a quarter to eleven, and the length at under twenty minutes or nearly half an hour. *(disputed)*
- At about a quarter past eleven, Adam's brigade went into the trenches behind a creeping barrage. *(israeli)*
- At about half past eleven, the paratroopers attacked the Egyptian gun lines. Egyptian ammunition trucks blew up, causing the paratroopers' heaviest losses, and the Egyptian guns largely fell silent. *(israeli)*
- The Egyptian infantry of the 12th Brigade fought trench by trench through the dark. By half past two in the morning, Israeli accounts say, only a third of the trenches had been cleared. *(israeli)*
- Engineers opened a first lane through the minefield at about half past two, losing a tank to a mine, and a second at about four. Zippori's tanks rolled through. At about half past four, Nir was badly wounded. *(israeli)*
- By six in the morning most of Um Katef was taken. At seven, Israeli tanks attacked the Egyptian tank and anti-tank units from two sides, Centurions from the east and Super Shermans from the west. They fought for three hours. *(israeli)*
- By half past ten the position was in Israeli hands, and by noon the crossroads was open. Yoffe's tanks passed through toward Jebel Libni, which fell by dusk. *(israeli)*
- Israel lost 32 to 40 killed, more than 120 wounded and 19 tanks. Egyptian losses are given as about 300 killed in the last phase, or about 2,000 for the whole battle, with 40 to 60 tanks. *(estimate)*
- In tanks and guns the two sides were close to even, about 88 Egyptian against 112 Israeli. Studies credit the night attack, the guns, the paratroopers and the dune march. *(estimate)*

### 12. The retreat order
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 6 June*
- By the 6th of June, Egypt's front line was broken at Rafah, the Jiradi and Abu-Ageila. Its second line, around Jebel Libni, was under attack. *(established)*
- In Cairo the night before, Baghdadi wrote, the war minister Shams Badran brought word that Khan Yunis had fallen and Rafah was besieged. Nasser withdrew to a bedroom. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 6th of June, Field Marshal Amer ordered a general withdrawal from Sinai. The hour is disputed: morning, by Nasser's account through the journalist Heikal; afternoon, after the news from Abu-Ageila, in Western histories; or evening. *(disputed)*
- General Fawzi later wrote that Amer gave him twenty minutes to draw up a plan. Fawzi brought a timetable over several days. Amer answered that he had already ordered the withdrawal. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- The words are reported differently too: "within one night", with personal weapons only, in Fawzi's account; "within 24 hours" in Al-Ahram. *(disputed)*
- A US Library of Congress study says Amer's staff persuaded him to cancel the order, but the front divisions were already moving. Other accounts do not mention a cancellation. *(disputed)*
- Fawzi wrote that the orders bypassed the chain of command, and that field commanders took orders from military police and intelligence officers. Fawzi blamed Amer, and reviewers note he is a partisan witness. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- By Fawzi's account, Badran guarded Amer's door, and the Soviet military attaché asked why combat units were not allowed to fight. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Anwar Sadat, then a senior politician and later a rival of the generals, wrote that the war "started and ended while Amer was still in the sky." *(arab: Egyptian)*
- There were no phases, no rearguards and no set routes. Units heard the order at different times. Some broke up, others kept fighting, and the retreat became a rout under air attack. *(established)*
- Fawzi's editor estimates that about 120,000 men were moving toward the Canal in a single evening. *(estimate)*
- On the Israeli side, at about four in the afternoon, Sharon's division was turned south toward Nakhl. The race for the passes had begun. *(israeli)*

### 13. Sharm el-Sheikh
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 7 June, 04:30–12:15*
- Sharm el-Sheikh, at the southern tip of Sinai, has guns overlooking the Straits of Tiran. Egypt's closing of the straits to Israeli shipping in May was one of the causes of the war. *(established)*
- One naval account says three small Israeli torpedo craft left Eilat on the night of the 6th of June and sailed down the Gulf of Aqaba. *(disputed)*
- At about half past four in the morning on the 7th of June, Israeli boats fired on the Egyptian shore batteries. *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers and commandos boarded helicopters and Noratlas transports. Rabin judged a landing straight onto Sharm too risky and aimed them first at El Tor, on the Gulf of Suez. *(israeli)*
- Air and naval reports then showed Sharm mostly abandoned the day before, and the force was sent there instead. *(israeli)*
- What happened next is disputed. Michael Oren describes a pitched battle in which 20 Egyptian soldiers were killed and eight captured. Other accounts say the position was taken without a shot, and the first paratroopers found the navy already there. *(disputed)*
- One naval article says the garrison had been a full infantry regiment, and that the bombing sent its officers north in their vehicles. *(disputed)*
- At a quarter past twelve, Dayan declared the Straits of Tiran an open international waterway. *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers also landed at El Tor. The next day they linked up at Abu Zenima with Yoffe's tanks coming down the Gulf of Suez coast. *(israeli)*

### 14. The passes
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 7–8 June*
- Between central Sinai and the Canal, the roads run through a few passes in the hills: Khatmia, Giddi and Mitla. Egyptian units retreating west had to use them. *(established)*
- Columns of the Egyptian 3rd Infantry, 4th Armoured and 6th Mechanised Divisions, and Major-General Saad el-Shazly's task force, headed for the passes. *(established)*
- On the 7th of June, Tal sought battle at Bir Gifgafa with the Egyptian 4th Armoured Division of Major-General Sidki el-Ghoul. After about two hours, the Egyptians pulled back south. One Israeli account says much of it escaped overnight. *(israeli)*
- The same day, Yoffe's southern force drove from Jebel Libni through Bir Hasana and Bir Thamada, reaching the eastern end of the Mitla Pass in the late afternoon. *(israeli)*
- At dusk Colonel Iska Shadmi blocked the pass with nine working Centurions, two of them on tow, a few half-tracks and three mortars. Three more tanks, out of fuel, were towed into place. *(israeli)*
- The Egyptian columns arrived into a jam, by one account three miles long, under Israeli air attack. Around midnight, the Israeli crews refuelled from improvised cans. Egyptian tanks tried to break through in groups of fifteen or twenty; of more than twenty SU-100 guns, one got through. *(israeli)*
- At first light on the 8th of June, only four Israeli Centurions could move. They held a column with 28 T-54s until the jets returned. Many Egyptian soldiers had already left their vehicles in the night and walked into the hills. *(israeli)*
- An aerial photograph of the burned vehicles at Mitla, by the UPI photographer Peter Skingley, won a World Press Photo prize that year. *(established)*
- At dawn on the 8th of June, near Nakhl, Sharon's division ambushed two brigades of the Egyptian 6th Mechanised Division. Sharon estimated 60 to 70 tanks, 100 guns and about 1,000 dead. Some tanks were found abandoned in working order. *(israeli)*
- Shazly's force, about 7,500 men with a brigade of 150 tanks, sat near the border by Kuntilla. Shazly says he never received the order of the 6th of June, moved into Israeli territory, and held a valley there. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- He says he was contacted only on the evening of the 7th. He marched about sixty miles through the night, was bombed at dawn with more than a hundred killed, and crossed the Canal at dusk on the 8th, by the one bridge still in Egyptian hands. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Some Western historians say Shazly stayed in place, or left his command. His escape is seen by some as skilful, by others as leaving the front. *(disputed)*
- An Israeli soldier named Asher, aged thirty-two, from Kibbutz Mishmar HaNegev, recalled that summer seeing Egyptian soldiers marching across the sand. They reminded him of "the Jews wandering through Europe." *(israeli)*

### 15. To the Canal
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 8 June – 9 June, 00:30*
- At three in the morning on the 8th of June, Egyptian tanks hit an Israeli light-tank camp near Bir Gifgafa and burned several vehicles before pulling back. On the coast road, Lieutenant-Colonel Yisrael Granit's group reached El Qantara, the first Israelis at the Canal. *(israeli)*
- Near the Canal, Colonel Eitan was shot in the head and badly wounded. Aharon Davidi took over. *(israeli)*
- In the south, Yoffe's forces reached the Gulf of Suez. The Egyptians set the Ras Sudar oil fields on fire; Israeli sources disagree whether Ras Sudar was taken on the 8th or the 9th. *(disputed)*
- Captain David Caspi led his Centurions through the Giddi Pass past a fresh Egyptian battalion, then raced for the Canal. He radioed that he could "spit on the canal." *(israeli)*
- At half past midnight on the 9th of June, Tal's reconnaissance group of six Pattons reached the Canal. By the end of that day Israel held all of Sinai to the Canal, except Port Fuad. *(established)*
- Egypt accepted the ceasefire on the evening of the 8th of June, about half past nine by one Israeli source; some sources say the 9th. Israel wanted to stand on the Canal before it took effect. *(disputed)*
- Tens of thousands of Egyptian soldiers were left to walk west through the desert in June heat. Egypt acknowledged losing 10,000 soldiers and 1,500 officers; observers believed about half of the dead died of thirst or exhaustion. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Amin Mohammad Darwish was on duty near the border on the 5th of June. He walked for three days past abandoned and burned-out weapons before he was captured, then was held in Israel for eight months. "I was humiliated," he said. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- A Swiss Red Cross delegate, Marcel Boisard, was sent to bring them home, under Red Cross flags made from hospital sheets. At El Qantara, those who spoke English were set aside as suspected officers. He estimated 20,000 to 30,000 men crossed home in two days, with no records kept. *(estimate)*
- Israel took between about 4,300 and 5,000 Egyptian prisoners, with about 500 officers; many were held at Atlit for months. Egypt captured a handful of Israelis, among them downed pilots. *(estimate)*
- Israel lost about 275 killed and 800 wounded in Sinai, by General Gavish's count. Egyptian dead are given as anywhere from 3,000 to 15,000, and 600 to 800 tanks lost. *(estimate)*
- The desert kept its dead for a long time. In 2024 the remains of an Egyptian soldier, Fawzy Mohammed Abdel-Muwla, almost twenty and engaged to be married in 1967, were found in North Sinai and buried with military honours. *(arab: Egyptian)*

## Part 4: Jerusalem and the West Bank

### 16. Jordan opens fire
*Map: Jerusalem · 5 June, about 07:00–13:30*
- In 1967 Jerusalem was a divided city. The Old City and East Jerusalem were held by Jordan, together with the West Bank, then ruled by Jordan. West Jerusalem was Israeli, and a small Israeli enclave sat on Mount Scopus, cut off behind the Jordanian lines. *(established)*
- Under the pact signed in Cairo on the 30th of May, the Jordanian front was commanded by an Egyptian, Lieutenant-General Abdul Munim Riad, who took his orders from Field Marshal Amer in Cairo. King Hussein remained supreme commander. *(established)*
- At seven in the morning, the Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw heavy air traffic over Israel. Jordanian and Egyptian accounts say the warning was sent to Cairo, but Cairo had changed its codes that day and could not read it in time. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At ten to nine, an aide phoned the palace in Amman: Radio Cairo was saying that Israel had attacked Egypt. At about nine, General Riad received a coded message from Amer. It claimed that Egypt had destroyed three quarters of Israel's aircraft and was counter-attacking in Sinai. None of this was true. Riad ordered offensive operations. Hussein later wrote that, for Jordan, the war began at nine in the morning. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At half past nine, machine guns opened up along the line in Jerusalem, then mortars and recoilless rifles. The heavy shelling of West Jerusalem and of Israeli towns began between about ten and a quarter past eleven; the sources disagree on the exact start. *(disputed)*
- That morning Israel's Prime Minister, Levi Eshkol, sent a message through the Norwegian chief of the UN observers, General Odd Bull: if Jordan stayed out, Israel would not attack it. Hussein wrote that his answer was that it was too late; the battle had begun. Whether the message arrived before or after Jordan opened fire is still argued. *(disputed)*
- Historians also disagree on why Jordan fought. Jordanian accounts stress the pact, Egyptian command and false reports from Cairo. Other historians see it as the King's own choice. *(disputed)*
- In West Jerusalem, families went down to the shelters. Israeli figures speak of about 6,000 shells in two days, most in the first day, and about 1,000 apartments damaged. Shells hit the Biblical Zoo and the President's residence, and damaged the Chagall windows at Hadassah hospital. *(israeli)*
- A mother named Yeivin later told reporters she sheltered with her three children, aged ten, nine and three, and about a dozen neighbours behind sandbags, and lay over her children for an hour, fearing a direct hit. *(israeli)*
- At half past eleven, Brigadier Ata Ali, commanding the Jordanian forces in Jerusalem, opened artillery fire on orders from West Bank headquarters. He asked Amman for air cover and was told it would come later. Shortly after half past twelve, Israeli aircraft destroyed Jordan's small air force on the ground. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Between half past eleven and half past three, a UN officer, Colonel Murray Stanaway, tried three times to arrange a ceasefire in the city. Jordanian accounts say their side accepted but wanted the Israelis to stop first; an Israeli diary has the Israeli commander, Major-General Uzi Narkiss, agreeing at noon and then telling his superiors he wanted to "get in". *(disputed)*
- Shortly after noon, West Bank command ordered the seizure of Government House, the UN headquarters on a ridge south of the Old City. Hazim Khalidi, a former British officer advising the Jordanians, urged waiting until evening and laying mines. Ata Ali refused. Between about one and half past one, two companies from the Sur Baher battalion took the compound. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- In East Jerusalem, hundreds of young men crowded police posts that afternoon asking for weapons. A civilian committee received some rifles and Bren guns, but almost none reached the Old City. *(arab: Palestinian)*

### 17. Government House retaken
*Map: Jerusalem · 5 June, afternoon and night*
- For Israel's commanders, Government House overlooked the road to the south of the city. Colonel Eliezer Amitai's Jerusalem Brigade, a brigade of reservists, was ordered to retake it. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts say the attack went in at about half past two with six Sherman tanks, and the ridge was retaken by mid-afternoon. A Jordanian account has the counter-attack at three, with a dozen tanks against Government House and another dozen against Sur Baher, and two Israeli tanks destroyed. *(disputed)*
- The Jordanian company commander reported that he withdrew out the back of the compound having lost 139 men dead or missing out of 400. Israeli figures do not confirm this number. *(disputed)*
- General Bull's UN staff were escorted into Israeli lines. *(established)*
- At the foot of Ammunition Hill, in the north of the city, reservists of the Jerusalem Brigade held a trench. Among them were lecturers from the Hebrew University. One of them, Corporal Shmaryahu Rivier, was killed by recoilless rifle fire at about a quarter past two. *(israeli)*
- That afternoon Colonel Mordechai "Motta" Gur's 55th Paratroop Brigade learned that its jump into Sinai was cancelled. It was sent to Jerusalem instead, and many of the paratroopers arrived at dusk in civilian buses. *(israeli)*
- Narkiss told Gur to link up with Mount Scopus: bear right toward the Rockefeller Museum, he said, and be ready to break into the Old City. *(israeli)*
- There was no Israeli plan to take the Old City. In the Knesset shelter, under shelling, ministers Menachem Begin and Yigal Allon pressed for it; Moshe Dayan, the new Defence Minister, and the religious party ministers were against. Eshkol made no final decision. *(israeli)*
- At about five in the evening, Colonel Uri Ben-Ari's Harel Brigade attacked the ridges north-west of Jerusalem, toward Nebi Samuel and the Ramallah road. *(israeli)*
- After sunset, the Jordanian West Bank headquarters pulled back. Telephone lines out of Jerusalem were cut at six, and the Ramallah transmitter was knocked out at half past seven. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Between about nine and eleven at night, Israeli aircraft caught a Jordanian relief column on the Jericho road: a tank regiment of the 60th Armoured Brigade and a battalion of Ata Ali's brigade. By eleven, Ata Ali was told it was gone. *(established)*
- One Jordanian-side account reports Israeli helicopter landings above Wadi al-Joz that night. No Israeli source mentions them. *(disputed)*

### 18. Ammunition Hill
*Map: Jerusalem · 6 June, about 02:00–06:30*
- Ammunition Hill and the Police School beside it guarded the road to Mount Scopus. They were held by the 2nd King Hussein Battalion under Major Mansour Kraishan, about 120 to 150 men in trenches and concrete bunkers. One Jordanian source names a different battalion. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about eleven at night, Israeli mortars and artillery opened on the northern line. Searchlights from Mount Scopus lit the ground. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Gur's paratroopers crossed near the Mandelbaum Gate after midnight. Lieutenant-Colonel Yossi Yaffe's 66th Battalion took the Ammunition Hill sector. The attack began between two and half past two; sources differ by half an hour. *(israeli)*
- The Police School was nearly empty. The defenders were in the bunkers on the hill, so the two sides met in roughly equal numbers. *(established)*
- From about half past two to half past six, the fighting went trench by trench, at close range, for about four hours. *(established)*
- An Israeli private, Yaki Haimovitch, aged twenty-one, took the lead when his commander was hit and destroyed a large bunker with explosives and a bazooka. Years later he called himself "just a soldier who did what they told me". *(israeli)*
- Another private, Eitan Neveh, left the trench to give covering fire and was killed. *(israeli)*
- Jordanian veterans later said their side fought hard to the end. At a reunion decades later, veterans from both sides spoke of each other as soldiers who had not given way. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- The losses most often given are about 36 Israeli and about 71 Jordanian soldiers killed. Other counts run up to 50 Israeli and 106 Jordanian dead. *(estimate)*
- After the battle, Meir Ronnen, a forty-year-old Israeli reservist, buried seventeen Jordanian dead and marked the grave with a rifle and a sign: "Here lie buried 17 brave Jordanian soldiers." *(israeli)*
- To the west, the Harel Brigade broke through the border defences near Ma'ale HaHamisha during the night, after hours of slow fighting through minefields. *(israeli)*

### 19. Around the walls
*Map: Jerusalem · 6 June, day and night*
- Israeli accounts say that at about three in the morning Ata Ali told the Governor of Jerusalem, Anwar al-Khatib, that the battle was lost, and left toward Jericho. Jordanian accounts say he called at dawn and moved into the Old City. *(disputed)*
- At first light, Israeli aircraft hit the remaining Jordanian guns, and seven tanks cleared the last strong point on the Nablus Road and moved toward the Damascus Gate. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At seven, Ata Ali told Khalidi he had about 500 men inside the walls. Shu'fat Hill, Augusta Victoria, Abu Tor and Ras al-Amud were still held. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Through the morning, Israeli troops took Sheikh Jarrah, the American Colony and the area of the Rockefeller Museum. By mid-morning they had reached the enclave on Mount Scopus, cut off since 1948. *(established)*
- Near the Rockefeller Museum, Israeli mortar fire fell on Israeli troops by mistake, killing nine paratroopers and several Jordanian prisoners. *(israeli)*
- Governor al-Khatib reached Herod's Gate and found Major Kraishan with sixty-nine men, apart from a company still on Shu'fat Hill. He moved into the Awqaf, the office of the Islamic endowments beside the Haram al-Sharif. It became the last civilian command post. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Inside the walls the hospitals ran out of blood by the first afternoon; at St Joseph's three patients died for lack of it. Nuns and a civil defence team under Musa Bitar cared for the wounded on the Via Dolorosa. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- One Palestinian family later said that Israeli soldiers shot two brothers and an uncle in their courtyard on the 6th, and that the family fled across the Jordan. It is a single account. *(disputed)*
- Shu'fat Hill fell just after noon, after two assaults; Abu Tor fell too. At Tel el-Ful, north of the city, Jordanian tanks were hit from the air and pulled back toward Jericho. *(established)*
- In Amman, the pressure on Hussein grew. One account says Egypt advised him at about ten to pull his army back across the Jordan; in the afternoon he learned of the UN's ceasefire call and decided to hold on. At midday General Riad passed on three choices: a ceasefire imposed from outside, leaving the West Bank that night, or one more day of fighting. *(disputed)*
- In a call intercepted and later released by Israel, Nasser and Hussein agreed to say that US and British planes were helping Israel. They were not. *(established)*
- At sunset, paratroopers attacked Augusta Victoria. Their reconnaissance unit took a wrong turn under fire from the walls, lost two tanks and jeeps, and pulled back after about two hours. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At ten at night, a Jordanian brigade came up from Jericho to relieve the city, but was blocked by the wreckage of its own armour on the road. The fire died down after midnight. That day Dayan had forbidden his troops to enter the Old City. *(disputed)*

### 20. The West Bank falls
*Map: West Bank · 5–7 June*
- Jordan had about 45,000 men on the West Bank, and Israel about 40,000 on this front: estimates differ. Israel's air force, free after the first morning, ruled the roads. *(estimate)*
- On the night of the 5th, Israeli troops took the Latrun salient, on the road from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem. The garrison had already left for Jerusalem. Trappist monks at the Latrun monastery said the Jordanians left without firing a shot. Two Egyptian commando battalions had also been sent to this area; accounts of what happened to them differ widely. *(established)*
- The villages of Imwas, Yalu and Beit Nuba, near Latrun, were emptied. Some fled, some were driven out; about 8,000 people, or more by some counts, walked toward Ramallah in the heat. Israeli officials said the villages were damaged in fighting; Israeli soldiers' testimony and the villagers say they were destroyed after the fighting. Within weeks all three were gone. *(disputed)*
- A French monk at Latrun, Brother Guy Khoury, kept a diary: about seventy elderly villagers, women and children sheltered at the monastery until an Israeli officer ordered them to leave for Ramallah. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- In the north, Brigadier-General Elad Peled's division crossed near Jenin at about five on the afternoon of the 5th. At dawn on the 6th, an Israeli feigned withdrawal drew out Jordanian Patton tanks, and eight were knocked out. Jenin was declared secure that evening. *(israeli)*
- A Jordanian tank gunner near Jenin, Corporal Hussein Saad al-Nuaimi, refused an order to withdraw. When told the rule did not apply, he asked, "and Jerusalem?" He was killed later that day. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Wassef Erekat, a nineteen-year-old Jordanian artillery officer, recalled that his battery pulled back on the morning of the 6th, and that Israeli tanks entered their camp after the cook opened the gate, believing they were Iraqis. An Iraqi brigade sent to help had been badly hit from the air before it arrived. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- The Jordanian armoured brigades suffered most from the air. Colonel Zaid ibn Shaker's 60th Armoured Brigade lost about half its eighty tanks. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- By the evening of the 6th, Ramallah, Qalqilya and Jenin were in Israeli hands. In Qalqilya about 850 buildings were demolished; after protests, the demolitions stopped and residents were allowed back. *(disputed)*
- Mahmoud Muali, a Jordanian signals man, was ordered to retreat. He left his rifle, burned his service records and walked two days to his village. "Everyone was shocked. Even me," he said. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- On the 7th, Bethlehem fell after a brief fight, with about forty Jordanian defenders killed; Nablus fell; and at about six in the evening Hebron fell, mostly without a fight. Its Hittin Brigade, under Bahjat al-Muhaisen, withdrew across the river. *(disputed)*
- That evening Israeli troops reached Jericho and the Jordan. The Allenby and Damiya bridges were blown; who blew which, and when, is disputed. *(disputed)*
- Over the next three months about 200,000 people crossed east over the river, many of them refugees from 1948 for a second time. Some fled from fear and the bombing; others were pushed out by loudspeakers or put on buses. *(estimate)*
- Jordan's losses are given as about 700 killed or missing, and higher counts reach 6,000 casualties of all kinds. *(estimate)*

### 21. The Old City
*Map: Jerusalem · 7 June, about 00:30–22:00*
- At half past midnight, Ata Ali told Governor al-Khatib that nearly all his officers had gone and he could not hold. The Governor refused to leave. He called Jerusalem his adopted city. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about one in the morning, the soldiers quietly gathered at the Dung Gate and left, about 500 of them. At half past two, police, volunteers and soldiers handed in their weapons at the Awqaf. Before dawn, a Jordanian sergeant and two Palestinian soldiers came back and dug in near the Golden Gate. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about seven, Dayan, hearing that a UN ceasefire was near, ordered the Old City taken. Whether the cabinet approved first is disputed. His orders said every holy building was to be protected. In Amman, Prime Minister Saad Juma'a had asked the US ambassador and the UN to stop Israel taking the Old City, warning that the monarchy itself could fall. *(disputed)*
- At sunrise, Israeli aircraft bombed Augusta Victoria. In the Awqaf, people cheered, thinking they were Iraqi planes. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At half past eight, Gur's paratroopers attacked on three lines. By half past nine they had linked up on the ridge and turned toward the walls. *(israeli)*
- At about a quarter to ten, tank fire opened the Lions' Gate, also called St Stephen's Gate, and Gur's half-track went in. One Jordanian soldier fired a Bren gun from the minaret above it. *(israeli)*
- The paratroopers went first onto the great platform that Jews call the Temple Mount and Muslims call al-Haram al-Sharif, the Noble Sanctuary, with the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque. At about ten, Gur spoke on the radio, in his own words: "The Temple Mount is in our hands." Then he ordered all firing to stop. *(israeli)*
- A little later the paratroopers came down to the Western Wall, the remaining wall of the ancient Temple compound and the holiest place where Jews can pray. Narkiss asked where it was. The Chief Rabbi of the army, Brigadier-General Shlomo Goren, said prayers and blew the shofar, the ram's horn. *(established)*
- One paratrooper, Yitzhak Yifat, later said: "We realised that we had conquered another people." *(israeli)*
- Gur's deputy tied an Israeli flag beside the Wall, and a flag was also raised on the Dome of the Rock. Dayan ordered that flag taken down. In the afternoon Dayan, Rabin and Narkiss walked through the Lions' Gate to the Wall. *(established)*
- Dayan left the Haram al-Sharif under the Muslim religious authority, the Waqf, which took it back within days. When Gur asked for a written statement that the holy sites were undamaged, Sheikh Sa'id Sabri refused until Muslims could inspect them. *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers took the Governor, the Muslim judge and Hazim Khalidi prisoner in the Awqaf. That day the Governor and the Mayor of East Jerusalem, Rouhi al-Khatib, surrendered the city. Within days Israel demolished the Mughrabi Quarter in front of the Wall; its story is told at the end. *(established)*
- The battle for Jerusalem cost Israel about 180 soldiers and about 20 civilians killed. One count gives about 330 Jordanian soldiers and about 100 civilians in East Jerusalem. *(estimate)*
- At ten at night, Israel time, the UN ceasefire with Jordan took effect. The West Bank and East Jerusalem were now held by Israel. *(established)*

## Part 5: The USS Liberty

### 22. USS Liberty
*Map: Region, inset off El Arish · 8 June, about two in the afternoon*
- On the 8th of June an American ship was sailing slowly off the coast of Sinai, about twenty-five nautical miles northwest of El Arish. She was the USS *Liberty*, a US Navy technical research ship gathering signals for the National Security Agency. *(established)*
- In the night the Pentagon had ordered her to stay at least one hundred miles from the coasts at war. The order was misrouted, held up and sent to the wrong stations, and it reached the ship only after the attack. *(established)*
- That morning Israeli aircraft flew over the *Liberty* many times. At three minutes past six an Israeli observer reported a US Navy supply ship, and later a naval officer matched her hull letters, GTR-5, in a reference book. At eleven o'clock the marker for the ship was taken off the plot in the naval headquarters. *(israeli)*
- Around midday there were reports that El Arish was being shelled from the sea. Three Israeli torpedo boats were sent toward El Arish. Their radar showed a ship moving at about thirty knots, the speed of a warship; the *Liberty* was moving at about five. Israel's navy asked for aircraft. *(israeli)*
- At about two in the afternoon, two Mirage jets led by Captain Iftach Spector attacked with cannon and rockets. Two Super Mystères followed with napalm and cannon fire. The ship's captain, Commander William McGonagle, was wounded but stayed on the bridge. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts say that on a low pass a pilot read the letters on the hull and the aircraft were called off. The torpedo boats then came up. One signalled "What ship?", and the Israelis took the reply as evasive. *(israeli)*
- At about twenty-five to three by the American log, eight minutes later by the Israeli clock, a torpedo struck the ship's side, killing twenty-five men below decks. The *Liberty* listed and lost power, but she did not sink. *(established)*
- The boats then offered help, which the crew turned down. Israel informed the United States in the afternoon, at half past three or at four by different accounts. *(disputed)*
- Thirty-four Americans were killed: officers, sailors, two marines and one civilian. About one hundred and seventy-one were wounded; some counts say one hundred and seventy-two or one hundred and seventy-four. *(estimate)*
- Israel said it had mistaken the ship for an Egyptian transport, the *El Quseir*. Its inquiries in 1967, and the army's history department in 1982, called it an innocent error, made amid false reports and lost information. *(israeli)*
- The US Navy Court of Inquiry, led by Rear Admiral Isaac Kidd, found mistaken identity, and said the flag may not have been visible in a calm sea. In July 1967 a report by Clark Clifford for the White House found no plan behind the attack, but "flagrant" gross negligence by Israel. *(established)*
- Many survivors believe the attack was deliberate. They point to the morning's identification, the ship's size and shape, which did not match the much smaller *El Quseir*, and a flag that they say was flying. Their critics answer that pilots at high speed could not have seen it. *(disputed)*
- Critics, among them a retired admiral, Thomas Moorer, whose independent commission reported in 2004, said the ship was attacked on purpose and that the truth was covered up. In 2004 a lawyer on the Navy court, Captain Ward Boston, said in a sworn statement that he and Kidd had privately believed it was deliberate. *(disputed)*
- Israel later paid compensation to the families, the wounded and the US government. Whether the attack was a mistake or deliberate is still disputed, and this page does not decide it. *(disputed)*

## Part 6: The Golan Heights

### 23. The decision on Syria
*Map: Golan · 8–9 June, night to morning*
- The Golan Heights rise steeply above the Hula and Jordan valleys and the Sea of Galilee. Syria had held them since 1948, with bunkers, trenches and minefields up to fifteen kilometres deep. *(established)*
- For about nineteen years, Syrian guns on the heights had fired down on the Israeli kibbutzim and villages below. At Gadot, children spent much of their lives in shelters, and residents said they could smell the coffee the Syrian soldiers brewed above them. *(israeli)*
- Who caused that border fighting is argued. Syria and some historians blame Israeli farming in the demilitarised zones. In interviews published after his death, Moshe Dayan said most of the clashes began when Israel sent tractors into those zones. *(disputed)*
- When the war began on the 5th of June, Syria shelled Israeli settlements and its jets attacked Galilee. That evening Israel's air force destroyed about two-thirds of Syria's air force. *(established)*
- On the 6th, Syrian infantry attacked toward She'ar Yashuv and Kibbutz Dan and were driven back. In the first four days, Israeli figures say, Syrian fire killed two Israeli civilians and wounded sixteen. *(israeli)*
- On the Syrian side, years of purges had removed more than two thousand experienced officers, and units lacked radios between tanks and infantry. Syria's three best brigades stayed near Damascus. *(arab: Syrian)*
- In Israel, Brigadier-General David Elazar of Northern Command, and leaders of the kibbutzim below, pressed for an attack on the heights. On the evening of the 8th, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan still said no, fearing Soviet intervention. *(israeli)*
- At about half past three in the morning of the 9th, Syria announced that it accepted the UN ceasefire. *(established)*
- A few hours later Dayan changed his mind. He read an intercepted message from Nasser urging Syria to stop fighting, and an analyst's report that the Syrian army was collapsing. *(israeli)*
- At six or seven in the morning, the accounts differ, Dayan phoned Elazar himself, going around the chief of staff, Yitzhak Rabin. Elazar recalled the question: could he attack? Then attack. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol was told afterwards. *(israeli)*
- In interviews published after his death, Dayan said the reason he gave was a pretext, that Syria was no threat by the fourth day, and that the attack was a mistake. Eshkol, Elazar and the kibbutz leaders said the attack was needed to end the shelling. *(disputed)*

### 24. The climb and Tel Faher
*Map: Golan · 9 June, about half past eleven in the morning to evening*
- All morning, for three or four hours, Israeli aircraft bombed the Syrian positions on the heights. *(israeli)*
- At about half past eleven, one source says ten, the 8th Armoured Brigade under Colonel Albert Mandler began to climb from near Kibbutz Kfar Szold, in the north, where the Syrian line was thinnest. Eight armoured bulldozers led, cutting a track for the tanks. *(disputed)*
- The Syrian guns opened fire, at first mostly on the Israeli settlements below. Then the gunners found the column. Five of the eight bulldozers never reached the top. *(israeli)*
- The tanks climbed in single file. Battalion 129 was drawn off its route toward Syrian fire. Its commander was wounded, and Major Rafi Mokady, who jumped into the lead tank, was killed near Sir a-Dib. *(israeli)*
- At dusk Lieutenant Natanel Horovitz led the last three working Shermans into the village of Qala. With his radio dead, he directed them with hand signals from the turret. *(israeli)*
- Further south, the Golani Infantry Brigade under Colonel Yona Efrat followed the same route about three hours behind. Its 13th Battalion took Tel Azaziat, above Kibbutz Dan, by late afternoon. *(israeli)*
- The Golani 12th Battalion, under Lieutenant-Colonel Moshe Klein, attacked Tel Faher, a fortress of trenches, wire and bunkers held by a Syrian company under Captain Ahmad Khalili. *(established)*
- At the foot of the hill the Israeli half-tracks were set on fire. The survivors of the leading company went into the trenches, and the fighting was hand to hand. Klein was killed. Private David Shirazi lay down across the wire so that others could cross it. *(israeli)*
- A Syrian report quoted after the war tells of soldiers who held on alone: a sergeant who knocked out the lead tank before he was killed, and a private, Hajj al-Din, who fired a heavy gun by himself and hit two tanks. *(arab: Syrian)*
- A later Syrian account says some men at Tel Faher refused an order to retreat and kept firing until they were killed. No named source confirms it. *(disputed)*
- Tel Faher fell at about six in the evening, after a fight of three to seven hours by different counts. About thirty-one to thirty-six Israelis and about sixty-two Syrians were killed there. *(estimate)*
- By night Israel held a strip about eight kilometres wide on the northern heights. One Syrian account says an armoured brigade ordered forward that night went back toward Damascus instead. *(disputed)*

### 25. Quneitra
*Map: Golan · 10 June, morning to half past six in the evening*
- Through the night UN observers logged heavy explosions and gunfire north of the Sea of Galilee. In the morning Israeli forces took Banias in the north; whether it was Golani or the 8th Armoured is reported both ways. *(disputed)*
- At about half past eight in the morning, Radio Damascus announced that Quneitra, the main town on the heights, had fallen. Israeli troops were still hours away. *(established)*
- Two hours later Syria broadcast a correction. The information minister, Muhammad al-Zu'bi, later said the first statement was Communiqué Number 66 and that he had first refused to read it. *(arab: Syrian)*
- Damascus radio announced the fall of Quneitra before Israeli troops arrived. Who ordered it is still argued: some Syrians blame the defence minister, Hafez al-Assad; others speak of panic or a plain error. *(disputed)*
- Hearing the news, Syrian soldiers pulled back and many civilians left. A boy from Quneitra remembered his father taking the family away after the broadcast, thinking they would be gone a short time. *(arab: Syrian)*
- Yusif Tannous, who worked with a UN team in Quneitra, stayed until the last day, then walked to Damascus, and wept when he heard the town had fallen. *(arab: Syrian)*
- At about half past two in the afternoon, Israeli accounts say, Mandler's brigade drove into Quneitra and found it empty. Israel later told the UN four o'clock, and at the UN that morning Israel had denied taking it at all. *(disputed)*
- In Moscow that morning, Prime Minister Alexei Kosygin warned President Lyndon Johnson on the hotline that the Soviet Union might take "an independent decision", including military action. Johnson sent the Sixth Fleet east as a signal, and the Soviet Union broke off relations with Israel. *(established)*
- The UN's chief observer, General Odd Bull, met Dayan and arranged a ceasefire. It took effect at half past six in the evening, Israel time. *(established)*
- In the last hour, helicopters landed Israeli troops at Butmiya, and a brigade took Mas'ade on the slopes of Mount Hermon. *(israeli)*
- Syria told the UN that Israel kept moving after the ceasefire, reaching Rafid more than three hours late, and Mount Hermon. Israel said it did not advance beyond the line. The UN's chief observer reported Israeli units still advancing on the 10th. *(disputed)*
- About one hundred and fifteen Israelis were killed on the Syrian front. Syrian dead are counted anywhere from about one hundred and fifty to two thousand five hundred; Syria never published its losses. *(estimate)*
- Before the war, between ninety thousand and one hundred and thirty thousand people lived on the Golan. In August 1967 an Israeli census found about six thousand, most of them Druze. *(estimate)*
- Some fled the fighting, some were driven out, and many villages on the Golan were destroyed afterwards. The Red Cross concluded that most were expelled, by force or under pressure; Israeli officials said they left on their own. *(disputed)*

## Part 7: After the six days

### 26. After the six days
*Map: Region · 9 June to 22 November 1967*
- On the evening of the 9th of June, Gamal Abdel Nasser spoke to Egypt on television. He took responsibility for what he called *al-Naksa*, "the setback", and said he would resign. *(established)*
- Crowds filled the streets of Cairo at once. A student, Sabry Mahmoud, remembered marching to Nasser's house; he said the protests went on for two days. How spontaneous they were is still argued. On the 10th Nasser withdrew his resignation. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- When the guns fell silent, Israel held the Sinai Peninsula to the Suez Canal, except Port Fuad; the Gaza Strip; the West Bank, including East Jerusalem; and the western Golan Heights. *(established)*
- The area under Israel's control was now about four times larger than before. About one million Arabs came under Israeli rule. *(estimate)*
- On the 11th the UN Security Council condemned the violations of the ceasefire and called on troops that had moved forward after it to go back. The last UN peacekeepers left Egypt on the 17th. *(established)*
- On the night of the 10th of June, Israel demolished the Mughrabi Quarter in front of the Western Wall, to open a wide plaza. Its residents, about six hundred and fifty people in about a hundred and thirty-five houses, were given hours to leave. *(established)*
- Accounts differ on the numbers and the dates, and on whether people died; one report names a woman killed when her house was knocked down. Mohammad Abu Znaid, then ten, remembered walking barefoot with his family to Shu'fat. *(disputed)*
- Israel's mayor, Teddy Kollek, called the old houses slums and said pilgrims needed room. Ya'akov Salman, the officer who carried out the order, later called it one of the hardest of his life. *(israeli)*
- Across the West Bank and Gaza, between about two hundred and eighty thousand and three hundred and twenty-five thousand Palestinians were displaced, most of them across the Jordan River. For many it was the second time since 1948. *(estimate)*
- Many fled the fighting and the air attacks. Some were driven out: villages near Latrun were destroyed. Israel let about fourteen thousand people back by the end of August. *(disputed)*
- From the Golan, more than one hundred thousand Syrians left or were driven out. The Druze villages, among them Majdal Shams, remained. *(estimate)*
- At Khartoum, from late August to the 1st of September, Arab leaders declared what became known as the "three noes": no peace with Israel, no recognition, no negotiation. Some historians read it as defiance; others say Arab leaders read it as leaving room for indirect deals. *(disputed)*
- On the 22nd of November, the Security Council passed Resolution 242 unanimously. It called for Israeli withdrawal "from territories occupied" in the war, and for every state in the area to live in peace within "secure and recognized boundaries". *(established)*
- The English text says "territories", not "the territories"; the French says "des territoires". Israel and the British and American drafters said this did not mean all the territories; the Arab states and the Soviet Union said it did. The argument has never been settled. *(disputed)*

## Debrief

### Losses (all estimates)
- Israel: between about seven hundred and eighty and nine hundred and eighty soldiers killed, depending on what is counted, and about four and a half thousand wounded. About twenty Israeli civilians were killed, most of them in Jerusalem. *(estimate)*
- Egypt: Egypt never published its dead. Estimates run from about three thousand to fifteen thousand killed or missing. Egypt itself spoke of about ten thousand soldiers and fifteen hundred officers lost, and many died of thirst in the desert. More than four thousand were taken prisoner. *(estimate)*
- Jordan: about seven hundred killed or missing, by the count of the Jordanian historian Samir Mutawi. A larger figure, about six thousand, counts the wounded and missing too. *(estimate)*
- Syria: from about one thousand to two thousand five hundred killed, by most counts; lower figures exist too. Syria never published its losses. *(estimate)*
- Iraq: about ten killed and thirty wounded, mostly from the air. *(estimate)*
- Civilians: no reliable count exists for Palestinian civilians killed. By September 1967, nearly one hundred and fifty Palestinians were killed trying to cross back from Jordan, according to Israeli military records reported later. *(estimate)*
- The UN: fifteen peacekeepers were killed, fourteen Indians and one Brazilian, mostly in Gaza. One count gives twenty-seven. *(estimate)*
- At sea: thirty-four Americans died on the *Liberty*. *(established)*

### What changed
- Israel held land about four times its own size. It annexed East Jerusalem at the end of June 1967. The UN General Assembly called this invalid, and most countries do not recognise it. *(established)*
- With the land came military rule over about a million Palestinians and Syrians, and the start of Israeli settlements, the first in 1967 and 1968. Most countries and the UN consider the settlements illegal under international law; Israel disputes this. *(established)*
- The fighting did not end. A war of attrition went on along the Suez Canal until 1970, and in October 1973 Egypt and Syria attacked to win back the land they had lost. *(established)*
- Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty in 1979, and Israel returned the whole of Sinai by 1982. Jordan made peace with Israel in 1994. *(established)*
- In 1981 Israel extended its law to the Golan Heights. Most countries and the UN do not recognise this, and consider the Golan occupied Syrian territory. *(established)*
- Resolution 242's formula, land for peace, became the starting point for most peace plans that followed. *(established)*
- For Palestinians, Syrians and Egyptians, the war is remembered as *al-Naksa*, the setback. Many Israelis remember it as a deliverance, and Israel marks the taking of East Jerusalem each year as Jerusalem Day. *(established)*

### Still argued about
- Who started it: whether Israel struck first to stop an attack that was coming, or launched a war Nasser did not intend to fight. Historians still disagree on whether Nasser meant to fight. *(disputed)*
- The *Liberty*: a mistake, as Israel and the American inquiries found, or a deliberate attack, as many survivors believe. *(disputed)*
- The refugees: how many fled the fighting, and how many were driven out, in the West Bank and on the Golan. *(disputed)*
- The Golan: whether the attack was needed to end the shelling, as Eshkol and the kibbutzim said, or a choice Dayan himself later called a mistake. And who ordered the early announcement of Quneitra's fall. *(disputed)*
- The numbers: the dead on almost every side, the aircraft destroyed, and the people displaced are all still given as ranges. *(estimate)*
