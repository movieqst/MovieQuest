**The approach:**
- **The whole war, in seven parts,** following the agreed plan: the crisis, the air strike, Sinai and Gaza, Jerusalem and the West Bank, the USS Liberty, the Golan, and the aftermath. Each part follows one front; the fronts overlap in time, and the clock in the corner of the map shows where we are.
- **Told from every side's sources, without taking sides.** Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian, Iraqi, Palestinian and Israeli accounts each have their room; where they disagree, the narration says who says what.
- **Chapter numbers** run in page order (1–26). The timeline's numbers differ for chapters 12–21 (timeline.md section 2): Sinai's 16, 19, 20, 21 are here 12–15, and Jerusalem's 12–15, 17, 18 are here 16–21.

**Tags** (one per line; unverified material is never narrated as fact):

| Tag | Meaning for this production |
|---|---|
| established | Strongly supported factual statement; not a claim that every government agrees. |
| israeli | A specifically Israeli account or interpretation, named in the spoken words where relevant. |
| arab: Egyptian / Jordanian / Syrian / Iraqi / Palestinian | A specifically identified account or interpretation; not an assertion that the speaker represents everyone in that population. |
| disputed | Identifiable accounts disagree on the same question. Name the disagreement. |
| estimate | An uncertain quantity with a defined date, geographical scope and category. |
| reconstruction | A scene or sequence inferred beyond the surviving evidence; explicitly identify it as such. |

An authentic quotation recalled decades later is testimony, not automatically a reconstruction. A witness's nationality does not make them a source for a side. That someone made a statement can be established while its contents remain unproved. A line mixing different kinds of evidence is split, each part with its own tag.

**Clocks:** Israel time (Amman the same; Cairo and Damascus one hour ahead; New York six hours behind). For every precise hour taken from a foreign source, the original time standard is recorded before conversion (UN reports often use GMT). Where no source gives a time, or the source's clock is unresolved: morning, afternoon, evening or night.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Six-Day War, fought from the 5th to the 10th of June 1967. Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria, with Iraqi forces also taking part. We begin with the tensions before the war, then follow a false Soviet warning and the crisis of May 1967.

## Introduction

### 1. THE SIX-DAY WAR
- In June 1967 Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria on three fronts at once. It began on the morning of the 5th of June with an Israeli air strike on Egypt's airfields. It ended on the evening of the 10th, with a ceasefire on the Golan Heights. *(established)*
- When it was over, Israel had captured territory more than three times the size of pre-war Israel. Hundreds of thousands of people fled or were driven from their homes. Roughly a million people remained in the occupied areas, under Israeli military control. *(estimate)*
- It became widely known as the Six-Day War. In Arabic it is often remembered as an-Naksa, meaning "the setback". *(established)*

### 2. The land and its peoples
- The war of 1948 ended in 1949 with agreements to stop the fighting, called armistices. Israel signed them with Egypt, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon. These agreements stopped the fighting but did not make peace. They generally did not settle final borders; the line with Lebanon followed an existing international boundary. *(established)*
- Egypt ran the Gaza Strip. Jordan held the West Bank, and East Jerusalem with its Old City. Israel held West Jerusalem. Mount Scopus stayed an Israeli enclave: a small area surrounded by territory held by the other side. Syria held the Golan Heights. Below them were demilitarised zones, where the armistice restricted military forces. *(established)*
- During the 1948 war, about seven hundred thousand Palestinians fled or were expelled from their homes. *(estimate)*
- Palestinians call it the Nakba, "the catastrophe". Many still lived in refugee camps in 1967. In this war, many would be uprooted a second time. *(established)*

### 3. Suez, 1956
- In 1956 Israel invaded Sinai in coordination with Britain and France. Under pressure from the United States and the Soviet Union, it withdrew in 1957. *(established)*
- A United Nations peacekeeping force, called UNEF, began deploying in late 1956. As the invading forces withdrew, it moved into posts in Gaza and Sinai, on the Egyptian side of the line only. One of its posts was at Sharm el-Sheikh, above the Straits of Tiran: Israel's sea road to its port of Eilat. *(established)*
- Israel warned that closing the straits again would be grounds for military action. *(established)*

### 4. The great powers
- This was during the Cold War, the rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union armed Egypt and Syria with MiG jets and Soviet tanks, and gave them political backing. *(established)*
- Israel's air force flew mostly French jets, such as the Mirage. The United States had close ties with both Israel and Jordan. Jordan's army used American Patton tanks; its air force flew British Hunter jets. *(established)*

### 5. Water and the border, 1964 to 1967
- Israel's National Water Carrier drew water from the Sea of Galilee. In January 1964 the Arab League decided to divert two of the Jordan River's sources, the Hasbani and the Banias, away from it. Syria began digging in 1965. That year, Israeli tanks and artillery fired on the works. *(established)*
- In the demilitarised zones below the Golan, disputes over cultivation and exchanges of fire repeatedly led to clashes. *(established)*
- In interviews published after his death, Moshe Dayan said Israel often sent tractors in knowing the Syrians would shoot. *(israeli)*
- From 1965 Fatah, a small Palestinian group backed by Syria, raided Israel. *(established)*
- Then a mine killed three men in an Israeli army patrol. After that, on the 13th of November 1966, a large Israeli force attacked the West Bank village of as-Samu. It destroyed many houses and fought Jordanian troops sent toward the village. The UN Security Council censured Israel, meaning it formally condemned the raid. *(established)*
- On the 7th of April 1967 a fight in the zone near the Sea of Galilee grew into an air battle that reached Damascus. *(established)*
- Israel reported shooting down six Syrian MiGs. Syria also claimed an Israeli aircraft; Israel denied losing one. *(disputed)*

### 6. The armies
- With its reserves, part-time soldiers called back into service, Israel had about two hundred and sixty-four thousand men and about eight hundred tanks. *(estimate)*
- Egypt sent about a hundred thousand men and nine hundred to nine hundred and fifty tanks into Sinai. Egypt's air force had about four hundred and twenty combat aircraft nationwide. At the same time, part of its army was still fighting a war in Yemen. *(estimate)*
- Jordan's army numbered about fifty-five thousand men. An estimated forty-five thousand were deployed on the West Bank. The tank totals vary by source and area counted. *(estimate)*
- Syria had perhaps fifty to seventy-five thousand men and about a hundred and twenty combat aircraft. Iraq prepared forces to send into Jordan. *(estimate)*
- The Arab states together had larger armed forces, but not all their troops could fight on the same front. Training, air power, supply and command mattered as well as numbers. *(established)*
- Armies are organised in units of different sizes. A division is a large formation made up of several brigades. A brigade is made up of several battalions. *(established; editorial definition of terms)*

### 7. How this page tells it
- This page draws on Israeli, Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian and Palestinian sources, and on American, Soviet and United Nations papers. *(established)*
- Each line carries a tag. "Established" means the evidence strongly supports a statement. A side's name identifies an account from that side. "Disputed" means identifiable sources disagree. "Estimate" marks an uncertain number. "Reconstruction" marks a scene pieced together from incomplete evidence. *(established; editorial definition, not a historical claim)*
- Clock times are Israel time unless we say otherwise. Where no source gives a time, we say morning, afternoon, evening or night. *(established; editorial convention)*

## Part 1: The crisis

### 1. The false warning
*Map: Region · 13 to 15 May 1967*
- On the 13th of May 1967 a Soviet intelligence report reached Egypt. It said Israel was massing about eleven brigades of troops on the Syrian border. *(established)*
- The report was false. The reported Israeli troop concentration was not found. *(established)*
- Accounts describe several channels through which the Soviet warning reached Egypt. *(established)*
- Why Moscow sent the report is still argued. Historians disagree about Soviet motives: to deter Israel, support Syria, or increase pressure through a crisis. *(disputed)*
- On the 14th of May Egypt put its forces on alert. Columns of troops and tanks paraded through Cairo on their way to Sinai, in full view. *(established)*
- The same day President Nasser sent his army Chief of Staff, General Mohamed Fawzi, to Damascus. Fawzi later wrote that he found no Israeli build-up at all. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Yet the Egyptian troops kept moving into Sinai. *(established)*
- In Israel, the army's Chief of Staff, Lieutenant-General Yitzhak Rabin, asked to call up the reserves. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol was also Defence Minister. He refused, and allowed only a higher state of alert. *(israeli)*
- On the 15th of May, at a song festival in West Jerusalem, Shuli Natan sang Naomi Shemer's new song, "Jerusalem of Gold", for the first time. It longs for the Old City, then in Jordanian hands. It became an unofficial song of the war for many Israelis. *(established)*
- For the Palestinian families who lived in the Old City, it was home. Haifa Khalidi was nineteen in 1967. She later recalled that she had been born in a house beside the Mughrabi, or Moroccan, Quarter, next to the Western Wall. *(arab: Palestinian)*

### 2. The peacekeepers go
*Map: Region · 16 to 19 May 1967*
- At about ten at night on the 16th of May, in Gaza, the commander of the UN force was handed a letter from General Fawzi. The commander was an Indian officer, Major-General Indar Jit Rikhye. *(established)*
- The letter said Egyptian troops were already gathered in Sinai. It asked the UN to withdraw its soldiers from their observation posts along the border. Rikhye answered that he could not move without orders from the Secretary-General, the head of the United Nations. *(established)*
- In New York, Secretary-General U Thant required a formal request from Egypt's government. He treated the request as a decision about UNEF's continued presence, not just a few border posts. *(established)*
- On the 18th of May Egypt's Foreign Minister, Mahmoud Riad, formally withdrew Egypt's consent for the force to stay. Egyptian troops were already blocking UN soldiers from their posts. U Thant agreed the same day. *(established)*
- The force stood on Egyptian soil only with Egypt's consent. U Thant asked whether Israel would take UNEF on its side of the line. Israel's UN ambassador, Gideon Rafael, said that was "entirely unacceptable". *(established)*
- U Thant said the force could not remain without Egypt's consent. Critics, then and since, disputed how quickly he acted. Some say he should have taken it to the General Assembly, the meeting of all UN members, or played for time. *(disputed)*
- U Thant ordered withdrawal to begin on the 19th of May. *(established)*
- UNEF did not vanish overnight. The Canadians were flown out by the 31st of May. Indian, Brazilian and Swedish soldiers were still in the area when war came. *(established)*

### 3. The straits close
*Map: Region · 22 to 23 May 1967*
- As UN troops withdrew from Sharm el-Sheikh, Egyptian troops took over its positions above the Straits of Tiran. *(established)*
- On the 22nd of May Rabin told the Israeli cabinet that the Egyptian forces in Sinai were deployed for defence, not for attack. *(israeli)*
- That night Nasser spoke to pilots at the Bir Gifgafa air base in Sinai. He announced that from the next day the Gulf of Aqaba was closed to Israeli ships. It was also closed to any ship carrying strategic goods to Israel: supplies important to the military, including oil. *(established)*
- Nasser said: "Under no circumstances can we permit the Israeli flag to pass through the Gulf of Aqaba." The wording varies with the translation. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Eilat was Israel's only Red Sea port, and the way in for its oil from Iran. *(established)*
- Israel treated the closure as grounds for war, as it had warned in 1957. *(established)*
- The American President, Lyndon Johnson, later said the closure was the step that, more than any other, made war likely. *(established)*
- Anwar Sadat, the Speaker of Egypt's National Assembly, later wrote that he had backed the closure. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 23rd of May Johnson publicly rejected Egypt's claim to control shipping in the Gulf. *(established)*
- Over the following days, American and British officials discussed an international naval effort to reopen the straits. *(established)*

### 4. Waiting
*Map: Region · 23 May to 3 June 1967*
- Israelis call the next twelve days the hamtana, "the waiting". The army stood mobilised, called up and ready for war. The mobilisation disrupted work and put the economy under growing strain. The cabinet chose to give diplomacy time. *(established)*
- On the 23rd of May the former Prime Minister, David Ben-Gurion, criticised Rabin for the mobilisation. Soon after, Rabin collapsed from strain and was given drugs to make him sleep. He later said he slept for about a day. *(israeli)*
- Accounts differ on the date and on how long he was out of action. *(disputed)*
- Foreign Minister Abba Eban flew west. In Paris, on the 24th of May, President de Gaulle warned Israel not to fire first. In London, Prime Minister Harold Wilson backed free passage through the straits. *(israeli)*
- The UN Security Council met from the 24th of May, but failed to agree on measures that resolved the crisis. U Thant went to Cairo, without result. *(established)*
- In Washington on the 25th and 26th of May, Eban warned of an imminent attack, one about to happen. American intelligence disagreed. Israel agreed to wait up to two weeks while Washington tried other ways. *(established)*
- In speeches in late May, Nasser threatened that if war came, its aim would be to destroy Israel. He also said Egypt would not fire first. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Historians still disagree on whether Nasser meant to fight, or hoped to win without war. *(disputed)*
- On the evening of the 26th of May in Washington, Johnson met Eban. In Israel it was already the small hours of the 27th. Johnson said his advisers saw no attack coming, and that Israel would win if attacked. "Israel will not be alone," he said, "unless it decides to go it alone." *(established)*
- Accounts describe an Egyptian first-strike plan called "Dawn". They disagree over whether Nasser approved it and how close it came to being carried out. *(disputed)*
- On the 27th or 28th of May the Israeli cabinet split nine to nine on going to war at once. Eshkol sided with Eban, and diplomacy got a few more days. *(israeli)*
- On the 28th of May Eshkol spoke live on the radio. He read from a text corrected by hand, and stumbled over the words. Many Israelis heard it as weakness. *(established)*
- David Bahat, a seventeen-year-old paratrooper, later recalled waiting with his unit in the Negev desert for three weeks. *(israeli)*
- Suraya Mahmoud was eight years old and living in Cairo. She later recalled hearing the children in her street chanting that the army would march into Tel Aviv. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 5. Hussein in Cairo
*Map: Region · 30 May to 4 June 1967*
- On the 30th of May King Hussein of Jordan flew to Cairo, unannounced, with his prime minister and his army and air force chiefs. Nasser had lately called him an "imperialist lackey", a servant of the Western powers. Now Nasser said their differences had vanished "in one moment". *(established)*
- They signed a defence pact on live radio. An attack on one would count as an attack on both. In war, Jordan's forces would come under Egyptian command. An Egyptian general, Abdul Munim Riad, was sent to Amman to lead the Jordanian front. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts saw this as the moment the ring around Israel closed. *(israeli)*
- Jordanian accounts describe a king pulled into a war he neither wanted nor expected. Hussein later said he feared that staying out would tear Jordan apart. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- On the 31st of May Iraqi troops and tanks began moving into Jordan. The same day Hussein asked the United States to stay neutral. *(established)*
- On the 31st of May and the 1st of June, Meir Amit, head of Israel's intelligence service, the Mossad, was in Washington. He told Defense Secretary Robert McNamara that Israel could not stay mobilised for long. The American record does not establish an explicit American authorisation for Israel to attack. *(established)*
- Accounts differ over whether Amit understood Washington's position as permission to act. *(disputed)*
- On the 1st of June Eshkol gave up the Defence Ministry to Moshe Dayan. The opposition leader, Menachem Begin, joined a government of national unity. *(established)*
- On the 2nd of June Israel's generals pressed for war; most ministers still hesitated. *(israeli)*
- General Fawzi later wrote that on the same day, Nasser told his generals to expect an Israeli attack on the 4th or 5th of June. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 3rd of June a letter from Johnson asked again that Israel not start the fighting. At the General Staff, the army's high command, Rabin warned that day that "a military and diplomatic noose" was tightening. He said delay could let Jordan, Iraq and Syria join. *(israeli)*
- The same day two Egyptian commando battalions were flown to Amman. *(established)*
- Mahmoud Muali, a Palestinian serving in Jordanian army radio communications, later recalled expecting war by the 2nd of June. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- On Sunday the 4th of June the Israeli cabinet met. The head of military intelligence, Aharon Yariv, reported that Jordan's army was under Egyptian command. He also reported Iraqi units entering Jordan. Even the cautious minister Haim-Moshe Shapira accepted that time had run out. Eshkol reminded them that "the Arabs will remain here", whatever the war's outcome. *(israeli)*
- That afternoon the cabinet voted for war. The Israeli Defence Ministry archive's account records eighteen ministers voting for military action. Eshkol and Dayan were left to fix the hour. That evening Rabin set it for the next morning. *(israeli)*
- Iraq also committed forces to the Arab coalition. *(established)*
- That day in Cairo, the American journalist James Reston wrote that Egypt did not want war. Egypt's Vice-President was due in Washington for talks on the 7th of June. *(established)*

## Part 2: The air strike

### 6. Operation Focus
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, morning to midday*
- On Monday the 5th of June, at a quarter to eight Israel time, Israeli jets struck about ten Egyptian airfields at once. In Cairo, an hour ahead, it was a quarter to nine. *(established)*
- Israel called it Operation Focus, in Hebrew *Moked*. The plan is credited to Ezer Weizman, the former air force commander. It was carried out by his successor, Mordechai Hod, known as Motti. *(israeli)*
- By Israeli accounts, the jets flew in groups of four. They went west over the sea, then turned south. They flew about twenty metres above the waves, in radio silence. Hod later called the wait "the longest 45 minutes of my life." *(israeli)*
- In Egypt, that same morning, Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer was flying from Almaza, near Cairo, to Bir Thamada in Sinai. With him was the air force commander, Sidki Mahmoud. *(established)*
- Egyptian accounts describe restrictions on anti-aircraft fire during Amer's inspection flight. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw large formations of aircraft, and sent a warning to Cairo in code. Egypt's foreign minister, Mahmoud Riad, later wrote that the secret code had just been changed. The message was not read in time. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Over each base the jets climbed sharply. They hit the runways first, then the aircraft parked in the open. *(established)*
- At El Arish, the Egyptian fighter pilot Fikry el-Gahramy later recalled: "There was no warning at all." *(arab: Egyptian)*
- At Kabrit, Flight Lieutenant Mustafa Hafez, aged twenty-six, was in the dining room when the bombs fell. He took off in whatever fighter was ready. Then he found his guns would not fire, because of a switch in the cockpit. "I was not actually due to fly that morning," he later said. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- A second wave attacked Egyptian airfields again, including additional targets. Egyptian bombers that had been moved south to Luxor for safety were attacked there at midday. *(israeli)*
- The aircraft totals vary. Sources count aircraft lost in the air, aircraft destroyed on the ground, and aircraft written off later in different ways. *(established)*
- Israeli-based totals for Egypt's losses that day run from about two hundred and ninety to three hundred and forty aircraft. An Arab chronology gives two hundred and nine. *(estimate; Israeli-derived totals; Al Jazeera Arabic, "Diary of the 1967 war")*
- On that first day, Israel lost about seventeen to nineteen aircraft of its own, most of them to anti-aircraft fire. *(estimate)*
- In Cairo, Nasser's former deputy, Abdul Latif al-Baghdadi, later wrote about that evening. Amer, he wrote, first claimed seventy-three Israeli planes shot down. Then Amer showed a list: forty-seven Egyptian aircraft left, thirty-five of them able to fly. Meanwhile, Radio Cairo was still announcing victories. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 7. Three air forces
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, 11:50–evening; 6 and 7 June*
- Jordanian accounts say Jordan's radar had read the heavy air traffic as Egyptian aircraft. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Cairo told Amman that Egypt's air force was striking Israel. Jordan, Syria and Iraq sent their own aircraft in. *(established)*
- At about ten to twelve, Jordanian Hawker Hunter jets from Mafraq attacked near Netanya. One account says they were aiming at an air base, missed, and hit a factory, killing one civilian and wounding seven. Whether three or four Hunters flew is disputed. *(disputed)*
- Syrian jets struck northern Israel. An Arab chronology names the Haifa oil refineries and Megiddo airfield as targets. Israeli accounts say three Syrian jets were shot down. *(disputed; Al Jazeera Arabic, "Diary of the 1967 war")*
- From about a quarter to one, Israel's third wave hit Mafraq and then Amman. By Israeli accounts, Jordan's air bases and most of the aircraft there had been put out of action that afternoon. *(israeli)*
- Sources differ on Jordan's aircraft losses, partly because they count different aircraft and locations. *(disputed)*
- Between two and four in the afternoon, Israeli jets struck Syrian bases around Damascus and beyond. Israel claimed about two-thirds of Syria's air force, fifty to sixty-one aircraft. Other accounts say Syria had spread its aircraft to distant bases, and lost far fewer. *(disputed)*
- At about three, four Israeli Vautour bombers struck the Iraqi base called H-3, in the western desert. Israel claimed about ten aircraft destroyed there. A single Lebanese Hunter was also shot down. *(israeli)*
- That night the chief of staff, Yitzhak Rabin, announced on the radio that four hundred enemy planes had been destroyed. Earlier, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan had asked the press not to publish the number. *(israeli)*
- Bahat also recalled hearing an Egyptian broadcast claiming that Tel Aviv had fallen. *(israeli)*
- The next morning, the 6th of June, an Iraqi bomber dropped bombs on a main street of Netanya. *(established; IDF timeline)*
- Anti-aircraft guns shot the bomber down near Afula. It fell on an army camp, killing its crew and about a dozen Israeli reservists. *(estimate)*
- On the 7th of June, Iraqi accounts credit Iraqi, Jordanian and Pakistani pilots flying Hunters from H-3 with shooting down two Vautours and a Mirage. *(arab: Iraqi)*
- Over the whole war, Israel claimed to have destroyed about four hundred and fifty Arab aircraft. Other counts run from about three hundred and seventy to four hundred and forty. Israel lost between twenty-six and forty-eight aircraft, depending on what is counted. *(estimate)*
- Israel had gained air superiority: its aircraft could operate much more freely, while Arab air support was greatly reduced. *(established)*

## Part 3: Sinai and Gaza

### 8. Through Rafah
*Map: North Sinai · 5 June, 07:50–night; El Arish 6–7 June*
- As the jets struck, Israel's Southern Command sent the code word "Red Sheet". Its commander was Major-General Yeshayahu Gavish. Three divisions moved into Sinai: Israel Tal's in the north, Avraham Yoffe's in the centre, and Ariel Sharon's at Abu-Ageila. *(israeli)*
- Israel had about forty-five to seventy thousand men and six hundred and fifty to seven hundred tanks in the south. Egypt had about a hundred thousand men and some nine hundred tanks, in three lines of defence. *(estimate)*
- At about ten to eight, Major-General Tal's division crossed the border at two points: opposite Nahal Oz, and south of Khan Yunis. Colonel Shmuel Gonen's 7th Armoured Brigade led. *(israeli)*
- Patton tanks pushed slowly through the streets of Khan Yunis. The historian Michael Oren writes that the railway junction fell in just over four hours. *(israeli)*
- At about noon, Israeli tanks attacked the Egyptian brigade dug in at Rafah junction, which had heavy Soviet-built tanks. *(israeli)*
- In the afternoon, at Sheikh Zuweid, Gonen's brigade was pinned down, then broke through. A young company commander, Avigdor Kahalani, was caught in an ambush. He was badly burned when his Patton tank caught fire. *(israeli)*
- West of Sheikh Zuweid lay the Jiradi, a narrow road between high dunes. At about half past two, Israeli Centurion tanks raced through it. Then the Egyptian brigade holding it closed the road behind them. *(israeli)*
- At half past four the Pattons met heavy fire from bunkers and anti-tank guns, and their commander, Ehud Elad, was killed. Tal sent in Colonel Rafael Eitan's paratroopers to help clear the pass. *(israeli)*
- The Jiradi was taken, lost and taken again. The Egyptian defenders fought on from their trenches after midnight. Israel's supply trucks only got through at about two in the morning. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts say the Jiradi fighting caused heavy losses. *(israeli)*
- Israeli forces took El Arish airfield and entered the town during the advance along the coast. Accounts date these stages differently. *(disputed)*
- Israeli accounts say snipers fired on them from the rooftops of El Arish. *(israeli)*
- Further south, Major-General Yoffe's reservists drove their Centurions through dunes Egypt thought impassable. They covered about fifty-five kilometres in about nine hours, mostly in first gear. *(israeli)*
- At the Bir Lahfan crossroads they blocked an Egyptian armoured brigade and a mechanised brigade, a large unit of infantry carried in armoured vehicles. The Sinai front commander, General Abd el-Mohsen Murtagi, had sent them north. There they fought Egyptian forces trying to reach El Arish. *(israeli)*

### 9. Gaza
*Map: North Sinai · 5–8 June*
- The Gaza Strip was a narrow coastal strip under Egyptian administration, crowded with refugees from the 1948 war and their children. *(established)*
- It was defended by the 20th Palestinian Division of the Palestine Liberation Army, a Palestinian military force under Arab state command. The division was under Egyptian military command, with Egyptian units beside it. *(established)*
- Defence Minister Dayan had ordered his troops to keep out of the Strip. Then guns in Gaza shelled Nirim and Kissufim, two kibbutzim, Israeli communal farming settlements. Rabin overruled Dayan, and sent in Colonel Yehuda Reshef's 11th Mechanised Brigade. *(israeli)*
- The last UN peacekeepers were still in their camps, waiting to leave. At half past two in the afternoon, the Indian battalion's main camp was shelled. The UN reported that Israeli artillery hit the Indian camp, killing an officer and a soldier. *(established)*
- The same day, by the UN's report, Israeli aircraft attacked a white-painted UN convoy with gunfire, south of Khan Yunis. Three Indian soldiers were killed. At the Rafah camp, a Brazilian soldier was wounded. *(established)*
- The UN's confirmed list records fifteen peacekeepers killed: fourteen from India and one from Brazil. *(established)*
- By sunset, Israeli troops held the Ali Muntar ridge above Gaza City, but were pushed back from the city itself. *(israeli)*
- The fighting also killed civilians, including the American Life photographer Paul Schutzer. *(established)*
- On the morning of the 6th of June, Eitan's paratroopers and the armour took Gaza City. *(israeli)*
- Israeli troops had broken into Khan Yunis on the first day, but the town and its refugee camp held out longer. Accounts differ on whether the fighting there ended on the 7th or the 8th of June. *(disputed)*
- In Beach refugee camp, Sarah Ayoub, then aged twenty-four, later recalled that she was baking bread when the explosions began. The next morning, she said, she saw soldiers across the camp, and houses destroyed. Her husband, Munther, was among the men rounded up. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- In al-Maghazi camp, Hajj Jumaa Abu Hadrous, a refugee from Jaffa, later recalled seeing tanks gather at the edge of the camp. He said the troops entered at night, with aircraft overhead, and that he fled with his wife and three children to Zawaida. "We did not think it was a war," he said. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- Ghazai Misleh, then ten, later recalled carrying his little sister to Zawaida. After they returned home, Misleh recalled, his father warned against lighting a cooking fire. He said Israeli soldiers fired towards smoke, and his father feared their home would be hit. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- After visiting Gaza, UNRWA's commissioner-general said civilian casualties appeared higher there than in other areas he had visited. He gave no overall death toll. *(established)*

### 10. Approach to Um Katef
*Map: Abu-Ageila · 5 June, 08:15–19:00*
- Abu-Ageila was a crossroads in eastern Sinai, where the road from the Israeli border meets the roads west into the peninsula. East of it rose the Um Katef ridge. *(established)*
- Here the Egyptian 2nd Infantry Division, under Major-General Sa'di Naguib, held three lines of trenches. They had concrete bunkers, minefields and barbed wire, all facing east. The dunes to the north were thought impassable, and had no mines. *(established)*
- Studies of the battle give about eight thousand Egyptian soldiers at Um Katef. They had a tank regiment of sixty-six tanks, twenty-two armoured vehicles carrying heavy guns, and a brigade of artillery further back. *(estimate)*
- Major-General Sharon had about fourteen to nineteen thousand men and about a hundred and fifty tanks. *(estimate)*
- His forces included Lieutenant-Colonel Mordechai Zippori's tanks and Colonel Kuti Adam's infantry brigade, which included reservists. There were also Danny Matt's paratroopers, six battalions of artillery, and Natke Nir's Centurion tanks. *(israeli)*
- The first Israeli probing attacks, in the morning and around midday, were stopped by minefields. Six or seven Centurions were lost. *(israeli)*
- Sharon's plan included an attack from the north, through the dunes. At one o'clock, civilian buses began carrying Adam's infantry toward the front. *(israeli)*
- North of the ridge, an Egyptian infantry battalion held a position called 181, with ten armoured vehicles carrying heavy guns. A second Israeli attack took it at about four in the afternoon. *(israeli)*
- Nir's Centurions drove west without stopping to refuel. Nir's force manoeuvred toward the rear of the Egyptian position. *(israeli)*
- Most of what is known of this battle comes from Israeli and Western studies. The sources are uneven, but Egyptian military accounts also describe the battle. *(established)*

### 11. The night assault
*Map: Abu-Ageila · 5 June 19:00 – 6 June 12:00; Jebel Libni into 7 June*
- From about seven in the evening, helicopters began carrying Matt's paratroopers to a landing area about five kilometres north of the Egyptian guns. Some lost their way. At about nine, Egyptian fire stopped the lift. *(israeli)*
- Accounts differ on how many helicopters and men took part. *(disputed)*
- Adam's infantry left their buses. They marched roughly ten to twelve kilometres through the sand, to the northern edge, or flank, of the Egyptian position. *(israeli)*
- Then Israel's guns opened fire, firing about six thousand shells. *(estimate)*
- Accounts differ on exactly when the shelling began, and how long it lasted. *(disputed)*
- At about a quarter past eleven, Adam's brigade went into the trenches. They advanced behind a creeping barrage: shellfire moving forward just ahead of the infantry. *(israeli)*
- At about half past eleven, the paratroopers attacked the Egyptian gun lines. Egyptian ammunition trucks blew up, causing the paratroopers' heaviest losses. The Egyptian guns largely fell silent. *(israeli)*
- The Egyptian infantry of the 12th Brigade fought trench by trench through the dark. By half past two in the morning, Israeli accounts say, only a third of the trenches had been cleared. *(israeli)*
- Engineers opened a first lane through the minefield at about half past two, losing a tank to a mine. They opened a second at about four. Zippori's tanks rolled through. At about half past four, Nir was badly wounded. *(israeli)*
- Israeli armour broke into the position before dawn. Israeli tanks attacked the Egyptian tank and anti-tank units from two sides: Centurions from the west and Shermans from the east. By morning, the Egyptian defence had largely collapsed. *(israeli)*
- By noon the crossroads was open. Yoffe's tanks moved toward Jebel Libni. Fighting there continued into the 7th of June. *(israeli)*
- Israel lost thirty-two to forty killed, more than a hundred and twenty wounded, and nineteen tanks. Egyptian losses included forty to sixty tanks. Estimates of the Egyptian dead vary, and cover different stages of the battle. *(estimate)*
- Military studies emphasise the combination of infantry, tanks, artillery and the attack through the dunes. *(established)*

### 12. The retreat order
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 6 June*
- By the 6th of June, Egypt's front line was broken at Rafah, the Jiradi and Abu-Ageila. Its second line, around Jebel Libni, was under attack. *(established)*
- Baghdadi later wrote about the night before, in Cairo. The war minister, Shams Badran, brought word that Khan Yunis had fallen and Rafah was besieged. Nasser, he wrote, withdrew to a bedroom. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 6th of June, Field Marshal Amer ordered a general withdrawal from Sinai. *(established)*
- The hour of the order is disputed. Nasser's account, through the journalist Heikal, says morning. Western histories say afternoon, after the news from Abu-Ageila. Others say evening. *(disputed)*
- General Fawzi later wrote that Amer gave him twenty minutes to draw up a plan. Fawzi brought a timetable over several days. Amer answered that he had already ordered the withdrawal. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- The words of the order are reported differently too. Fawzi's account has "within one night", with personal weapons only. The newspaper Al-Ahram has "within 24 hours". *(disputed)*
- Fawzi wrote that the orders bypassed the chain of command. Field commanders, he wrote, took orders from military police and intelligence officers. Fawzi blamed Amer. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- His account must be read with care: he was involved in the events and later became a critic of Amer's leadership. *(established; editor's note on the source)*
- By Fawzi's account, Badran guarded Amer's door, and the Soviet military attaché asked why combat units were not allowed to fight. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- The general withdrawal lacked an effective coordinated plan. Units heard the order at different times. Some units held together and fought; others broke up under air attack. *(established)*
- On the Israeli side, at about four in the afternoon, Sharon's division was turned south toward Nakhl. The race for the passes had begun. *(israeli)*

### 13. Sharm el-Sheikh
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 7–8 June*
- Sharm el-Sheikh, at the southern tip of Sinai, has guns overlooking the Straits of Tiran. Egypt's closing of the straits to Israeli shipping in May was one of the causes of the war. *(established)*
- A later naval account, quoting Israeli officers, says three small torpedo boats left Eilat on the night of the 6th of June. They sailed down the Gulf of Aqaba. *(israeli; Revista de Marina, 1974)*
- Paratroopers and commandos boarded helicopters and transport planes. Rabin judged a landing straight onto Sharm too risky. He aimed them first at El Tor, on the Gulf of Suez. *(israeli)*
- Air and naval reports then showed that Sharm had been mostly abandoned the day before. The force was sent there instead. *(israeli)*
- Israeli forces occupied Sharm el-Sheikh on the 7th of June. Accounts differ over whether Egyptian troops still resisted. *(disputed)*
- With Sharm el-Sheikh occupied, Israel announced that the straits were open again. *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers also landed at El Tor. The next day they linked up at Abu Zenima with Yoffe's tanks, coming down the Gulf of Suez coast. *(israeli)*

### 14. The passes
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 7–8 June*
- Between central Sinai and the Canal, the roads run through a few passes in the hills: Khatmia, Giddi and Mitla. These passes channelled many of the Egyptian units retreating west through central Sinai. *(established)*
- Columns of the Egyptian 3rd Infantry, 4th Armoured and 6th Mechanised Divisions headed for the passes. So did a special task force under Major-General Saad el-Shazly. *(established)*
- On the 7th of June, Tal sought battle at Bir Gifgafa with the Egyptian 4th Armoured Division of Major-General Sidki el-Ghoul. After about two hours, the Egyptians pulled back south. One Israeli account says much of the division escaped overnight. *(israeli)*
- The same day, Yoffe's southern force drove from Jebel Libni through Bir Hasana and Bir Thamada. It reached the eastern end of the Mitla Pass in the late afternoon. *(israeli)*
- At dusk, Colonel Iska Shadmi blocked the pass with nine Centurions, two being towed. He also had a few half-tracks, armoured vehicles with wheels at the front and tracks at the back, and three mortars. Three more tanks, out of fuel, were towed into place. *(israeli)*
- The Egyptian columns arrived into a traffic jam, by one account nearly five kilometres long, under Israeli air attack. Around midnight, the Israeli crews refuelled from improvised cans. *(israeli)*
- Egyptian tanks tried to break through in groups of fifteen or twenty. Of more than twenty armoured vehicles carrying heavy guns, one got through. *(israeli)*
- At first light on the 8th of June, only four Israeli Centurions could move. They held a column with twenty-eight Egyptian tanks until the jets returned. Many Egyptian soldiers had already left their vehicles in the night, and walked into the hills. *(israeli)*
- An aerial photograph of the burned vehicles at Mitla, by the UPI photographer Peter Skingley, won a World Press Photo prize that year. *(established)*
- At dawn on the 8th of June, near Nakhl, Sharon's division ambushed two brigades of the Egyptian 6th Mechanised Division. Sharon estimated sixty to seventy tanks, a hundred guns and about a thousand dead. Some tanks were found abandoned in working order. *(israeli)*
- Shazly commanded a mixed force near Kuntilla, close to the border. *(established)*
- Shazly says he never received the withdrawal order of the 6th of June. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- He says he was contacted only on the evening of the 7th. He says he marched about a hundred kilometres through the night, and was bombed at dawn, with more than a hundred killed. At dusk on the 8th, he says, he crossed a bridge over the Suez Canal. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 15. To the Canal
*Map: Central/South Sinai & passes · 8–9 June, with later accounts of prisoners and repatriation*
- At three in the morning on the 8th of June, Egyptian tanks hit an Israeli light-tank camp near Bir Gifgafa. They burned several vehicles before pulling back. *(israeli)*
- On the coast road, Lieutenant-Colonel Yisrael Granit's group reached El Qantara, the first Israelis at the Canal. *(israeli)*
- Near the Canal, Colonel Eitan was shot in the head and badly wounded. Aharon Davidi took over. *(israeli)*
- In the south, Yoffe's forces reached the Gulf of Suez. The Egyptians set the Ras Sudar oil fields on fire. Israeli sources disagree whether Ras Sudar was taken on the 8th or the 9th. *(disputed)*
- Captain David Caspi led his Centurions through the Giddi Pass, past a fresh Egyptian battalion, then raced for the Canal. He radioed that he could "spit on the canal." *(israeli)*
- An Israeli account places Tal's reconnaissance tanks at the Canal shortly after midnight on the 9th. *(israeli)*
- By the end of that day, Israel held all of Sinai up to the Canal, except Port Fuad. *(established)*
- On the 8th of June, Egypt notified the UN that it accepted the ceasefire, on condition that Israel also stopped firing. *(established)*
- Israel wanted to stand on the Canal before the ceasefire took effect. *(israeli)*
- Tens of thousands of Egyptian soldiers were left to walk west through the desert in the June heat. Thousands of Egyptian servicemen died in the fighting and retreat. The total is uncertain; some died from thirst and exhaustion. *(estimate)*
- Amin Mohammad Darwish was on duty near the border on the 5th of June. He later recalled walking for three days, past abandoned and burned-out weapons, before he was captured. He was then held in Israel for eight months. "I was humiliated," he said. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- After the fighting, Red Cross delegates helped Egyptian servicemen return home. *(established)*
- Israel captured between about four thousand three hundred and five thousand Egyptian prisoners, with about five hundred officers. Many were held at Atlit for months. *(estimate)*
- Egypt also held Israeli prisoners. The Red Cross records nine Israeli servicemen returning in the January 1968 exchange, and an Israeli pilot released earlier. *(established)*
- Israel lost about two hundred and seventy-five killed and eight hundred wounded in Sinai, by General Gavish's count. *(estimate)*
- The desert kept its dead for a long time. In 2024, Egyptian news reports identified remains found in North Sinai as those of Fawzy Mohammed Abdel-Muwla, a soldier killed in 1967. He was given a military funeral. *(arab: Egyptian)*

## Part 4: Jerusalem and the West Bank

### 16. Jordan opens fire
*Map: Jerusalem · 5 June, about 07:00–13:30*
- In 1967 Jerusalem was a divided city. The Old City and East Jerusalem were held by Jordan, together with the West Bank, then ruled by Jordan. West Jerusalem was Israeli, and a small Israeli enclave sat on Mount Scopus, cut off behind the Jordanian lines. *(established)*
- Under the pact signed in Cairo on the 30th of May, the Jordanian front was commanded by an Egyptian, Lieutenant-General Abdul Munim Riad. He took his orders from Field Marshal Amer in Cairo. King Hussein remained supreme commander. *(established)*
- At seven in the morning, the Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw heavy air traffic over Israel. Jordanian and Egyptian accounts say the warning was sent to Cairo. But Cairo had just changed its secret codes and could not read it in time. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At ten to nine, an aide phoned the palace in Amman: Radio Cairo was saying that Israel had attacked Egypt. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about nine, General Riad received a message from Amer. It claimed a major Egyptian air victory. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- The report of a major Egyptian air victory was false. *(established)*
- Riad ordered offensive operations. Hussein later wrote that, for Jordan, the war began at nine in the morning. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At half past nine, machine guns opened up along the line in Jerusalem, then mortars and recoilless rifles. The heavy shelling of West Jerusalem and of Israeli towns began between about ten and a quarter past eleven. The sources disagree on the exact start. *(disputed)*
- Israeli accounts add that soon after ten, Jordan's long-range guns shelled the Israeli air base at Ramat David and the outskirts of Tel Aviv. In these accounts, this is what made Israel strike back. *(israeli)*
- That morning Israel's Prime Minister, Levi Eshkol, sent a message through the Norwegian chief of the UN observers, General Odd Bull. If Jordan stayed out, Israel would not attack it. *(established)*
- Hussein later told historian Avi Shlaim that the message came after Jordanian aircraft had already gone into action. He said he answered that it was too late. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Exactly when the message reached Hussein, before or after Jordan opened fire, is still argued. *(disputed)*
- Hussein faced pressures from the pact, Egyptian command, reports from Cairo and politics at home. Historians disagree about how much freedom he still had to keep Jordan out. *(disputed)*
- In West Jerusalem, families went down to the shelters. Israeli figures speak of about 6,000 shells in two days, most in the first day, and about 1,000 apartments damaged. Shells hit the Biblical Zoo and the President's residence, and damaged the Chagall windows at Hadassah hospital. *(israeli)*
- A mother named Yeivin later told reporters that she sheltered behind sandbags with her three children, aged ten, nine and three, and about a dozen neighbours. She lay over her children for an hour, fearing a direct hit. *(israeli)*
- At half past eleven, Brigadier Ata Ali, commanding the Jordanian forces in Jerusalem, opened artillery fire on orders from West Bank headquarters. He asked Amman for air cover and was told it would come later. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Shortly after half past twelve, Israeli aircraft struck Jordan's air bases and destroyed most of its small air force on the ground. *(israeli)*
- Between half past eleven and half past three, a UN officer, Colonel Murray Stanaway, tried three times to arrange a ceasefire in the city. Jordanian accounts say their side accepted but wanted the Israelis to stop first. An Israeli diary has the Israeli commander, Major-General Uzi Narkiss, agreeing at noon. Then, it says, he told his superiors he wanted to "get in". *(disputed)*
- Shortly after noon, West Bank command ordered the seizure of Government House, the headquarters of the UN observers, on a ridge south of the Old City. Hazim Khalidi, a former British officer advising the Jordanians, urged waiting until evening and laying mines. Ata Ali refused. Between about one and half past one, two companies from the Sur Baher battalion took the compound. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- In East Jerusalem, hundreds of young men crowded police posts that afternoon asking for weapons. A civilian committee received some rifles and Bren guns, but almost none reached the Old City. *(arab: Palestinian)*

### 17. Government House retaken
*Map: Jerusalem · 5 June, afternoon and night*
- For Israel's commanders, Government House overlooked the road to the south of the city. Colonel Eliezer Amitai's Jerusalem Brigade, a brigade of reservists, was ordered to retake it. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts say the attack went in at about half past two with six Sherman tanks, and the ridge was retaken by mid-afternoon. A Jordanian account has the counter-attack at three, with a dozen tanks against Government House and another dozen against Sur Baher. It says two Israeli tanks were destroyed. *(disputed)*
- Israeli accounts add that the brigade went on to take Sur Baher by nightfall, cutting the road from Jerusalem to Hebron. *(israeli)*
- A Jordanian account reports heavy losses in the withdrawal from Government House. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- General Bull's UN staff were escorted into Israeli lines. *(established)*
- At the foot of Ammunition Hill, in the north of the city, reservists of the Jerusalem Brigade held a trench. Among them were lecturers from the Hebrew University. *(israeli)*
- That afternoon Colonel Mordechai "Motta" Gur's 55th Paratroop Brigade learned that its jump into Sinai was cancelled. It was sent to Jerusalem instead, and many of the paratroopers arrived that night in civilian buses. *(israeli)*
- Narkiss told Gur to link up with Mount Scopus: bear right toward the Rockefeller Museum, he said, and be ready to break into the Old City. *(israeli)*
- Israeli leaders had not yet agreed to order the capture of the Old City. In the Knesset shelter, under shelling, ministers Menachem Begin and Yigal Allon pressed for it. Moshe Dayan, the new Defence Minister, and the religious party ministers were against. Eshkol made no final decision. *(israeli)*
- At about five in the evening, Colonel Uri Ben-Ari's Harel Brigade attacked the ridges north-west of Jerusalem, toward Nebi Samuel and the Ramallah road. *(israeli)*
- After sunset, the Jordanian West Bank headquarters pulled back. Telephone lines out of Jerusalem were cut at six, and the Ramallah transmitter was knocked out at half past seven. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Between about nine and eleven at night, Israeli aircraft caught a Jordanian relief column on the Jericho road. It was made up of a tank regiment of the 60th Armoured Brigade and a battalion of Ata Ali's brigade. *(established)*
- A Jordanian account says that by eleven, Ata Ali was told the column could no longer relieve Jerusalem. *(arab: Jordanian)*

### 18. Ammunition Hill
*Map: Jerusalem · 6 June, about 02:00–06:30*
- Ammunition Hill and the Police School beside it guarded the road to Mount Scopus. Jordanian troops defended the Police School and the hill's trenches and bunkers. Major Mansour Kraishan commanded the battalion defending this sector. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about eleven at night, Israeli mortars and artillery opened on the northern line. Searchlights from Mount Scopus lit the ground. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Gur's paratroopers crossed near the Mandelbaum Gate after midnight. Lieutenant-Colonel Yossi Yaffe's battalion took the Ammunition Hill sector. The attack began between two and half past two; sources differ by half an hour. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts differ about the Police School. Some say it was nearly empty; others say a company fought there for over an hour. *(disputed)*
- From about half past two to half past six, the fighting went trench by trench, at close range, for about four hours. *(established)*
- An Israeli private, Yaki Haimovitch, aged twenty-one, took the lead when his commander was hit. With another private, Shalom David, he blew up a large bunker with a bag of explosives, after a bazooka had failed. Years later he called himself "just a soldier who did what they told me". *(israeli)*
- Another private, Eitan Neveh, left the trench to give covering fire and was killed. *(israeli)*
- Jordanian veterans later said their side fought hard to the end. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At a reunion decades later, Israeli and Jordanian veterans spoke of the determination of the men they had fought. *(established)*
- Both sides suffered heavy losses. Published death tolls differ, partly because they cover different parts of the fighting. *(established)*
- After the battle, Meir Ronnen, a forty-year-old Israeli reservist, buried seventeen Jordanian dead. He marked the grave with a rifle and a sign: "Here lie buried 17 brave Jordanian soldiers." *(israeli)*
- To the west, the Harel Brigade broke through the border defences near Ma'ale HaHamisha during the night, after hours of slow fighting through minefields. *(israeli)*

### 19. Around the walls
*Map: Jerusalem · 6 June, day and night*
- Israeli accounts say that at about three in the morning Ata Ali told the Governor of Jerusalem, Anwar al-Khatib, that the battle was lost. Then, they say, he left toward Jericho. Jordanian accounts say he called at dawn and moved into the Old City. *(disputed)*
- At first light, Israeli aircraft hit the remaining Jordanian guns. Seven tanks cleared the last strong point on the Nablus Road and moved toward the Damascus Gate. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At seven, Ata Ali told Khalidi he had about 500 men inside the walls. Shu'fat Hill, Augusta Victoria, Abu Tor and Ras al-Amud were still held. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Through the morning, Israeli troops took Sheikh Jarrah, the American Colony and the area of the Rockefeller Museum. By about midday they had reached the enclave on Mount Scopus, cut off since 1948. *(established)*
- Near the Rockefeller Museum, Israeli mortar fire fell on Israeli troops by mistake, killing nine paratroopers and several Jordanian prisoners. *(israeli)*
- Governor al-Khatib moved to the offices of the Muslim religious administration near the holy compound. They became the last civilian command post. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Inside the walls, medical staff struggled to treat the wounded. Nuns and a civil defence team under Musa Bitar cared for the wounded on the Via Dolorosa. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- Shu'fat Hill fell just after noon, after two assaults; Abu Tor fell too. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts say that at Tel el-Ful, north of the city, Israeli tanks set six Jordanian Patton tanks on fire, and others were abandoned. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts also describe air attacks on the Jordanian armour as it withdrew toward Jericho. *(israeli)*
- In Amman, the pressure on Hussein grew. One account says Egypt advised him at about ten to pull his army back across the Jordan. At midday General Riad passed on three choices: a ceasefire imposed from outside, leaving the West Bank that night, or one more day of fighting. In the afternoon Hussein learned of the UN's ceasefire call and decided to hold on. *(disputed)*
- In a call recorded and released by Israel, Nasser and Hussein discussed blaming American and British aircraft. Neither country's aircraft took part in Israel's attacks. *(established)*
- At sunset, paratroopers attacked Augusta Victoria. Their reconnaissance unit took a wrong turn under fire from the walls. It lost two tanks and some jeeps, and pulled back after about two hours. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At ten at night, a Jordanian brigade came up from Jericho to relieve the city. It was blocked by the wreckage of its own armour on the road. The fire died down after midnight. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- That day Dayan had forbidden his troops to enter the Old City. *(israeli)*

### 20. The West Bank falls
*Map: West Bank · 5–8 June, with a later refugee summary*
- Jordan had about 45,000 men on the West Bank, and Israel about 40,000 on this front; estimates differ. *(estimate)*
- Israel's air force, free after the first morning, ruled the roads. *(established)*
- On the night of the 5th, Israeli troops took the Latrun salient, on the road from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem. The garrison had already left for Jerusalem. Trappist monks at the Latrun monastery said the Jordanians left without firing a shot. *(established)*
- Two Egyptian commando battalions had also been sent to this area. Accounts of what happened to them differ widely. *(disputed)*
- Israeli forces expelled residents from the Latrun villages of Imwas, Yalu and Beit Nuba, forcing them out of their homes. Israeli forces also demolished the three villages. Other residents had already fled. *(established)*
- Many walked toward Ramallah in the heat. Accounts differ over how many people were driven out. *(estimate)*
- Israeli officials gave military and security reasons for destroying the villages. Soldiers and residents also described demolitions after the fighting. *(established)*
- In his diary, Brother Guy Khoury, a French monk at Latrun, recorded that about seventy elderly villagers, women and children sheltered at the monastery. Then an Israeli officer ordered them to leave for Ramallah. *(established)*
- In the north, Major-General Elad Peled's division crossed near Jenin at about five on the afternoon of the 5th. At dawn on the 6th, Israeli tanks pretended to retreat, drawing out Jordanian Patton tanks, and eight were knocked out. Jenin fell on the 6th. *(israeli)*
- A Jordanian tank gunner near Jenin, Corporal Hussein Saad al-Nuaimi, refused an order to withdraw. When told the rule did not apply, he asked, "and Jerusalem?" He was killed later that day. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Wassef Erekat, a young Palestinian artillery officer in the Jordanian army, later recalled that his battery pulled back on the morning of the 6th. He said Israeli tanks entered their camp after the cook opened the gate, believing they were Iraqis. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- An Iraqi brigade sent to help had been badly hit from the air before it arrived. *(israeli)*
- A Jordanian account describes severe losses to Jordan's armoured brigades under air attack. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- By one account, Colonel Zaid ibn Shaker's 60th Armoured Brigade lost about half of its eighty tanks. *(estimate)*
- Israeli figures say the 40th Armoured Brigade, Jordan's other tank brigade, fared even worse. Only eight of its Patton tanks got back across the Damiya Bridge. *(israeli)*
- By the evening of the 6th, Ramallah, Qalqilya and Jenin were in Israeli hands. *(established)*
- In Qalqilya, many buildings were demolished. After protests, the demolitions stopped and residents were allowed back. *(established)*
- Mahmoud Muali, the Palestinian radio man in the Jordanian army, later recalled that he was ordered to retreat. He recalled leaving his rifle at the base and walking for two days to his village. Back there, he burned photographs and evidence of his army service. "Everyone was shocked. Even me," he said. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- On the 7th, Israel's Jerusalem Brigade pushed south. Bethlehem fell after a brief fight; an Israeli account gives about forty Jordanian defenders killed. *(israeli)*
- Nablus also fell on the 7th. *(established)*
- Further south, Israeli forces occupied Hebron as Jordanian troops withdrew. *(established)*
- Hebron's Jordanian brigade, the Hittin Brigade under Bahjat al-Muhaisen, withdrew across the river. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- That evening Israeli troops reached Jericho and the river Jordan. *(established)*
- The bridges over the Jordan were blown. Accounts differ on who blew them and when. *(disputed)*
- Over the next three months, about 200,000 people crossed east over the river. Many of them were refugees from 1948 for a second time. *(estimate)*
- Some fled from fear and the bombing. Others were pushed out by loudspeakers or put on buses. How many fled and how many were pushed out is still argued. *(disputed)*
- Jordan's losses are given as about 700 killed or missing. The figure of 6,000 sometimes quoted was an early estimate of killed and wounded together. *(estimate)*

### 21. The Old City
*Map: Jerusalem · 7 June, about 00:30–22:00*
- At half past midnight, Ata Ali told Governor Anwar al-Khatib that nearly all his officers had gone and he could not hold. The Governor refused to leave. He called Jerusalem his adopted city. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At about one in the morning, the soldiers quietly gathered at the Dung Gate and left, about 500 of them. At half past two, police, volunteers and soldiers handed in their weapons at the religious administration's offices. Before dawn, a Jordanian sergeant and two Palestinian soldiers came back and dug in near the Golden Gate. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Israeli accounts say that at about seven, hearing that a UN ceasefire was near, Dayan ordered the Old City taken. Some Israeli accounts say the order reached Narkiss at five in the morning, by telephone from the deputy chief of staff. Whether the cabinet approved first is also disputed. *(disputed)*
- Dayan's orders said every holy building was to be protected. *(israeli)*
- In Amman, Prime Minister Saad Juma'a had asked the US ambassador and the UN to stop Israel taking the Old City. He warned that the monarchy itself could fall. *(established)*
- At sunrise, Israeli aircraft bombed Augusta Victoria. In the religious administration's offices, people cheered, thinking they were Iraqi planes. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- At half past eight, Gur's paratroopers attacked on three lines. By half past nine they had linked up on the ridge and turned toward the walls. *(israeli)*
- At about a quarter to ten, Israeli troops entered through the Lions' Gate, also called St Stephen's Gate, under covering fire. Gur's half-track went in. One Jordanian soldier fired a Bren gun from the minaret above the gate. *(israeli)*
- The paratroopers went first onto the great platform that Jews call the Temple Mount and Muslims call al-Haram al-Sharif, the Noble Sanctuary. On it stand the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque. At about ten, Gur spoke on the radio, in his own words: "The Temple Mount is in our hands." Then he ordered all firing to stop. *(israeli)*
- A little later the paratroopers came down to the Western Wall. It is a surviving retaining wall of the ancient Temple platform, and a deeply sacred Jewish place of prayer. Narkiss asked where it was. The army's Chief Rabbi, Shlomo Goren, said prayers and blew the shofar, the ram's horn. *(established)*
- Looking back in a 2017 interview, one paratrooper, Yitzhak Yifat, said: "We realised that we had conquered another people." *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers tied an Israeli flag beside the Wall, and a flag was also raised on the Dome of the Rock. Dayan ordered that flag taken down. In the afternoon Dayan, Rabin and Narkiss walked through the Lions' Gate to the Wall. *(established)*
- Dayan left the running of the Haram al-Sharif with the Islamic Waqf, the Muslim religious administration. Israel retained overall security control, while Muslim authorities administered the holy compound. *(established)*
- When Gur asked for a written statement that the holy sites were undamaged, Sheikh Sa'id Sabri refused until Muslims could inspect them. *(israeli)*
- Paratroopers took the Governor, the Muslim judge and Hazim Khalidi prisoner in the religious administration's offices. The city was surrendered that day. *(established)*
- On the night of the 10th and 11th of June, Israeli authorities began demolishing the Mughrabi Quarter beside the Wall. Its story is told at the end. *(established)*
- The battle for Jerusalem cost Israel about 180 soldiers and about 20 civilians killed. One count gives about 330 Jordanian soldiers and about 100 civilians killed in East Jerusalem. *(estimate)*
- The UN ceasefire with Jordan was set to begin at ten at night, Israel time. The West Bank and East Jerusalem were now held by Israel. *(established)*

## Part 5: The USS Liberty

### 22. USS Liberty
*Map: Region, inset off El Arish · 8 June, about two in the afternoon*
- On the 8th of June an American ship was sailing slowly in international waters off northern Sinai. She was the USS *Liberty*, a US Navy technical research ship. Her job was to gather signals for the National Security Agency, the American agency that listens in on other countries' communications. *(established)*
- In the night, the Pentagon, America's defence headquarters, had ordered her to stay at least one hundred nautical miles from the coasts of the countries at war. That is about a hundred and eighty-five kilometres. The order was misrouted, held up and sent to the wrong stations. It reached the ship only after the attack. *(established)*
- That morning Israeli aircraft flew over the *Liberty* many times. Israeli accounts say that at three minutes past six an observer reported a US Navy supply ship. Later a naval officer matched her hull letters, GTR-5, in a reference book. At eleven o'clock the marker for the ship was taken off the map of ships at sea in the naval headquarters. *(israeli)*
- Around midday there were reports that El Arish was being shelled from the sea. Three Israeli torpedo boats were sent toward El Arish. Israeli radar operators reported a ship with a speed of about thirty knots, or nautical miles an hour. Israel's navy asked for aircraft. *(israeli)*
- The *Liberty* was actually making about five knots: one sixth of that speed. *(established)*
- At about two in the afternoon, Israeli Mirage fighter jets attacked the ship with cannon fire. Super Mystère jets followed with napalm, an incendiary fuel that burns intensely. *(established)*
- The ship's captain, Commander William McGonagle, was wounded but stayed on the bridge, where the ship is commanded. *(established)*
- The ship flew an American flag. During the attack, a larger one replaced a flag that had been damaged. *(established)*
- Accounts disagree about what the attacking crews could see and identify. *(disputed)*
- Israeli accounts describe growing doubts about the ship's identity after the air attack. The torpedo attack nevertheless went ahead. *(israeli)*
- Israeli accounts say a torpedo boat asked the ship to identify itself and received what it took as an evasive reply. Clark Clifford's later report for the White House rejected the claim that the *Liberty* sent that reply. *(disputed)*
- Soon afterwards a torpedo struck the ship's side, killing twenty-five men below decks. The *Liberty* leaned heavily to one side and lost power, but she did not sink. *(established)*
- The boats then offered help, which the crew turned down. Israel told the United States what had happened later that afternoon. *(established)*
- Accounts give different times for that message, half past three or four o'clock. *(disputed)*
- Thirty-four Americans were killed: officers, sailors, two marines and one civilian. *(established)*
- More than one hundred and seventy were wounded. *(estimate)*
- Israel said it had mistaken the ship for an Egyptian transport ship, the *El Quseir*. Israeli inquiries in 1967, and the army's history department in 1982, called it an innocent error. They said it was made amid false reports and lost information. *(israeli)*
- The US Navy's inquiry, led by Rear Admiral Isaac Kidd, investigated the circumstances of the attack and how the ship responded. - In July 1967 Clark Clifford reported on the attack for the White House. He found no evidence then available of a planned attack on a ship known to be American. He held Israel responsible for gross negligence, meaning very serious carelessness, and called it "flagrant". *(established)*

- Israel later paid compensation to the families, to the wounded, and to the US government for the ship. *(established)*
- Paying compensation did not settle the question of intent. Whether the attack was a mistake or deliberate is still disputed, and this page does not decide it. *(disputed)*

## Part 6: The Golan Heights

### 23. The decision on Syria
*Map: Golan · 8–9 June, night to morning*
- The Golan Heights rise steeply above the Hula and Jordan valleys and the Sea of Galilee. They were Syrian territory. Syria had fortified them with bunkers, trenches and minefields up to fifteen kilometres deep. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts say that for about nineteen years, Syrian guns on the heights had fired down on the villages below. Among them were kibbutzim, Israeli communal farming settlements. Residents of Gadot recalled children repeatedly taking shelter during shelling. Residents said they could smell the coffee the Syrian soldiers brewed above them. *(israeli)*
- Who caused that border fighting is argued. Syria and some historians blame Israeli farming in the demilitarised zones, strips along the border where the armistice restricted military forces. In interviews published after his death, Moshe Dayan said most of the clashes began when Israel sent tractors into those zones. *(disputed)*
- When the war began on the 5th of June, Syria shelled Israeli settlements and its jets attacked Galilee. *(established)*
- Israeli attacks severely damaged Syria's air force. Sources disagree on the number of aircraft lost. *(estimate)*
- On the 6th, Syrian infantry attacked toward She'ar Yashuv and Kibbutz Dan and were driven back. In the first four days, Israeli figures say, Syrian fire killed two Israeli civilians and wounded sixteen. *(israeli)*
- Political purges had removed many experienced Syrian officers before the war. One history counts about two thousand replaced in 1966 alone. *(estimate; Dunstan p. 25)*
- Captain Muhammad Ammar, who served at Tel Faher, later described the mood before the war. He said the soldiers believed they were the stronger side and that the Golan could not be broken. They took heart from the unity of Syria, Egypt and Jordan. *(arab: Syrian)*
- In Israel, Major-General David Elazar, head of Northern Command, pressed for an attack on the heights. So did leaders of the kibbutzim below. *(israeli)*
- On the evening of the 8th, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan still said no. He feared that the Soviet Union would step in. *(israeli)*
- Early on the morning of the 9th, Syria announced that it accepted the UN ceasefire. *(established)*
- A few hours later Dayan changed his mind. He had read an intercepted message from Nasser urging Syria to stop fighting. He had also read an analyst's report that the Syrian army was collapsing. *(israeli)*
- At six or seven in the morning, the accounts differ, Dayan phoned Elazar himself. He went around the chief of staff, Yitzhak Rabin. Elazar recalled the question: could he attack? Then attack. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol was told afterwards. *(israeli)*
- In interviews published after his death, Dayan said the reason he had given was an excuse. He said Syria was no threat by the fourth day, and that the attack was a mistake. *(israeli)*
- Eshkol, Elazar and the kibbutz leaders said the attack was needed to end the shelling. Historians still debate the security arguments. *(disputed)*

### 24. The climb and Tel Faher
*Map: Golan · 9 June, about half past eleven in the morning to evening*
- All morning, for three or four hours, Israeli aircraft bombed the Syrian positions on the heights. *(israeli)*
- At about half past eleven, Israeli tanks began to climb from near Kibbutz Kfar Szold, in the north, where the Syrian line was thinnest. They were the 8th Armoured Brigade, a large tank force, under Colonel Albert Mendler. *(israeli)*
- Eight bulldozers went first, cutting a track for the tanks. They had no armour, and their drivers were army engineers. *(israeli)*
- The Syrian guns opened fire, at first mostly on the Israeli settlements below. Then the gunners found the column. Five of the eight bulldozers never reached the top. *(israeli)*
- The tanks climbed in single file. One tank battalion was drawn off its route toward Syrian fire. Its commander, Lieutenant-Colonel Arye Biro, was wounded. Major Rafi Mokady jumped into the lead tank to take over, and was killed near Sir a-Dib. *(israeli)*
- At dusk Lieutenant Natanel Horovitz entered the village of Qala with two other tanks. *(israeli)*
- Mendler later said of the day: "The Syrians fought well and bloodied us." *(israeli)*
- Further south, the Golani Infantry Brigade under Colonel Yona Efrat followed the same route about two hours behind. One of its battalions took Tel Azaziat, above Kibbutz Dan, by late afternoon. *(israeli)*
- Another Golani battalion, under Lieutenant-Colonel Moshe Klein, attacked Tel Faher. It was a hilltop fortress of trenches, barbed wire and bunkers. *(established)*
- A history drawn mainly from Israeli sources says part of the hill was held by a Syrian company, a small unit, under Captain Ahmad Khalili. *(israeli; Dunstan p. 83)*
- At the foot of the hill the Israeli half-tracks were set on fire. Half-tracks are armoured vehicles with wheels at the front and tracks at the back. *(israeli)*
- The survivors of the leading company fought their way into the trenches, and the fighting was hand to hand. Klein was killed. Private David Shirazi lay down across the barbed wire so that others could cross it. *(israeli)*
- The close-range battle ended that evening. Tel Faher fell at about six. *(israeli)*
- About thirty-one to thirty-six Israelis were killed there. About sixty-two Syrians were killed, and twenty were taken prisoner. *(estimate)*
- By night Israel held a strip about eight kilometres wide on the northern heights. *(israeli)*

### 25. Quneitra
*Map: Golan · 10 June, morning to half past six in the evening*
- Through the night UN observers logged heavy explosions and gunfire north of the Sea of Galilee. *(established)*
- Israeli forces also took Banias, in the far north. Accounts differ on which Israeli unit took it, and on whether it fell on the 9th or on the morning of the 10th. *(disputed)*
- On the morning of the 10th, Radio Damascus announced that Quneitra, the main town on the heights, had fallen. Israeli troops were still hours away. *(established)*
- About two hours later Syria broadcast a correction. *(established)*
- The information minister, Muhammad al-Zu'bi, later said the first statement was Communiqué Number 66, an official announcement. He said he had at first refused to read it. *(arab: Syrian)*
- Who ordered the early announcement is still argued. Some Syrians blame the defence minister, Hafez al-Assad. Others speak of panic or a plain error. *(disputed)*
- The too-early announcement added to the confusion. Syrian forces withdrew, and civilians also fled. *(disputed)*
- Abu Hussein, then a boy in Quneitra, later recalled that his father took the family away after hearing the broadcast. They thought they would be gone only a short time. *(arab: Syrian)*
- Yusif Tannous worked with a UN team in Quneitra. He later recalled staying until the last day, then walking to Damascus. He wept when he heard the town had fallen. *(arab: Syrian)*
- Israeli forces entered Quneitra that afternoon. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts describe entering a largely deserted town. *(israeli)*
- On the 10th of June, the Soviet prime minister, Alexei Kosygin, used the hotline, the direct link between Moscow and Washington. He warned President Lyndon Johnson that the Soviet Union might act independently, including militarily, unless the fighting stopped. *(established)*
- Johnson ordered the American Sixth Fleet to move east, as a signal. Separately, the Soviet Union broke off relations with Israel. *(established)*
- The UN's chief observer, General Odd Bull, met Dayan and arranged a ceasefire. It took effect at half past six in the evening, Israel time. *(established)*
- In the last hour, helicopters landed Israeli paratroopers at Butmiya. An Israeli brigade took Mas'ade, on the slopes of Mount Hermon. *(israeli)*
- Syria told the UN that Israel kept moving after the ceasefire. It said Israeli forces reached Rafid more than three hours late, and Mount Hermon. Israel said it did not advance beyond the ceasefire line. The UN's chief observer reported Israeli units still advancing on the 10th. *(disputed)*
- A history drawn mainly from Israeli sources dates Israel's helicopter landing on Mount Hermon to the 12th of June, two days after the ceasefire. *(israeli; Dunstan pp. 17, 88)*
- Between about one hundred and fifteen and one hundred and forty Israelis were killed on the Syrian front. *(estimate)*
- Syrian military deaths are uncertain, and published estimates differ widely. One Western history gives about five hundred Syrians killed and two and a half thousand wounded. *(estimate)*
- In August 1967 Israel held a census in the part of the Golan it now occupied. It found about six thousand people still living there, most of them Druze. *(estimate)*
- Many residents fled during the fighting. Red Cross delegates later witnessed deportations, people being forcibly removed, from the occupied Golan. Many villages on the Golan were destroyed afterwards. *(established)*
- The proportions who fled during the fighting and those subsequently forced out remain disputed. Israeli officials attributed the main wartime exodus to flight ahead of the fighting. *(disputed)*

## Part 7: After the six days

### 26. After the six days
*Map: Region · 9 June to 22 November 1967*
- On the evening of the 9th of June, Gamal Abdel Nasser spoke to Egypt on television. He took responsibility for what he called *al-Naksa*, "the setback", and said he would resign. *(established)*
- Crowds filled the streets of Cairo at once. *(established)*
- A student, Sabry Mahmoud, later recalled marching to Nasser's house. He said the protests went on for two days. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- How far the protests were organised, and how far they were spontaneous, is still argued. *(disputed)*
- On the 10th Nasser withdrew his resignation. *(established)*
- When the guns fell silent, Israel held the Sinai Peninsula to the Suez Canal, except Port Fuad. It also held the Gaza Strip, the West Bank including East Jerusalem, and the western Golan Heights. *(established)*
- The area under Israeli control was now roughly four times its pre-war size. After the displacement, roughly a million people remained in the occupied areas, under Israeli military control. *(estimate)*
- This was military control. It did not mean that the world recognised these lands as part of Israel. *(established)*
- On the 11th the UN Security Council condemned the violations of the ceasefire. It called on troops that had moved forward after it to go back. The last UN peacekeepers left Egypt on the 17th. *(established)*
- On the night of the 10th and 11th of June, Israeli authorities began demolishing the Mughrabi, or Moroccan, Quarter beside the Western Wall. Residents were forced to leave so that a large prayer plaza could be created. *(established)*
- Records list roughly one hundred and thirty-five houses destroyed. *(estimate)*
- About six hundred and fifty people are thought to have lived there. They were given only hours to leave. *(estimate)*
- The exact number of residents and deaths is uncertain. Some accounts report an elderly woman killed during the demolition. *(disputed)*
- Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were displaced during and after the war. Estimates vary with the date. They also vary with whether they count only people who crossed borders, or also those displaced inside the occupied areas. *(estimate)*
- Most of those who left the West Bank crossed the Jordan River to the east. For many, it was the second time they had been displaced, after the war of 1948. *(estimate)*
- Many fled the fighting and the air attacks. Some were driven out, and villages near Latrun were destroyed. *(established)*
- Under a limited return scheme, about fourteen thousand people returned to the West Bank by the end of August. *(estimate)*
- Around one hundred thousand Syrians left the occupied Golan or were forced out; the total varies by source and period. *(estimate)*
- The Druze villages, among them Majdal Shams, remained. *(established)*
- At Khartoum, from late August to the 1st of September, Arab leaders met at a summit. They declared what became known as the "three noes": no peace with Israel, no recognition, no negotiation. *(established)*
- Some historians read it as defiance. Others say Arab leaders saw it as leaving room for indirect deals. *(disputed)*
- On the 22nd of November, the UN Security Council passed Resolution 242 unanimously. *(established)*
- Resolution 242 called for Israeli withdrawal from territories occupied in the war, and an end to the state of war. It called for secure and recognised boundaries for every state, and a just settlement of the refugee problem. It also rejected acquiring territory through war. *(established)*
- Governments disagreed over how much withdrawal the resolution required, and over how final borders would be agreed. The argument has never been settled. *(disputed)*

## Debrief

### Losses (most are estimates)
- Israel: Israel's Defence Ministry archive records seven hundred and seventy-six military deaths between the 5th and the 14th of June. It records two thousand five hundred and ninety-three soldiers injured in the fighting. *(israeli; Defence Ministry archive)*
- Other publications use different totals for Israel's losses. *(established)*
- Egypt: estimates of Egyptian military deaths run into the thousands. Sources disagree substantially. The number killed must be kept separate from the missing, the wounded and the captured. *(estimate)*
- More than four thousand Egyptian servicemen were taken prisoner. *(estimate)*
- Some soldiers died from thirst and exhaustion during the retreat. *(established)*
- Jordan: about seven hundred killed or missing, by the count of the Jordanian historian Samir Mutawi. *(estimate; Jordanian historian)*
- An early estimate of about six thousand counted the killed and the wounded together, so it is not a death toll. It was later revised to about seven hundred dead and two and a half thousand wounded. *(estimate; Dunstan p. 64)*
- Syria: Syrian military deaths are uncertain, and published estimates differ widely. *(estimate)*
- Civilians: the total number of Palestinian civilians killed remains uncertain. *(established)*
- The UN: the UN's confirmed list records fifteen peacekeepers killed: fourteen Indians and one Brazilian. *(established)*
- At sea: thirty-four Americans died on the *Liberty*. *(established)*

### What changed
- The total area under Israeli control was roughly four times its pre-war size. *(estimate)*
- At the end of June, Israel extended its law and administration to an enlarged East Jerusalem. The UN rejected the changes to the city's status. *(established)*
- Roughly a million Palestinians and Syrians remained in the occupied areas under Israeli control. *(estimate)*
- Israel began establishing settlements, Israeli communities, in the occupied territories. The UN Security Council has stated that Israeli settlements in the occupied Palestinian territory have no legal validity. Israel disputes that position. *(established)*
- The fighting did not end. A war of attrition, a long struggle to wear the other side down, went on along the Suez Canal until 1970. In October 1973 Egypt and Syria attacked to win back the land they had lost. *(established)*
- Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty in 1979. Israel withdrew from Sinai in 1982, apart from the disputed Taba border area, which was returned to Egypt in 1989. Jordan made peace with Israel in 1994. *(established)*
- In 1981 Israel extended its law to the Golan Heights. The UN Security Council rejected this in Resolution 497. Most countries and the UN consider the Golan occupied Syrian territory. *(established)*
- Resolution 242 became the starting point for most peace plans that followed. Its idea was later summed up as "land for peace". *(established)*
- In the Arab world the war is often remembered as *al-Naksa*, the setback. Many Israelis remember relief and a dramatic military victory. Israel marks the taking of East Jerusalem each year as Jerusalem Day. *(established)*

### Still argued about
- Who struck first is not in question: Israel launched the opening air strikes on the 5th of June. *(established)*
- Historians debate the leaders' intentions, how immediate the danger was, and whether war could have been avoided. *(disputed)*
- The *Liberty*: Israel said the attack was a mistake. Several American official assessments supported that explanation; many survivors and other critics challenged it. *(disputed)*
- The refugees: flight, expulsions and the destruction of villages are all documented. *(established)*
- The exact numbers and circumstances of displacement remain disputed. *(disputed)*
- How many fled, how many were expelled, and when, varied from place to place. *(established)*
- The Golan: historians debate the security arguments and the decision to capture the Golan. Dayan's later criticism is one important account, not a final answer. *(disputed)*
- Who ordered the early announcement of Quneitra's fall is also still argued. *(disputed)*
- The numbers: the dead on most sides, the aircraft destroyed, and the people displaced are still uncertain. *(estimate)*
