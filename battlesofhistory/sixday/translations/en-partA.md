## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Six-Day War, fought from the 5th to the 10th of June 1967. Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria, with Iraqi forces also taking part. We begin with the tensions before the war, then follow a false Soviet warning and the crisis of May 1967.

## Introduction

### 1. THE SIX-DAY WAR
- In June 1967 Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria on three fronts at once. It began on the morning of the 5th of June with an Israeli air strike on Egypt's airfields. It ended on the evening of the 10th, with a ceasefire on the Golan Heights. *(established)*
- When it was over, Israel had captured territory more than three times the size of pre-war Israel. Hundreds of thousands of people fled or were driven from their homes. Roughly a million people remained in the occupied areas, under Israeli military control. *(estimate)*
- It became widely known as the Six-Day War. In Arabic it is often remembered as an-Naksa, meaning "the setback". *(established)*

### 2. The land and its peoples
- The war of 1948 ended in 1949 with agreements to stop the fighting, called armistices. Israel signed them with Egypt, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon. These agreements stopped the fighting but did not make peace. They generally did not settle final borders; the line with Lebanon followed an existing international boundary. *(established)*
- Egypt ran the Gaza Strip. Jordan held the West Bank, and East Jerusalem with its Old City. Israel held West Jerusalem. Mount Scopus stayed an Israeli enclave: a small area surrounded by territory held by the other side. Syria held the Golan Heights. Below them were demilitarised zones, where the armistice restricted military forces. *(established)*
- During the 1948 war, about seven hundred thousand Palestinians fled or were expelled from their homes. *(estimate)*
- Palestinians call it the Nakba, "the catastrophe". Many still lived in refugee camps in 1967. In this war, many would be uprooted a second time. *(established)*

### 3. Suez, 1956
- In 1956 Israel invaded Sinai in coordination with Britain and France. Under pressure from the United States and the Soviet Union, it withdrew in 1957. *(established)*
- A United Nations peacekeeping force, called UNEF, began deploying in late 1956. As the invading forces withdrew, it moved into posts in Gaza and Sinai, on the Egyptian side of the line only. One of its posts was at Sharm el-Sheikh, above the Straits of Tiran: Israel's sea road to its port of Eilat. *(established)*
- Israel warned that closing the straits again would be grounds for military action. *(established)*

### 4. The great powers
- This was during the Cold War, the rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union armed Egypt and Syria with MiG jets and Soviet tanks, and gave them political backing. *(established)*
- Israel's air force flew mostly French jets, such as the Mirage. The United States had close ties with both Israel and Jordan. Jordan's army used American Patton tanks; its air force flew British Hunter jets. *(established)*

### 5. Water and the border, 1964 to 1967
- Israel's National Water Carrier drew water from the Sea of Galilee. In January 1964 the Arab League decided to divert two of the Jordan River's sources, the Hasbani and the Banias, away from it. Syria began digging in 1965. That year, Israeli tanks and artillery fired on the works. *(established)*
- In the demilitarised zones below the Golan, disputes over cultivation and exchanges of fire repeatedly led to clashes. *(established)*
- In interviews published after his death, Moshe Dayan said Israel often sent tractors in knowing the Syrians would shoot. *(israeli)*
- From 1965 Fatah, a small Palestinian group backed by Syria, raided Israel. *(established)*
- Then a mine killed three men in an Israeli army patrol. After that, on the 13th of November 1966, a large Israeli force attacked the West Bank village of as-Samu. It destroyed many houses and fought Jordanian troops sent toward the village. The UN Security Council censured Israel, meaning it formally condemned the raid. *(established)*
- On the 7th of April 1967 a fight in the zone near the Sea of Galilee grew into an air battle that reached Damascus. *(established)*
- Israel reported shooting down six Syrian MiGs. Syria also claimed an Israeli aircraft; Israel denied losing one. *(disputed)*

### 6. The armies
- With its reserves, part-time soldiers called back into service, Israel had about two hundred and sixty-four thousand men and about eight hundred tanks. *(estimate)*
- Egypt sent about a hundred thousand men and nine hundred to nine hundred and fifty tanks into Sinai. Egypt's air force had about four hundred and twenty combat aircraft nationwide. At the same time, part of its army was still fighting a war in Yemen. *(estimate)*
- Jordan's army numbered about fifty-five thousand men. An estimated forty-five thousand were deployed on the West Bank. The tank totals vary by source and area counted. *(estimate)*
- Syria had perhaps fifty to seventy-five thousand men and about a hundred and twenty combat aircraft. Iraq prepared forces to send into Jordan. *(estimate)*
- The Arab states together had larger armed forces, but not all their troops could fight on the same front. Training, air power, supply and command mattered as well as numbers. *(established)*
- Armies are organised in units of different sizes. A division is a large formation made up of several brigades. A brigade is made up of several battalions. *(established; editorial definition of terms)*

### 7. How this page tells it
- This page draws on Israeli, Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian and Palestinian sources, and on American, Soviet and United Nations papers. *(established)*
- Each line carries a tag. "Established" means the evidence strongly supports a statement. A side's name identifies an account from that side. "Disputed" means identifiable sources disagree. "Estimate" marks an uncertain number. "Reconstruction" marks a scene pieced together from incomplete evidence. *(established; editorial definition, not a historical claim)*
- Clock times are Israel time unless we say otherwise. Where no source gives a time, we say morning, afternoon, evening or night. *(established; editorial convention)*

## Part 1: The crisis

### 1. The false warning
*Map: Region · 13 to 15 May 1967*
- On the 13th of May 1967 a Soviet intelligence report reached Egypt. It said Israel was massing about eleven brigades of troops on the Syrian border. *(established)*
- The report was false. The reported Israeli troop concentration was not found. *(established)*
- Accounts describe several channels through which the Soviet warning reached Egypt. *(established)*
- Why Moscow sent the report is still argued. Historians disagree about Soviet motives: to deter Israel, support Syria, or increase pressure through a crisis. *(disputed)*
- On the 14th of May Egypt put its forces on alert. Columns of troops and tanks paraded through Cairo on their way to Sinai, in full view. *(established)*
- The same day President Nasser sent his army Chief of Staff, General Mohamed Fawzi, to Damascus. Fawzi later wrote that he found no Israeli build-up at all. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Yet the Egyptian troops kept moving into Sinai. *(established)*
- In Israel, the army's Chief of Staff, Lieutenant-General Yitzhak Rabin, asked to call up the reserves. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol was also Defence Minister. He refused, and allowed only a higher state of alert. *(israeli)*
- On the 15th of May, at a song festival in West Jerusalem, Shuli Natan sang Naomi Shemer's new song, "Jerusalem of Gold", for the first time. It longs for the Old City, then in Jordanian hands. It became an unofficial song of the war for many Israelis. *(established)*
- For the Palestinian families who lived in the Old City, it was home. Haifa Khalidi was nineteen in 1967. She later recalled that she had been born in a house beside the Mughrabi, or Moroccan, Quarter, next to the Western Wall. *(arab: Palestinian)*

### 2. The peacekeepers go
*Map: Region · 16 to 19 May 1967*
- At about ten at night on the 16th of May, in Gaza, the commander of the UN force was handed a letter from General Fawzi. The commander was an Indian officer, Major-General Indar Jit Rikhye. *(established)*
- The letter said Egyptian troops were already gathered in Sinai. It asked the UN to withdraw its soldiers from their observation posts along the border. Rikhye answered that he could not move without orders from the Secretary-General, the head of the United Nations. *(established)*
- In New York, Secretary-General U Thant required a formal request from Egypt's government. He treated the request as a decision about UNEF's continued presence, not just a few border posts. *(established)*
- On the 18th of May Egypt's Foreign Minister, Mahmoud Riad, formally withdrew Egypt's consent for the force to stay. Egyptian troops were already blocking UN soldiers from their posts. U Thant agreed the same day. *(established)*
- The force stood on Egyptian soil only with Egypt's consent. U Thant asked whether Israel would take UNEF on its side of the line. Israel's UN ambassador, Gideon Rafael, said that was "entirely unacceptable". *(established)*
- U Thant said the force could not remain without Egypt's consent. Critics, then and since, disputed how quickly he acted. Some say he should have taken it to the General Assembly, the meeting of all UN members, or played for time. *(disputed)*
- U Thant ordered withdrawal to begin on the 19th of May. *(established)*
- UNEF did not vanish overnight. The Canadians were flown out by the 31st of May. Indian, Brazilian and Swedish soldiers were still in the area when war came. *(established)*

### 3. The straits close
*Map: Region · 22 to 23 May 1967*
- As UN troops withdrew from Sharm el-Sheikh, Egyptian troops took over its positions above the Straits of Tiran. *(established)*
- On the 22nd of May Rabin told the Israeli cabinet that the Egyptian forces in Sinai were deployed for defence, not for attack. *(israeli)*
- That night Nasser spoke to pilots at the Bir Gifgafa air base in Sinai. He announced that from the next day the Gulf of Aqaba was closed to Israeli ships. It was also closed to any ship carrying strategic goods to Israel: supplies important to the military, including oil. *(established)*
- Nasser said: "Under no circumstances can we permit the Israeli flag to pass through the Gulf of Aqaba." The wording varies with the translation. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Eilat was Israel's only Red Sea port, and the way in for its oil from Iran. *(established)*
- Israel treated the closure as grounds for war, as it had warned in 1957. *(established)*
- The American President, Lyndon Johnson, later said the closure was the step that, more than any other, made war likely. *(established)*
- Anwar Sadat, the Speaker of Egypt's National Assembly, later wrote that he had backed the closure. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 23rd of May Johnson publicly rejected Egypt's claim to control shipping in the Gulf. *(established)*
- Over the following days, American and British officials discussed an international naval effort to reopen the straits. *(established)*

### 4. Waiting
*Map: Region · 23 May to 3 June 1967*
- Israelis call the next twelve days the hamtana, "the waiting". The army stood mobilised, called up and ready for war. The mobilisation disrupted work and put the economy under growing strain. The cabinet chose to give diplomacy time. *(established)*
- On the 23rd of May the former Prime Minister, David Ben-Gurion, criticised Rabin for the mobilisation. Soon after, Rabin collapsed from strain and was given drugs to make him sleep. He later said he slept for about a day. *(israeli)*
- Accounts differ on the date and on how long he was out of action. *(disputed)*
- Foreign Minister Abba Eban flew west. In Paris, on the 24th of May, President de Gaulle warned Israel not to fire first. In London, Prime Minister Harold Wilson backed free passage through the straits. *(israeli)*
- The UN Security Council met from the 24th of May, but failed to agree on measures that resolved the crisis. U Thant went to Cairo, without result. *(established)*
- In Washington on the 25th and 26th of May, Eban warned of an imminent attack, one about to happen. American intelligence disagreed. Israel agreed to wait up to two weeks while Washington tried other ways. *(established)*
- In speeches in late May, Nasser threatened that if war came, its aim would be to destroy Israel. He also said Egypt would not fire first. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Historians still disagree on whether Nasser meant to fight, or hoped to win without war. *(disputed)*
- On the evening of the 26th of May in Washington, Johnson met Eban. In Israel it was already the small hours of the 27th. Johnson said his advisers saw no attack coming, and that Israel would win if attacked. "Israel will not be alone," he said, "unless it decides to go it alone." *(established)*
- Accounts describe an Egyptian first-strike plan called "Dawn". They disagree over whether Nasser approved it and how close it came to being carried out. *(disputed)*
- On the 27th or 28th of May the Israeli cabinet split nine to nine on going to war at once. Eshkol sided with Eban, and diplomacy got a few more days. *(israeli)*
- On the 28th of May Eshkol spoke live on the radio. He read from a text corrected by hand, and stumbled over the words. Many Israelis heard it as weakness. *(established)*
- David Bahat, a seventeen-year-old paratrooper, later recalled waiting with his unit in the Negev desert for three weeks. *(israeli)*
- Suraya Mahmoud was eight years old and living in Cairo. She later recalled hearing the children in her street chanting that the army would march into Tel Aviv. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 5. Hussein in Cairo
*Map: Region · 30 May to 4 June 1967*
- On the 30th of May King Hussein of Jordan flew to Cairo, unannounced, with his prime minister and his army and air force chiefs. Nasser had lately called him an "imperialist lackey", a servant of the Western powers. Now Nasser said their differences had vanished "in one moment". *(established)*
- They signed a defence pact on live radio. An attack on one would count as an attack on both. In war, Jordan's forces would come under Egyptian command. An Egyptian general, Abdul Munim Riad, was sent to Amman to lead the Jordanian front. *(established)*
- Israeli accounts saw this as the moment the ring around Israel closed. *(israeli)*
- Jordanian accounts describe a king pulled into a war he neither wanted nor expected. Hussein later said he feared that staying out would tear Jordan apart. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- On the 31st of May Iraqi troops and tanks began moving into Jordan. The same day Hussein asked the United States to stay neutral. *(established)*
- On the 31st of May and the 1st of June, Meir Amit, head of Israel's intelligence service, the Mossad, was in Washington. He told Defense Secretary Robert McNamara that Israel could not stay mobilised for long. The American record does not establish an explicit American authorisation for Israel to attack. *(established)*
- Accounts differ over whether Amit understood Washington's position as permission to act. *(disputed)*
- On the 1st of June Eshkol gave up the Defence Ministry to Moshe Dayan. The opposition leader, Menachem Begin, joined a government of national unity. *(established)*
- On the 2nd of June Israel's generals pressed for war; most ministers still hesitated. *(israeli)*
- General Fawzi later wrote that on the same day, Nasser told his generals to expect an Israeli attack on the 4th or 5th of June. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- On the 3rd of June a letter from Johnson asked again that Israel not start the fighting. At the General Staff, the army's high command, Rabin warned that day that "a military and diplomatic noose" was tightening. He said delay could let Jordan, Iraq and Syria join. *(israeli)*
- The same day two Egyptian commando battalions were flown to Amman. *(established)*
- Mahmoud Muali, a Palestinian serving in Jordanian army radio communications, later recalled expecting war by the 2nd of June. *(arab: Palestinian)*
- On Sunday the 4th of June the Israeli cabinet met. The head of military intelligence, Aharon Yariv, reported that Jordan's army was under Egyptian command. He also reported Iraqi units entering Jordan. Even the cautious minister Haim-Moshe Shapira accepted that time had run out. Eshkol reminded them that "the Arabs will remain here", whatever the war's outcome. *(israeli)*
- That afternoon the cabinet voted for war. The Israeli Defence Ministry archive's account records eighteen ministers voting for military action. Eshkol and Dayan were left to fix the hour. That evening Rabin set it for the next morning. *(israeli)*
- Iraq also committed forces to the Arab coalition. *(established)*
- That day in Cairo, the American journalist James Reston wrote that Egypt did not want war. Egypt's Vice-President was due in Washington for talks on the 7th of June. *(established)*

## Part 2: The air strike

### 6. Operation Focus
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, morning to midday*
- On Monday the 5th of June, at a quarter to eight Israel time, Israeli jets struck about ten Egyptian airfields at once. In Cairo, an hour ahead, it was a quarter to nine. *(established)*
- Israel called it Operation Focus, in Hebrew *Moked*. The plan is credited to Ezer Weizman, the former air force commander. It was carried out by his successor, Mordechai Hod, known as Motti. *(israeli)*
- By Israeli accounts, the jets flew in groups of four. They went west over the sea, then turned south. They flew about twenty metres above the waves, in radio silence. Hod later called the wait "the longest 45 minutes of my life." *(israeli)*
- In Egypt, that same morning, Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer was flying from Almaza, near Cairo, to Bir Thamada in Sinai. With him was the air force commander, Sidki Mahmoud. *(established)*
- Egyptian accounts describe restrictions on anti-aircraft fire during Amer's inspection flight. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw large formations of aircraft, and sent a warning to Cairo in code. Egypt's foreign minister, Mahmoud Riad, later wrote that the secret code had just been changed. The message was not read in time. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- Over each base the jets climbed sharply. They hit the runways first, then the aircraft parked in the open. *(established)*
- At El Arish, the Egyptian fighter pilot Fikry el-Gahramy later recalled: "There was no warning at all." *(arab: Egyptian)*
- At Kabrit, Flight Lieutenant Mustafa Hafez, aged twenty-six, was in the dining room when the bombs fell. He took off in whatever fighter was ready. Then he found his guns would not fire, because of a switch in the cockpit. "I was not actually due to fly that morning," he later said. *(arab: Egyptian)*
- A second wave attacked Egyptian airfields again, including additional targets. Egyptian bombers that had been moved south to Luxor for safety were attacked there at midday. *(israeli)*
- The aircraft totals vary. Sources count aircraft lost in the air, aircraft destroyed on the ground, and aircraft written off later in different ways. *(established)*
- Israeli-based totals for Egypt's losses that day run from about two hundred and ninety to three hundred and forty aircraft. An Arab chronology gives two hundred and nine. *(estimate; Israeli-derived totals; Al Jazeera Arabic, "Diary of the 1967 war")*
- On that first day, Israel lost about seventeen to nineteen aircraft of its own, most of them to anti-aircraft fire. *(estimate)*
- In Cairo, Nasser's former deputy, Abdul Latif al-Baghdadi, later wrote about that evening. Amer, he wrote, first claimed seventy-three Israeli planes shot down. Then Amer showed a list: forty-seven Egyptian aircraft left, thirty-five of them able to fly. Meanwhile, Radio Cairo was still announcing victories. *(arab: Egyptian)*

### 7. Three air forces
*Map: Air strike · 5 June, 11:50–evening; 6 and 7 June*
- Jordanian accounts say Jordan's radar had read the heavy air traffic as Egyptian aircraft. *(arab: Jordanian)*
- Cairo told Amman that Egypt's air force was striking Israel. Jordan, Syria and Iraq sent their own aircraft in. *(established)*
- At about ten to twelve, Jordanian Hawker Hunter jets from Mafraq attacked near Netanya. One account says they were aiming at an air base, missed, and hit a factory, killing one civilian and wounding seven. Whether three or four Hunters flew is disputed. *(disputed)*
- Syrian jets struck northern Israel. An Arab chronology names the Haifa oil refineries and Megiddo airfield as targets. Israeli accounts say three Syrian jets were shot down. *(disputed; Al Jazeera Arabic, "Diary of the 1967 war")*
- From about a quarter to one, Israel's third wave hit Mafraq and then Amman. By Israeli accounts, Jordan's air bases and most of the aircraft there had been put out of action that afternoon. *(israeli)*
- Sources differ on Jordan's aircraft losses, partly because they count different aircraft and locations. *(disputed)*
- Between two and four in the afternoon, Israeli jets struck Syrian bases around Damascus and beyond. Israel claimed about two-thirds of Syria's air force, fifty to sixty-one aircraft. Other accounts say Syria had spread its aircraft to distant bases, and lost far fewer. *(disputed)*
- At about three, four Israeli Vautour bombers struck the Iraqi base called H-3, in the western desert. Israel claimed about ten aircraft destroyed there. A single Lebanese Hunter was also shot down. *(israeli)*
- That night the chief of staff, Yitzhak Rabin, announced on the radio that four hundred enemy planes had been destroyed. Earlier, Defence Minister Moshe Dayan had asked the press not to publish the number. *(israeli)*
- David Bahat, the young Israeli paratrooper, later recalled hearing an Egyptian broadcast claiming that Tel Aviv had fallen. *(israeli)*
- The next morning, the 6th of June, an Iraqi bomber dropped bombs on a main street of Netanya. *(established; IDF timeline)*
- Anti-aircraft guns shot the bomber down near Afula. It fell on an army camp, killing its crew and about a dozen Israeli reservists. *(estimate)*
- On the 7th of June, Iraqi accounts credit Iraqi, Jordanian and Pakistani pilots flying Hunters from H-3 with shooting down two Vautours and a Mirage. *(arab: Iraqi)*
- Over the whole war, Israel claimed to have destroyed about four hundred and fifty Arab aircraft. Other counts run from about three hundred and seventy to four hundred and forty. Israel lost between twenty-six and forty-eight aircraft, depending on what is counted. *(estimate)*
- Israel had gained air superiority: its aircraft could operate much more freely, while Arab air support was greatly reduced. *(established)*
