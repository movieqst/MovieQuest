# Six-Day War, 5-10 June 1967: research brief

Prepared for the Battles of History reenactment. Written November 2026.

## How this brief was made, and its limits

- **Method.** Built from web-search results only. Direct page fetches were blocked in this environment for en.wikipedia.org, wikiwand.com, jewishvirtuallibrary.org, history.state.gov, idf.il and archives.mod.gov.il. So the facts come from search-engine summaries of those pages and others. The page itself was not read.
  - Where a figure or quotation matters, check it against the page named before it goes into narration.
  - Wikipedia was reached only through mirrors (ipfs, kiwix, cavac, doc.surf and others). These are cited as "Wikipedia (via mirror)".
- **Tags.** Every statement carries one tag:
  - **[established]**: historians on all sides agree.
  - **[israeli]**: rests on Israeli official or participant accounts.
  - **[arab]**: rests on Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian or Palestinian accounts.
  - **[disputed]**: historians disagree. The main positions and who holds them are given.
  - **[estimate]**: a number that is an estimate. A range is given where sources differ.
  - **[unverified]**: comes from standard histories I know but did not confirm with a source during this session. Check it before use.
- **Fact and interpretation.** Each section gives the event first. Later interpretation is marked "Interpretation:".
- **Names.** Spellings vary between sources: Abu-Ageila/Abu Ageila, Um Katef/Umm Qatef, Tel Fakhr/Tel Faher, Mendler/Mandler, Narkis/Narkiss, Murtagi/Mortagy, Gifgafa/Gafgafa/Jifjafa. Both forms are given where useful.
- **What the war is called.** Israel calls it the Six-Day War. In Arabic it is usually *an-Naksa* ("the setback"), the word Nasser used in his 9 June speech, or the June War / 1967 War. The page should name both.

## 1. Background, 1948-1967

### 1948-1956, in brief
- The 1948 war ended in 1949 with armistice agreements between Israel and Egypt, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon. These were not peace treaties. The armistice lines left Jerusalem divided: West Jerusalem was held by Israel, and East Jerusalem with the Old City by Jordan. The Mount Scopus campus was an Israeli enclave inside Jordanian-held territory. Jordan held the West Bank, Egypt administered Gaza, and demilitarized zones (DMZs) lay along the Syrian line. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Battle of Ammunition Hill": "after the 1949 Armistice Agreements, parts of Mount Scopus remained an Israeli enclave")
- The 1948 war created the Palestinian refugee problem (the *Nakba*). Its causes are a separate, long-disputed subject. Here it matters only because many refugees of 1967 were already refugees from 1948 (see section 6). [established]

### The 1956 Suez war and UNEF
- In 1956 Israel invaded Sinai in coordination with Britain and France. Under US and Soviet pressure it withdrew in 1957. The UN Emergency Force (UNEF I) was then deployed on the Egyptian side only: along the armistice line in Gaza and Sinai, and at Sharm el-Sheikh, which controls the Straits of Tiran. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "United Nations Emergency Force"; UN UNISPAL)
- Israel declared that it would treat any renewed closure of the Straits of Tiran to its shipping as an act of war, a *casus belli*. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Origins of the Six-Day War": "any closure of the Straits would be considered an act of war, or justification for war")

### Water: the National Water Carrier and the Headwaters Diversion Plan
- Israel's National Water Carrier drew water from the Sea of Galilee, and was completed in 1964 [unverified: completion year].
- At its January 1964 summit in Cairo, the Arab League decided to divert two Jordan headwaters, the Hasbani and the Banias, away from the Sea of Galilee. The Banias was to go towards the Yarmouk and a dam at Mukhaiba, and the Hasbani towards the Litani. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Headwater Diversion Plan (Jordan River)" and "War over Water (Jordan River)")
  - The plan would have cut the Carrier's installed capacity by about 35% and Israel's overall water supply by about 11%. [estimate] (same)
- Syria began its part, a Banias-to-Yarmouk canal, in 1965, financed by Egypt and Saudi Arabia. Israeli tanks and artillery fired on the diversion works in March, May and August 1965. [established] (same)
  - Israeli-sympathetic accounts say the 1965 clashes began with Syrian fire on Israeli patrols. [israeli]
- Lebanon halted its part in July 1965, and the Arab states eventually abandoned the project. [established] (same)
- Interpretation: historians agree the water dispute fed the escalation. They disagree on how much weight it carries among the causes of the war. [disputed]

### Syrian-Israeli clashes in the demilitarized zones
- Israel and Syria each claimed rights in the DMZs set by the 1949 armistice. Israeli cultivation there, and Syrian fire on it from the heights above, caused repeated clashes between 1949 and 1967. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Origins of the Six-Day War")
- **Dayan's later statement.** In interviews with the journalist Rami Tal in 1976-77, published in *Yedioth Ahronoth* in 1997 after Dayan's death, Moshe Dayan said Israel sent tractors into the DMZ knowing the Syrians would shoot. If they did not shoot, the tractors were told to advance further. He said "at least 80 percent" of the clashes started this way. [israeli: an Israeli participant's own retrospective account] (Deseret News, 12 May 1997, https://www.deseret.com/1997/5/12/19311705/dayan-felt-anguish-over-key-decisions/; Wikipedia via mirror, "Origins of the Six-Day War")
  - Caveats: the exact wording differs between versions, the interview was not recorded as an official document, and Dayan was speaking a decade later. [disputed: weight and reliability]
  - Some sources date the interview to 1976 and some to 1976-77, but none to 1979. [established that the publication year was 1997]
- Syrian positions on the Golan escarpment also shelled Israeli settlements in the Hula and Jordan valleys. [israeli; the shelling itself is established, its causes are disputed as above]

### Fatah raids
- From January 1965 Fatah, then a small group backed by Syria, carried out sabotage raids into Israel. Many were launched through Jordan or Lebanon. [established; unverified: exact start date and number of raids]
- Syria's Ba'athist government, after the February 1966 coup that installed Nureddin al-Atassi as president with Salah Jadid as the effective strongman, openly backed a "people's war". [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Ahmed Suwaydani")

### The Samu raid, 13 November 1966
- On 11 November 1966 an Israeli border-police vehicle hit a mine near the Jordanian border. Three policemen were killed and six wounded. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "1966 attack on Samu")
- On 13 November a large Israeli force attacked the West Bank village of as-Samu, then under Jordan, and destroyed many houses. A Jordanian force sent to intervene was ambushed. [established]
  - **Casualties.** The figures differ: a Jordanian count of soldiers and civilians, and an Israeli count of one officer killed and about ten wounded. The search snippets were incomplete, so I could not confirm the exact Jordanian toll. [estimate; the UNTSO report and the Jordanian complaint S/7593 hold the official figures] (UN UNISPAL, UNTSO report, https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-180254/)
- UN Security Council Resolution 228 (25 November 1966) censured Israel by 14 votes to 0, with New Zealand abstaining. It warned that further military reprisals "will not be tolerated". [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "UNSC Resolution 228")
- Interpretation: historians widely argue that the raid humiliated King Hussein, set off riots in the West Bank and added to the pressure that led him into the pact with Nasser in May 1967. [established as the mainstream reading] (Cambridge UP, *Jordan in the 1967 War* (Mutawi), chapter "On the brink of war")

### The air battle of 7 April 1967
- The clash began over cultivation and fire in the DMZ near the Sea of Galilee. It grew into artillery duels and then an air battle that ran as far as Damascus. Israeli Mirages shot down six Syrian MiG-21s, two of them in sight of Damascus. [established for 6] (israeled.org timeline; Aviation Safety Network records for 7 April 1967; Wikipedia via mirror)
  - **Contemporary claims differed.** A CIA memo dated 7 April 1967 records an Israeli official claiming seven MiG-21s in 30 minutes. It also records the Syrian claim to have downed at least one Mirage, which Israel denied. [disputed: 6 vs. 7 kills; Syrian claim unconfirmed] (CIA FOIA reading room, https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp79t00826a001800010032-1)
- Interpretation: many historians count this as the first step on "the road to war in June 1967". [established as a common reading]

## 2. The crisis, May-June 1967

### The Soviet report (13 May)
- On about 13 May 1967 Soviet officials, including Deputy Foreign Minister Vladimir Semyonov, told Egypt that Israel was massing 10-12 brigades on the Syrian border. The message went through the Egyptian delegation in Moscow, which included Anwar Sadat, then Speaker of the National Assembly. [established that the warning was given]
  - **Date.** Some sources put Sadat's briefing on 13 May, others on 15 May. [disputed] (Middle East Journal editor's blog, https://mideasti.blogspot.com/2017/05/may-1967-soviet-warning-egypt-orders.html; Wilson Center, "The Soviet Union and the Six-Day War: Revelations from the Polish Archives")
  - **The warning was false.** No such concentration existed. U Thant reported that UN observers "have confirmed the absence of troop concentrations and significant troop movements on both sides of the line". [established]
  - Egypt's chief of staff, General Mohamed Fawzi, visited the Syrian front on 14 May and later said he saw no Israeli build-up. [arab: Fawzi's own account] (KCL *Fifty Years War* interview with Fawzi, 1997, https://archives.kingscollections.org/index.php/fifty-years-war-4-5)
  - Israel offered to fly the Soviet ambassador to the border to see for himself. He declined. [israeli]
- Interpretation: why Moscow sent the warning is still debated. The possibilities raised include a deliberate attempt to provoke, a misreading of Israeli threats against Syria, or an attempt to shore up the Syrian regime. [disputed] (Wilson Center; see Ginor and Remez, *Foxbats over Dimona*, for one minority thesis [unverified])

### Egyptian troops into Sinai (from 14 May)
- On 14-15 May Egypt put its forces on alert and sent divisions into Sinai in open parades through Cairo. [established] (Jewish Virtual Library, "Egypt's Statement on Troop Movements into Sinai")
- By early June about 100,000 Egyptian troops, 900-950 tanks and about 1,100 APCs were in Sinai (see section 3). [estimate]

### UNEF withdrawal (16-19 May)
- **16 May, about 22:00.** Brigadier Eiz-El-Din Mokhtar gave the UNEF commander, Indian General Indar Jit Rikhye, a letter from General Fawzi. It asked for the withdrawal of "all U.N. troops which install O.P.s along our borders". Mokhtar also asked verbally that UNEF leave Sharm el-Sheikh and El Sabha that night. Rikhye said he could not comply without orders from the Secretary-General. [established] (FRUS XIX doc. 6, editorial note; U Thant's report A/6730)
- U Thant said a request had to come from the Egyptian government to him directly, and that it would be for complete withdrawal, not a partial one. [established] (U Thant's report)
- **18 May.** Foreign Minister Mahmoud Riad formally asked for UNEF's termination. U Thant agreed the same day. He held that UNEF was stationed on Egyptian soil with Egypt's consent, and Israel had refused to host it on its side. [established] (A/6730; Spectator, 26 May 1967, "U Thant's war")
  - One UN document (S/7906) gives the date as "13 May", which is probably a typing or OCR error. [established that 18 May is correct]
- **19 May.** The UN flag was lowered in Gaza. [established]
- Interpretation: U Thant's quick consent was much criticised, then and since. Critics say he should have referred the matter to the General Assembly or played for time. His defenders say he had no legal choice. [disputed] (Rikhye, *The Sinai Blunder*, 1980; Spectator 1967)

### Closing the Straits of Tiran (22-23 May)
- Egyptian forces took over Sharm el-Sheikh. On 22 May, speaking to pilots at Bir Gifgafa air base in Sinai, Nasser announced that the Gulf of Aqaba was closed to Israeli-flagged ships and to ships carrying strategic goods to Israel. The closure took effect on 23 May. [established] (Jewish Virtual Library, "Egypt Reimposes a Blockade on the Straits of Tiran"; Middle East Journal blog, 22-23 May; Jerusalem Post "Casus Belli in the Red Sea")
  - Sources date the announcement 21, 22 or 23 May, depending on whether they mean the speech, the broadcast or the effective date. The speech is usually dated the night of 22 May. [disputed: minor] (israeled.org gives 21 May)
  - Nasser's widely quoted line: "Under no circumstances can we permit the Israeli flag to pass through the Gulf of Aqaba." [arab: Nasser's own speech; wording varies by translation]
  - Egypt announced that the straits were mined. Later accounts say they were not. [disputed/unverified]
- **Why it mattered.**
  - Eilat was Israel's only Red Sea port and the entry point for its oil from Iran. [established]
  - One source says about 90% of Israel's oil came through the straits. [estimate; figure from a single partisan source, check against Oren or Morris]
  - Israel had said since 1957 that closure would be a casus belli. [established]
  - President Johnson said publicly on 19 June 1967 that the closure was the act that most made war likely. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Origins")
- **Nasser's own expectation.** He reportedly told colleagues that with troops in Sinai the chance of war was "fifty-fifty", and that closing the straits would make war "one hundred percent" certain. [arab: attributed by Egyptian participants; quoted by Karsh and others; verify the original source]

### Egypt-Jordan defence pact (30 May) and Iraq
- On 30 May King Hussein flew to Cairo and signed a mutual defence pact with Nasser in front of television cameras. Jordan's forces would come under Egyptian command in war: the Egyptian general Abdul Munim Riad was sent to Amman to command the Jordanian front. [established] (Cambridge UP, Mutawi, *Jordan in the 1967 War*; Wikipedia via mirror, "Abdul Munim Riad")
- Iraqi troops and armour began to move towards Jordan, and the pact allowed them in. A New Zealand paper carried the headline on 1 June. Iraq formally joined the Egypt-Syria-Jordan alignment on 4 June. [established] (Papers Past, *The Press*, 1 June 1967; Wikipedia via mirror)
- Interpretation: the pact meant Israel faced armies on three fronts under a unified command. Israeli accounts saw it as the moment encirclement became real. [israeli] Jordanian accounts present Hussein as a reluctant ally who could not stay out of a war his people wanted. [arab] (Cambridge UP, Mutawi: Jordan "embroiled in a war he neither wanted nor anticipated")

### Israel's "waiting period" (*hamtana*), 23 May-4 June
- Israel mobilised its reserves, which halted much of its economy. The cabinet chose to wait while diplomacy ran its course. [established]
- **Eban in Washington.** Foreign Minister Abba Eban went to Paris, London and Washington. On 26 May President Johnson told him: "Israel will not be alone unless it decides to go it alone." [established] (FRUS XIX, summary and doc. 82)
  - The US proposed an international naval convoy (the "Regatta" plan) to open the straits. It drew little support. [established; unverified: details]
- **28 May.** The Israeli cabinet postponed military action. That evening Prime Minister Levi Eshkol gave a live radio address from a hand-corrected text and stumbled over it. The public heard it as hesitant, and it hurt confidence. [established] (National Library of Israel blog, "The Prime Minister's Stuttering Speech", https://blog.nli.org.il/en/eshkol_stuttering_speech/)
- **National unity government.** Under public and political pressure, Gahal (Menachem Begin, Yosef Sapir) joined as ministers without portfolio. Moshe Dayan of Rafi became Minister of Defence on **1 June 1967**, a post Eshkol had held himself. [established] (Israel MoD Archives exhibition, "The National Unity Government"; Begin Heritage Center timeline)
- **Meir Amit's mission.** Mossad chief Meir Amit went to Washington around 31 May-1 June. He returned with the impression that the US would not object to Israeli action. Historians often call this a "yellow" rather than a "green" light. [israeli/disputed: the phrase is historians' interpretation, not a quotation from the documents] (FRUS XIX; CounterPunch summary; Oren, *Six Days of War*, [unverified])
  - Amit reportedly told McNamara that Israel could expect about 4,000 casualties in a war. [estimate; single secondary source]
- **4 June.** The Israeli cabinet approved the attack. Hod and Rabin had fixed the timing of Operation Moked. [established] (idf.il, Operation Moked page)

### Diplomacy: US, USSR, UN
- The US tried to prevent war through the convoy idea and through messages to Nasser. Talks for an Egyptian vice-president, Zakaria Mohieddin, to visit Washington were set for 7 June but overtaken by the war. [established; unverified: date of the planned visit]
- The USSR urged restraint on both sides once the crisis was under way. In late May it warned Egypt against striking first. [established]
- **Operation Dawn (*al-Fajr*).** An Egyptian air strike on Israel, planned by Field Marshal Amer for 27 May, was cancelled at the last minute by Nasser. [disputed]
  - Accounts of why differ: Soviet pressure after Washington learned of the plan, or Nasser's own decision.
  - Some historians, for example John Quigley, doubt that a real attack was imminent. Others, for example Oren, treat it as a real plan.
  - (Wikipedia via mirror, "Operation Dawn (1967)", citing Hussein el-Shafei's testimony)
- The UN Security Council met but took no effective action before 5 June. [established]

### The disputed questions

**Did Nasser intend war?** [disputed]
- **Position A: he did not want or plan war, and it was brinkmanship.**
  - Avi Shlaim: "There is general agreement among commentators that Nasser neither wanted nor planned to go to war with Israel." (quoted in AIJAC, "Was 1967 an accident?")
  - Yitzhak Rabin, Israel's chief of staff, told *Le Monde* (Eric Rouleau, 28 or 29 February 1968): "I do not think Nasser wanted war. The two divisions he sent into Sinai on 14 May would not have been enough to unleash an offensive against Israel. He knew it and we knew it." Rouleau confirmed the quotation. Wording differs slightly between translations. [israeli: Israeli participant] (Cambridge UP, *The Six-Day War and Israeli Self-Defense* (Quigley), chapter "No threat, no matter")
  - Menachem Begin, in a speech to the Israeli National Defense College (dated 2 or 8 August 1982), said the Egyptian concentrations "did not prove that Nasser was really about to attack us. We must be honest with ourselves. We decided to attack him." [israeli; date disputed]
- **Position B: he knowingly took steps he expected to lead to war, and hoped Israel would fire first.**
  - Efraim Karsh, Michael Oren and Benny Morris, in different degrees, hold this view. They point to the closing of the straits despite the known casus belli, the "fifty-fifty" remark above, Nasser's public speeches (for example on 26 May to trade unionists, about the battle being general and the aim the destruction of Israel [arab: Nasser's speech; check the wording]), and Operation Dawn. (AIJAC; Jewish Chronicle, 2 June 2017; Wikipedia via mirror, "Origins")
- **Position C: Israel used his brinkmanship as a pretext.**
  - Ilan Pappé (Palestine Chronicle) and Norman Finkelstein.
- **On the evidence.** Yezid Sayigh notes that the lack of Egyptian official documents keeps the question open. [established that the evidence gap exists]

**Was Israel's strike pre-emptive or preventive?** [disputed]
- **"Pre-emptive"** means attacking an imminent attack. This is the official Israeli position, and the one held by most Israeli and many Western historians, including Oren. They cite the scale of Egyptian deployments, the blockade (itself an act of war under the 1957 understanding), the encirclement by pact, and Israel's economic inability to stay mobilised.
- **"Preventive"** means striking before a threat matured, or choosing war when the threat was not imminent. This is held by Quigley, Shlaim (in part), Finkelstein and most Arab historiography. They cite the Rabin and Begin statements above, and US intelligence estimates that Israel would win quickly whoever struck first.
- **US intelligence.** The CIA judged in late May that Israel would win quickly. This is documented in FRUS XIX. [established]
- **A legal question as well.** Some scholars argue the blockade made Israel's action lawful self-defence whether or not an Egyptian ground attack was imminent. [disputed]

## 4. The war, day by day

Times are local Israel time unless stated (Israel and Egypt were then one hour apart, Cairo ahead) [unverified: offset].

### 5 June: Operation Focus (*Moked*) and the opening of all fronts

**The air strike**
- The first Israeli wave struck Egyptian airfields at about **07:45 Israel time** (08:45 Cairo). Most planes flew low over the Mediterranean to avoid radar, and some came in from unexpected directions. [established] (Jewish Virtual Library, "Israel Air Force in the Six-Day War"; idf.il)
  - The time was chosen because Egyptian dawn patrols had landed, crews were at breakfast and commanders were on their way to work. [israeli: the planners' account; widely accepted]
- **The first wave** attacked 11 Egyptian airfields with 183 aircraft (some give 196). It hit runways first, using special runway-penetrating bombs, then aircraft on the ground. [estimate] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Operation Focus")
- **First-wave Egyptian losses** are variously given:
  - 197 aircraft and 8 radar stations (idf.il, Operation Moked page; Wikipedia via mirror)
  - 189 (Jewish Virtual Library)
  - about 186 (other compilations)
  - [estimate]
- **The second wave** hit about 14 more airfields and destroyed some 107 more Egyptian aircraft. [estimate] (Wikipedia via mirror)
- Ground crews turned aircraft around (refuelled and rearmed) in about 7.5-10 minutes, so the IAF could fly repeated sorties. [israeli/estimate]
- **Total Egyptian aircraft destroyed on 5 June:** about 286-300+ by most counts. One source says "over 250 destroyed or disabled". [estimate]
- **Fields hit included** (by name in the literature) Bir Gifgafa, Bir Thamada, El Arish, Jebel Libni, Abu Suweir, Kabrit, Fayid, Inshas, Cairo West, Beni Suef, Cairo International, Luxor and Ras Banas. [unverified: list; check Hammel or the Operation Focus article]
- Interpretation: the strike gave Israel air superiority for the rest of the war. It is the single most decisive event of the war. [established]
- **The famous Egyptian command problem:** Amer and senior officers were in the air flying to Sinai when the strike came, and Egyptian air defences reportedly held fire. Mohamed Sedky Mahmoud's Wikipedia entry places him with Amer and Gen. Anwar al-Qadi on that flight. [established/arab]

**Jordan, Syria and Iraq**
- Around midday, after Jordanian, Syrian and Iraqi aircraft attacked Israeli targets, the IAF struck their air forces.
  - **Jordan.** The Royal Jordanian Air Force of about 34 combat aircraft (Hunters) was effectively destroyed, mostly at Amman and Mafraq. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Operation Focus")
  - **Syria.** About two-thirds of the Syrian air force was destroyed. [estimate; unverified this session]
  - **Iraq.** The Iraqi base H-3 was struck that evening. [established in outline; unverified: details]
- **Total destroyed on 5 June:** about **450 Arab aircraft** by evening. Some sources say 452 across the war, including 79 in air combat. [estimate] (Wikipedia via mirror)
- **Israeli aircraft lost on 5 June:** 19 is commonly given, mostly to ground fire. [estimate]
- **Israeli aircraft lost in the whole war:** given variously as 46, 40-48 or 32. [estimate; disputed]

**Jordanian front opens (5 June)**
- That morning Israel sent King Hussein a message through the UNTSO chief of staff, Norwegian General Odd Bull. It said Israel would not attack Jordan if Jordan stayed out. [established that a message was sent; exact wording only partly preserved] (search summaries of Wikipedia and Schleifer)
- **What Hussein was told.** Cairo told him the Egyptian air force was striking Israel, when it had in fact just been destroyed. Jordanian radar at Ajloun saw heavy air traffic, which was passed to Riad and read as Egyptian aircraft. [established as the outline]
  - Hussein's own account is *My War with Israel* (with Vance and Lauer, 1969). [arab]
  - The memoirs of Zaid Ibn Shaker, reported in the Jerusalem Post, blame false Egyptian information. [arab]
- **About 10:00 onwards.** Jordanian artillery began shelling West Jerusalem, Israeli towns and air bases. Long-range "Long Tom" guns fired at the Tel Aviv area and Ramat David air base. [established] (israeled.org; Wikipedia via mirror)
  - **Israeli count.** About 6,000 shells fell on Israeli Jerusalem over the fighting and some 900 buildings were damaged. [estimate; israeli]
  - **Israeli civilian casualties in Jerusalem:** 20 killed, more than 1,000 wounded. [israeli/estimate] (Jerusalem Post "Remembering and commemorating"; Wikipedia via mirror, "Jordanian campaign (1967)")
- **Shortly after noon.** Jordanian troops seized Government House (Armon HaNatziv), the UNTSO headquarters in the demilitarised zone south of the Old City. Schleifer says West Bank Command ordered it. [established] (Schleifer, IPS)
- **The afternoon of 5 June.** An Israeli counter-attack (Jerusalem Brigade) retook Government House. [established]

**Interpretation:** why Jordan entered the war is disputed [disputed]:
- Hussein was obliged by the pact and by Egyptian deception, and Riad held command. This is the Jordanian/Arab and mainstream view.
- Hussein chose to fight for Arab legitimacy and survival. (Mutawi; Shlaim's *Lion of Jordan* [unverified])
- Israel would have taken the West Bank anyway. This is argued by some Arab and revisionist writers and rejected by most Israeli historians, who say Israel had no plan to attack Jordan on 5 June.

### Sinai: the three divisional thrusts (5-9 June)

The Egyptian line was based on fortified positions at Rafah-El Arish in the north and Abu-Ageila/Um Katef in the centre, with armour in depth at Jebel Libni, Bir Gifgafa and Bir Hasana. [established]

**Tal (north): Rafah, Khan Yunis, El Arish**
- On 5 June, after the air strike (about 08:00-08:15), Tal's division attacked at Khan Yunis and Rafah at the junction of Gaza and Sinai. It went round the strongest defences and through them in about 12 hours of hard fighting at Rafah and the Jiradi pass. It reached the outskirts of El Arish that night and took El Arish on 6 June. [established] (Weapons and Warfare, "Israel Tal"; warhistory.org "The Sinai Campaign of 1967 Part II")
- Gavish and Hod had argued over how long to leave between the air strike and the ground attack. Hod wanted a long gap. They settled on about half an hour. [israeli] (Forward, "Forverts in 1967")

**Yoffe (centre): through the dunes**
- Yoffe's reservist division crossed sand dunes between Tal and Sharon that the Egyptians thought impassable for armour. On the first night it took the Bir Lahfan junction and blocked Egyptian reinforcements moving north. [established]
  - A night tank battle at Bir Lahfan is dated 5-6 June by some sources and 6-7 June by others. [disputed: date]
  - (Wikipedia via mirror, "Avraham Yoffe"; warhistory.org)

**Sharon (centre-south): Abu-Ageila / Um Katef**
- The Abu-Ageila defences were a deep, layered position: three trench lines, minefields, artillery and an armoured reserve. They blocked the road from Nitzana. [established]
- **The plan.** Sharon combined armour, infantry and paratroopers:
  - a frontal tank attack;
  - an infantry brigade moving at night through the dunes to attack the trenches from the flank;
  - helicopter-landed paratroopers to silence the Egyptian artillery;
  - artillery concentrated for a short, heavy barrage. [established]
- **Timings.**
  - Wikipedia gives the armoured assault on Um Katef as starting at 08:15 on 5 June.
  - The main night attack followed a barrage from about 23:30 to 24:00 on 5-6 June.
  - Both are probably right, for different phases. [disputed: sequence]
  - (Wikipedia via mirror, "Battle of Abu-Ageila (1967)"; Military Wiki)
- The position fell by the morning of 6 June. [established]
- Interpretation: Abu-Ageila is studied as a model of a combined-arms night attack. [established as an assessment, in US Army and other staff literature]

**Amer's withdrawal order (6 June)**
- On the afternoon of 6 June, after news of Abu-Ageila's fall, Field Marshal Amer ordered all forces in Sinai to withdraw west of the Suez Canal. [established]
  - Fawzi's memoirs say Amer ordered a withdrawal "within one night", with troops to take their personal weapons. The operations branch then changed it to three nights, and later two. [arab: Fawzi's memoir] (summarised by Weapons and Warfare; CIA *Studies in Intelligence* vol. 59 no. 1, review of Fawzi's memoirs)
  - Other accounts say "within a single day". [disputed: wording]
- The order had no plan for phases or rearguards. Different units received it at different times. Some units fell apart and others kept fighting, and the withdrawal turned into a rout under air attack. [established] (Weapons and Warfare; Library of Congress Egypt Country Study, https://countrystudies.us/egypt/130.htm)
- Interpretation: Egyptian generals' memoirs (Fawzi, and others summarised in al-Majalla) blame Amer's leadership and the politicised high command. Nasser's circle blamed Amer. Amer's family and supporters dispute this. [disputed]

**Mitla and Giddi passes; reaching the Suez Canal (7-9 June)**
- Yoffe's brigades raced to the western passes to block the Egyptian retreat. Israeli forces blocked the Giddi and Mitla passes and Bir Gifgafa, and aircraft struck columns jammed in the bottlenecks. [established] (Egypt Country Study)
  - Some accounts say the blocking forces at Mitla were tanks nearly out of fuel, some towed into position. [israeli; unverified]
- Wrecked vehicles filled the Mitla Pass. Photographs (Magnum; UPI, World Press Photo 1967) show the scene on 8 June. [established]
  - Figures for Egyptian tanks destroyed vary widely. [estimate; disputed]
- Israeli forces reached the Suez Canal on 8 June. By 9 June Israel held all of Sinai to the canal. [established]
  - Gavish later said he took the canal line, and the Gaza Strip, without explicit orders from above. Dayan had at first wanted to stop short of the canal. [israeli: Gavish's account] (KCL *Fifty Years War* interview with Gavish; ahron.substack summary)
- **Sharm el-Sheikh** was taken on 7 June by Israeli naval forces and paratroopers. The Egyptian garrison had largely left. [established that it was taken on 7 June without significant resistance; unverified: whether it was "found abandoned"] (Time, 1967; Wikipedia via mirror)

**Gaza (5-6 June)**
- The Gaza Strip was defended by the Egyptian-commanded 20th Palestinian Division (Palestine Liberation Army) and Egyptian units. Tal's forces and a reserve brigade took it on 5-6 June after hard fighting at Khan Yunis and Gaza city. [unverified: unit details; that Gaza fell on 5-6 June is established]
- **UNEF casualties.** UNEF units still in Gaza, not yet withdrawn, were hit:
  - 15 peacekeepers were killed, 14 Indian and 1 Brazilian, with 17 wounded. [established]
  - Three Indian soldiers died when Israeli aircraft strafed a white-painted UNEF convoy just south of Khan Yunis. Others died when Israeli artillery hit Indian camps. [established] (UN documents S/7930 and S/7957, https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/522195/files/S_7930-EN.pdf; Wikipedia via mirror, "UNEF")
  - Interpretation: UN reports attribute these deaths to Israeli fire, in a battle zone. [established]

### Jordan, the West Bank and Jerusalem (5-7 June)

**Jerusalem**
- **Night of 5-6 June.** The 55th Paratroopers Brigade (Gur) was moved to Jerusalem on short notice. It had been meant for Sinai. [established]
- **Ammunition Hill, 6 June, from about 02:30.** The 3rd Company of the 66th Battalion attacked the Jordanian position at the Police School and Ammunition Hill, a fortified hill with deep trenches. A force from the 2nd Company joined. The fighting was hand-to-hand in the trenches for about four hours. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Battle of Ammunition Hill"; Ammunition Hill museum site)
  - **Killed:**
    - 36 Israelis (21 on the hill and 12 in the communication trenches, per the museum account; 35 or 37 in some counts)
    - 71 Jordanians (about 70)
    - [estimate: small variation] (Israel National News; Ammunition Hill museum; Wikipedia via mirror)
  - Interpretation: both sides honour the battle. Israel keeps a national memorial there. The Jordanian defenders are remembered for fighting to the last; the Israeli memorial itself acknowledges this [unverified: wording of the Israeli memorial's tribute].
- **6 June.** Israeli forces linked up with the Mount Scopus enclave by mid-morning, and took the American Colony, Sheikh Jarrah and the Rockefeller Museum area. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror)
- **6 June.** The Harel Brigade took the ridges north of Jerusalem, Radar Hill and Nebi Samuel, and cut the Jerusalem-Ramallah road. [unverified: details]
- **Latrun.** The Latrun police fort was taken on 6 June after the Jordanian garrison left it under tank fire. [established] (search summary)
- **7 June, morning.**
  - The paratroopers took the **Augusta Victoria** ridge (Mount of Olives), with Harel helping. The night before, Israeli aircraft had attacked Jordanian reinforcements coming up from Jericho. [established] (Hebrew-language Hamichlol, "The campaign for Jerusalem in the Six-Day War")
  - They then broke in through the **Lions' Gate** (St Stephen's Gate) into the Old City. [established]
  - Gur radioed: "*Har HaBayit beyadeinu*", "The Temple Mount is in our hands." [israeli: well documented; recording exists] (Jewish Virtual Library; Israel National News)
  - The paratroopers reached the **Western Wall**. The IDF Chief Rabbi, Shlomo Goren, blew the shofar there. [established]
  - Dayan, Rabin and Narkiss walked to the Wall that afternoon (GPO photographs). [established]
- **Israeli soldiers killed in the Jerusalem fighting:** about 180-182. [estimate] (Jerusalem Post; Ammunition Hill memorial)
- **Jordanian losses in Jerusalem:** "several hundred soldiers and civilians". [estimate] (search summary of Jerusalem Post / Forward)
- Schleifer (IPS) gives a Jordanian-side and Palestinian-side narrative of the fall of the Arab city. He stresses the lack of anti-aircraft guns and tanks in the city and the small garrison of fewer than 5,000. [arab]

**Northern West Bank (Jenin, Nablus)**
- Peled's division (Northern Command) crossed into the Jenin area at about 17:00 on 5 June. Lt. Col. Moshe Bar-Kochva's brigade met strong resistance at Arraba and on the Jenin-Nablus road. [established] (Eric Hammel, Warfare History Network, "The Rumble and Roar of Tanks")
- Jenin fell on 6 June after a tank battle with Jordanian Pattons. [established; unverified: exact time]
- Jordan's 40th Armoured Brigade moved from the Damiya bridge area towards the battle under air attack. JCPA says only eight of its tanks made it back across the river. [estimate; israeli] (JCPA; Hammel)
- Nablus fell on 7 June. [established]

**Southern West Bank and the Jordan Valley**
- On 7 June Israeli forces took Bethlehem and Hebron. Hebron surrendered without a fight and white sheets hung from houses. Hammel suggests the residents feared revenge for the 1929 massacre of Jews there. [established that it surrendered; the motive is that writer's interpretation]
- Jericho was taken on 7 June. [established]
- The **Allenby (King Hussein) Bridge** and **Damiya (Adam) Bridge** were brought down during the fighting. A photograph caption (Australian War Memorial) says the Allenby Bridge was destroyed by Israeli forces. [established for Allenby; unverified for Damiya]
- **Ceasefire on the Jordanian front.** Jordan accepted the UN ceasefire on 7 June, and it took effect that evening. [established; unverified: exact hour]

**The Nasser-Hussein phone call (6 June)**
- Israeli military intelligence (then Unit 515, now 8200) recorded a radio-telephone call between Nasser and Hussein early on 6 June. In it they agreed to say that US and British aircraft were fighting for Israel. Israel released it on 8 June. [established: Time 1967 reported that neither side denied it] (Time, "The Quickest War"; Middle East Quarterly, "The lie that won't die")
- Radio Cairo then claimed US and British carriers were giving Israel air cover. Egypt and other Arab states cut relations with the US. [established]
- Hussein later accepted that no US or British aircraft took part. [established]

### The USS Liberty (8 June)

**Facts generally agreed** [established]
- USS *Liberty* (AGTR-5), a US Navy signals-intelligence ship working for the NSA, was in international waters off the north Sinai coast near El Arish.
- On the afternoon of 8 June, from about 14:00, Israeli aircraft attacked it with cannon, rockets and napalm, followed by three Israeli motor torpedo boats from about 14:24.
- A torpedo hit amidships, tore a hole of about 40 feet, and killed 25 men in the research (cryptologic) spaces.
- **Toll:** 34 Americans killed and 171 wounded (sources give 171-174 wounded).
- (Britannica, "USS Liberty incident"; USNI *Proceedings*, "Friendless Fire", June 2003; Al Jazeera, 9 June 2015)
- Israel apologised, called it mistaken identification, and paid compensation to the families and the US government [unverified: amounts and dates].

**Official findings**
- The US Navy Court of Inquiry (June 1967) found mistaken identity. Its public summary said it was not the Court's job to rule on the culpability of the attackers, and it heard no evidence from Israel. [established]
- The CIA found "no malice". [established]
- Israel's own inquiry (from 18 June) found error. Israeli accounts say the ship was confused with the Egyptian vessel *El Quseir*. [israeli; the El Quseir point was not confirmed in this session's searches]

**Disputed questions** [disputed]
- **Was it deliberate?**
  - Many survivors (USS Liberty Veterans Association), Admiral Thomas Moorer's 2003 independent panel, and some former US officials say yes. These include an NSA director quoted as saying it "couldn't be anything else but deliberate", and Capt. Ward Boston, the Court of Inquiry's legal counsel, who in a 2004 affidavit said senior officials told the Court to reach the "accident" verdict. (Fox News; Al Jazeera; Arab News)
  - Judge A. Jay Cristol (*The Liberty Incident*, 2002) and Israeli official histories say no: it was a series of errors under fog of war. (Navy History, review; americandiplomacy.web.unc.edu)
- **Was the flag visible, and how long did the attack last?** Survivors say the flag was flying and the attack lasted about 75 minutes. Cristol puts it at about 22-25 minutes.
- **Was the US inquiry adequate?** Critics note it lasted about a week and had no access to Israeli participants.
- **The calls continue.** In 2025-26 US Representative Thomas Massie called for a congressional investigation. [established that the call was made] (The New Arab)

Narration advice: present the agreed facts and both positions. Do not settle the question.

### Syria and the Golan (9-10 June)

**Background, 5-8 June**
- Syria shelled Israeli settlements from the heights. A small Syrian probe at Tel Dan and She'ar Yashuv on 6 June was repelled. Syria did not launch the full offensive the Arab plan foresaw. [established; unverified: detail of probe]

**Israel's decision (9 June)**
- Dayan had opposed attacking Syria, fearing Soviet intervention and over-stretch. [established]
- Settlers from the northern kibbutzim, who had been shelled for years, lobbied the government hard to take the heights. [established] (Der Freitag; Zisser, *Israel Studies*)
- Early on 9 June, after Syria said it accepted a ceasefire (reported at 03:30), Dayan changed his mind. He phoned Elazar directly and ordered the attack without first telling Eshkol or Rabin. [israeli: established in Israeli sources]
  - The time of the call is given variously as dawn, 06:00 or 07:00 (the IDF timeline gives 07:00 for the authorisation). [disputed: minor] (israeled.org; RUSI Journal vol. 170 issue 3; IDF timeline)
- Dayan later spoke of the decision with regret, as the Rami Tal interviews show. [israeli]

**The assault up the escarpment (9 June)**
- The main effort was in the north, where the slope was steepest and Syria expected it least. [established]
- **The 8th Armoured Brigade** (Mendler) was down to about 33 serviceable Shermans after Sinai. Its bulldozers led it up from near Kfar Szold and Givat HaEm through the Syrian line. Its objective was **Qala**, which opened the Mas'ade-Quneitra road. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Tel Faher", "Givat HaEm"; israeled.org)
  - The advance from Givat HaEm began at about 11:30. Some give 10:00. [disputed: minor]
- **The Golani Brigade** (Col. Yona Efrat) attacked the fortified Syrian posts:
  - **Tel Azaziat** was taken at about 17:00, relatively easily. [established]
  - **Tel Fakhr/Faher** had minefields, bunkers, trenches and wire. A Golani battalion moved against it from about 14:00. The bunkers fell at about 18:20 after close fighting. [established] (Jewish Virtual Library "Tel Faher"; Wikipedia via mirror)
  - **Losses at Tel Faher** [estimate; disputed]:
    - Israeli killed are given as 31-34 depending on source and scope. One Wikipedia figure, tagged "citation needed": of 260 Israeli attackers, 34 killed and 113 wounded; 62 Syrians killed and 20 captured. The memorial at the site lists 32 names.
    - Golani's total for the war is given as 59 dead, 23 of them at Tel Faher. That figure is for the brigade only and is not consistent with the others.
- Further south, other forces advanced from the Sea of Galilee area. [unverified: details]

**10 June: Banias, Quneitra, Butmiya, Rafid**
- During the night of 9-10 June the 8th Brigade advanced towards Banias, which was taken on the morning of 10 June. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror)
- On 10 June Israeli forces advanced across the plateau, from the north towards Mas'ade and Quneitra. Forces in the south moved up from the Sea of Galilee and the Yarmouk area through Fiq and Butmiya towards Rafid, and helicopter-borne troops were used [unverified: detail of the southern thrusts].
- **The disputed Quneitra announcement.** Damascus radio announced that Quneitra had fallen at about 08:45 (some say 08:30) on 10 June, roughly three hours before Israeli troops arrived, when they were still about 10 km away. [established that the announcement came early]
  - **Effect:** the Syrian redeployment turned into a disordered retreat along the Damascus road. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Quneitra"; Time 1967)
  - **Why it was made** [disputed]:
    - (a) To press the UN and the USSR to enforce a ceasefire quickly. This is the reading in Time in 1967 and in many Israeli and Western accounts.
    - (b) Blunder or panic in the Syrian high command.
    - (c) Later Syrian opposition and Ba'athist rivals, including Suwaydani, blamed Defence Minister Hafez al-Assad. Enab Baladi reports he "broke the loss news before the Israeli army's entry". The charge was used politically and is unproven.
  - Who wrote or authorised the broadcast is not established in the sources found.
- Quneitra was occupied on 10 June. [established]
- **Soviet pressure.** On 10 June Premier Kosygin sent Johnson several hotline messages, the first at 09:05 Washington time [unverified: time zone], warning of "independent decisions" and military action if Israel did not stop. Johnson ordered the Sixth Fleet to move towards the eastern Mediterranean as a signal. [established] (LBJ Library hotline log, https://discoverlbj.org/item/nsf-nsch-b19-e196706-i54; FRUS XIX docs. 243, 254-255; JTA)
- **Ceasefire: 10 June, 18:30.** [established] (IDF timeline)
  - One source gives 18:00 for Dayan's agreement. [disputed: minor]
  - The war ended with Israel holding the Golan up to a line east of Quneitra. [established]

### Nasser's resignation (9-10 June)
- On the evening of 9 June Nasser announced on television that he took full responsibility and was resigning in favour of Vice-President Zakaria Mohieddin. In the speech he called the defeat *al-Naksa*, "the setback". [established] (israeled.org transcript PDF; Library of Congress Egypt Country Study)
- Huge crowds in Cairo and other Arab cities demanded that he stay. The National Assembly refused the resignation, and on 10 June he withdrew it. [established] (Ahram Online, "The 'Sacred March'"; Gulf News)
  - Interpretation: whether the demonstrations were spontaneous or partly organised is disputed. [disputed]
- **Amer** was removed and arrested after an alleged coup plot. He died on 13 September 1967 [disputed]:
  - the government said it was suicide by poison;
  - his family said he was poisoned. (al-Majalla, "Did Abdel Nasser's right-hand man commit suicide, or was he killed?")

## 6. Aftermath

### Territory taken [established]
- **From Egypt:** the Sinai Peninsula (returned to Egypt by 1982 under the 1979 peace treaty) and the Gaza Strip.
- **From Jordan:** the West Bank, including East Jerusalem.
- **From Syria:** most of the Golan Heights.
- (FRUS XIX summary)
- Israel's area grew to about three and a half times its pre-war size [estimate; standard figure, unverified this session].
- On 27-28 June 1967 Israel extended its law and administration to East Jerusalem and the surrounding area, widening the city's boundaries. The UN General Assembly declared the measures invalid (Resolutions 2253 and 2254, July 1967). [established; unverified: resolution numbers]
- On 10-11 June 1967 the Moroccan (Mughrabi) Quarter in front of the Western Wall was demolished to create the Western Wall plaza, and its residents were removed. [established; not covered by this session's searches, verify]

### Palestinian refugees of 1967 (*an-Naksa* displacement)

**Numbers** [estimate; disputed]
- Israeli official estimate: about 200,000-250,000 left the West Bank.
- Jordanian and UNRWA counts: about 217,000 (177,000 registered with UNRWA).
- One compilation of UNRWA statistics confirmed by Egyptian sources: about 210,000 into Jordan, 125,000 Syrians from the Golan, and 220,000 in Egypt (mostly people leaving the Suez Canal cities).
- Some Palestinian and Jordanian estimates are much higher (800,000-1 million). These figures probably count people displaced more than once, or later.
- Wikipedia: 280,000-325,000 Palestinians left the West Bank and Gaza. About 145,000 of them were already refugees from 1948, so they were displaced a second time.
- Sources: Badil, 1995 compilation, https://badil.org/periodicals/article-74/1995/art12b.htm; Wikipedia via mirror, "Naksa"; Al-Araby.

**Circumstances** [disputed]
- **Flight.** Many fled the fighting and the air attacks, especially in the Jordan Valley camps near Jericho. Others went to join family or followed the Jordanian army. Mainstream historians agree that much of the movement was flight. [established]
- **Expulsion and destruction.** Some cases are documented, including by Israeli soldiers and historians:
  - The Latrun villages of **Imwas, Yalu and Beit Nuba** were emptied and destroyed. Soldiers report bulldozing from about 7 June. The site later became Canada Park. Israeli officials said the villages were destroyed in the fighting. Palestinian and Israeli human-rights groups (Al-Haq, Zochrot) call it planned expulsion. [established that the villages were emptied and razed; motive disputed] (Al-Haq; Zochrot; +972)
  - Houses in Qalqilya were demolished. [established in outline; unverified: scale]
  - Some people were bussed to the Jordan bridges. [disputed: scale]
  - After the war Israel allowed only a limited number to return. Some 14,000 to 17,000 came back under a 1967 Red Cross scheme. [unverified]
- **Golan.** The population before the war is put at about 130,000-137,000 (Akevot), or about 90,000 in another Akevot estimate. After the war only about 6,400 remained, mostly Druze in villages near Mount Hermon (August 1967 census).
  - Akevot cites an ICRC finding that the IDF "systematically expelled" residents. [arab/Israeli NGO documentation]
  - Israeli accounts stress flight with the retreating army. [israeli]
  - [disputed: proportion fled vs. expelled]
  - (Akevot Institute, "Displacement in the Golan"; +972; Brunel University thesis)
- From 1991 the UN General Assembly brought the 1967 "displaced persons" within UNRWA's mandate. [established]

### The Khartoum summit (29 August-1 September 1967)
- The Arab League summit in Khartoum ended on 1 September 1967. Its resolution called for "no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with it". These are the "three no's". [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Khartoum Resolution")
- **Interpretation** [disputed]:
  - In Israel, and in much Western writing, the resolution was taken as a flat rejection of peace.
  - Fred Khouri, Avi Shlaim and Yoram Meital argue that it also cleared the way for Egypt and Jordan to seek a political, not military, settlement short of formal peace and recognition. Shlaim notes that Israeli leaders did not read it that way at the time.
  - The summit also agreed Saudi, Kuwaiti and Libyan subsidies to Egypt and Jordan, and the lifting of the oil embargo. [established in outline]

### UN Security Council Resolution 242 (22 November 1967)
- Adopted unanimously. It calls for:
  - "withdrawal of Israeli armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict";
  - an end to the state of belligerency, and respect for the right of every state in the area "to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries";
  - freedom of navigation;
  - "a just settlement of the refugee problem".
  - [established] (UN text; FRUS XIX, final section)
- **The disputed wording** [disputed]:
  - The English text says "from territories", without "the". The equally authentic French text says "des territoires", which can be read as "from the territories".
  - Israel, the US drafters and the British sponsor Lord Caradon held that the omission was deliberate, to allow border adjustments. Caradon also said he did not intend large changes, which both camps quote. (Israel National News; ZOA; Milestone Documents)
  - Arab states, the USSR and, at the time, France held that it required withdrawal from all territories. Eugene Rostow, a US drafter, argued the reverse of the Arab reading, and others argue the French text controls. (palquest; lesclesdumoyenorient)
  - The resolution does not mention the Palestinians by name, only "refugees". This was a central Palestinian objection. [established]
- 242, together with Resolution 338 (1973), became the basis of most later peace diplomacy. [established]

### The War of Attrition (1967/69-1970)
- Fighting went on along the Suez Canal almost at once.
  - At **Ras el-Ish** (1 July 1967) an Egyptian commando force held a position south of Port Said against an Israeli advance. [established]
  - Egyptian missile boats **sank the Israeli destroyer INS *Eilat*** on 21 October 1967 off Port Said, killing 47. Some sources give other counts. It was the first sinking of a ship by anti-ship missiles. [established]
  - Nasser declared the War of Attrition formally on 8 March 1969. [established]
  - It ended with a US-brokered ceasefire under the **Rogers** initiative on 7-8 August 1970. [established]
  - (Wikipedia via mirror, "War of Attrition"; idf.il, "The War of Attrition")
  - The war's start date is given as July 1967 or March 1969, depending on the source. [disputed: framing]
- **Oil embargo.** The Arab states declared an embargo on oil to the US, UK and West Germany in June 1967. It had little effect and was lifted after Khartoum. [established] (FRUS XIX editorial note)

### The road to 1973 and the long-term consequences
- Nasser died in September 1970. Sadat's offers to reopen the canal in 1971 led nowhere. Egypt and Syria attacked on 6 October 1973 (Yom Kippur / October War) to break the post-1967 deadlock. [established]
- Interpretation: Israeli confidence after 1967 (the *conceptzia*) is widely blamed for the surprise of 1973. [established as the mainstream reading]
- **Long-term consequences** [established unless noted]:
  - Israeli rule over the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem and the Golan, and the settlement policy that followed. Settlement began in 1967-68 (e.g., Kfar Etzion, Merom Golan). The international community, through the UN and the ICJ advisory opinions of 2004 and 2024 [unverified wording], regards the territories as occupied and the settlements as unlawful. Israel disputes this, especially for Jerusalem and the Golan.
  - Israel annexed East Jerusalem in effect (1967; Basic Law 1980) and applied its law to the Golan (1981). Both moves are not internationally recognised, except for the US recognition of 2017-2019 [established; unverified: details].
  - The rise of the PLO under Fatah as an independent actor after Arab armies failed (the battle of Karameh, March 1968). [established]
  - The decline of Nasserist pan-Arabism, and a turn towards political Islam in parts of the region. [interpretation; widely held]
  - Closer US-Israel strategic ties, and deeper Soviet involvement with Egypt and Syria. [established] (FRUS XIX summary)
  - The Suez Canal stayed closed from June 1967 to June 1975. [established]
  - "Land for peace" as a principle: the 1979 Egypt-Israel peace treaty returned Sinai. [established]
  - Religious and national meanings attached to the Old City and the Western Wall for Israelis. The loss of Jerusalem and the West Bank is remembered as *an-Naksa* by Palestinians and Arabs (marked each year on 5 June). [established]

## 7. People and stories for narration

Each episode gives its source and tag. Quotations are only those found in sources this session, and translations vary.

1. **"The Temple Mount is in our hands" (7 June).**
   - Col. Motta Gur's radio call ("*Har HaBayit beyadeinu*"), the paratroopers at the Western Wall, and Rabbi Shlomo Goren blowing the shofar. A recording of Gur's call survives. [israeli; the event is established] (Jewish Virtual Library; Israel National News; NLI)
   - David Rubinger's photograph of three paratroopers at the Wall, taken on 7 June 1967. The men were Zion Karasenti, Yitzhak Yifat and Haim Oshri [unverified: names]. [established] (Wikipedia via mirror, "Paratroopers at the Western Wall")
2. **Ammunition Hill (6 June).** A night battle in the trenches lasting about four hours. Both sides honour the fighting. Use the figures of 36 Israeli and about 71 Jordanian dead to show the cost on both sides. Avoid the language of a single hero. [established]
3. **The Egyptian retreat across Sinai (6-9 June).**
   - Amer's order "within one night" reached units as one, two or three nights. Soldiers walked west through the desert towards the canal, many without water, and abandoned vehicles jammed the Mitla Pass. [arab: Fawzi memoir; established in outline] (Weapons and Warfare; Egypt Country Study; Magnum/UPI photographs)
   - A young officer is quoted: "It was a massacre, a disaster." [arab; single source, verify]
   - I could not verify the "barefoot" detail. Do not use it unless a source is found.
   - Atlantic Council, "Fifty years on, a defeat at Israel's hands haunts Egyptian military veterans", has veterans' testimony. [arab]
4. **Seventy-two hours in Jerusalem, Arab side.** Schleifer's eyewitness account of the fall of the Arab city (Institute for Palestine Studies). It covers the fewer-than-5,000 defenders, the lack of anti-aircraft guns and the civilian experience. [arab]
5. **The climb up the Golan (9 June).**
   - The 8th Armoured Brigade's bulldozers cut a way up the slope for 33 worn Shermans. Golani's fight at Tel Faher went bunker to bunker. Today the site is "Mitzpe Golani", with the names of 32 fallen. [israeli; established] (Tour Golan, "Mitzpe Golani (Tel Faher)"; Jewish Virtual Library)
   - Pair this with the Golan villagers who fled or were expelled. [arab/NGO] (Akevot; Palestine Studies, "Voices from the Golan")
6. **The radio that announced a fall too early (10 June).** Damascus radio announced that Quneitra had fallen hours before Israeli troops arrived. [established; motive disputed]
7. **Two leaders on the telephone (6 June).** The intercepted Nasser-Hussein call and the "Anglo-American air cover" claim. [established]
8. **The stammering speech (28 May).** Eshkol's halting broadcast and the crisis of confidence that brought Dayan into government. [established] (NLI blog)
9. **Nasser resigns, and the crowds say no (9-10 June).** [established; spontaneity disputed]
10. **USS Liberty (8 June).** 34 American dead. Present the agreed facts and both sides of the dispute. [established/disputed]
11. **Imwas, Yalu and Beit Nuba.** Three villages near Latrun emptied and razed. The ground became a park. Present the Israeli and Palestinian accounts. [established/disputed]
12. **The waiting reservists (May).** Israel's citizen army mobilised for three weeks. Parks were consecrated as possible mass graves [unverified: often told; check Segev or Oren before use].

## 10. Disagreements between sources, and what is not known

**Major interpretive disputes**
1. **Nasser's intent**: brinkmanship or a plan for war (Shlaim, Rabin's 1968 remark vs. Oren, Karsh, Morris). Egyptian archives are mostly closed (Sayigh).
2. **Pre-emptive vs. preventive** Israeli strike.
3. **Soviet motive** for the false troop report.
4. **Jordan's entry**: deception and obligation, or choice. Did Israel's message via Odd Bull reach Hussein in time, and in what words?
5. **Dayan's DMZ statements**: how representative, and how far Israel provoked the clashes on the Syrian border.
6. **USS Liberty**: deliberate or mistaken.
7. **Quneitra announcement**: purpose and responsibility (the charge against Assad is political, not proven).
8. **1967 refugees**: numbers (about 200,000 to over 300,000 from the West Bank and Gaza; 800,000+ in some Palestinian estimates) and the mix of flight and expulsion. The same questions apply to the Golan (about 90,000-137,000 before the war).
9. **Resolution 242**: "territories" or "the territories".
10. **Khartoum's "three no's"**: rejection or a bargaining position.
11. **Egyptian POW killings**: alleged by Israeli historian Yitzhaki and a 2007 documentary; denied by those named; never independently investigated.
12. **Amer's death**: suicide or poisoning.

**Numerical disagreements**
- Aircraft destroyed in the first wave: 186-197. On 5 June overall, about 450. Israeli aircraft lost: 19 on day one; 32-48 in the war.
- Arab casualties, especially Jordan: about 700 killed (Wikipedia/Mutawi) vs. 6,000 "casualties" (Britannica Kids).
- Israeli tanks lost: about 100 to 400 (depending on whether damaged tanks are counted).
- Tel Faher Israeli dead: 23, 31, 32 or 34. Ammunition Hill: 35-37 Israeli, about 70-71 Jordanian.
- Force sizes: Syrian strength (50,000-75,000), Egyptian total army (150,000-240,000).

**Small date and time variants**
- Soviet warning: 13 or 15 May.
- Tiran closure: 21, 22 or 23 May.
- Begin's speech: 2 or 8 August 1982.
- Rabin's *Le Monde* interview: 28 or 29 February 1968.
- Dayan's Golan order: about 06:00 or 07:00 on 9 June.
- Golan ceasefire: 18:00 or 18:30 on 10 June.
- Um Katef attack: 08:15 armour vs. midnight infantry (different phases).
- Bir Lahfan battle: night of 5-6 or 6-7 June.
- Start of the War of Attrition: July 1967 or March 1969.

**Not known, or not verified in this session**
- The names and roles of the Egyptian Sinai front commanders (Murtagi and others) were not confirmed by a source found.
- The exact list of Egyptian airfields hit in each wave.
- The precise Samu raid casualty figures (see UN S/7593 and the UNTSO report).
- The "barefoot soldiers" detail of the Sinai retreat.
- Whether Sharm el-Sheikh was "found abandoned".
- The Israeli explanation naming *El Quseir* in the Liberty case.
- The demolition of the Moroccan Quarter, and the post-war return figures for refugees.
- Coordinates for Um Katef, Sharm el-Sheikh, Tel Fakhr (derived only), Butmiya and Givat HaEm.
- Arab civilian deaths in the war (no reliable total found).
- Egyptian official archives for 1967 remain largely closed. Much of the Arab side rests on memoirs (Fawzi, Riad, Hussein, Shazly, el-Gamasy) and Israeli or Western intercepts and documents.
