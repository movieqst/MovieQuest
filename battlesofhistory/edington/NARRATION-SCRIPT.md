## **welcome**
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Edington, which happened in May 878, between the West Saxons of Alfred the Great and the Viking Great Army of Guthrum.

## **intro-1**: BATTLE OF EDINGTON
- By 878 the Viking Great Army had conquered Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia. Wessex was the last English kingdom standing.
- This introduction separates what the sources say from what historians have reconstructed. Watch for the coloured tags.

## **intro-2**: Surprise at Chippenham
- In January 878, just after Twelfth Night, Guthrum’s army fell on the royal estate at Chippenham in the middle of winter, when no one expected an attack.
- The Chronicle says the Vikings occupied Wessex, drove many people overseas and forced most of the rest to submit, all except King Alfred.
- Alfred escaped with a small band into the woods and the marshes of Somerset.

## **intro-3**: Athelney
- At Easter Alfred built a fort at Athelney, an island in the Somerset marshes, and raided the Vikings from it with the men of Somerset.
- The famous story of Alfred burning a woman’s cakes was first written down about a century later. It may be a legend.
- That winter another Viking force landed in Devon and was beaten at Countisbury, a sign that Wessex could still fight back.

## **intro-4**: The muster at Egbert’s Stone
- In the seventh week after Easter, in early May, Alfred rode to Egbert’s Stone, east of Selwood Forest.
- The men of Somerset, Wiltshire and the part of Hampshire west of the sea came to meet him there, and were glad to see him.
- Where Egbert’s Stone stood is not known for certain.

## **intro-5**: The two armies
- Both armies fought on foot in a shield wall: a packed line of men with round shields overlapping, spears in front and more men pushing behind.
- No source gives the size of either army. Modern guesses run to a few thousand men on each side.
- Most men carried a spear, a shield and a long knife. Only the richer men had mail, a helmet and a sword.

## **intro-6**: The sources
- The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Asser’s Life of King Alfred were both written within a few years of the battle, at Alfred’s court. They tell his side of the story.
- They say little about the fighting itself. Where the game shows more, it is a reconstruction.
- Ethandun is usually placed at Edington in Wiltshire, but historians still argue about the exact field.

## **event-1**: The march to Ethandun
From Egbert’s Stone the army marched one day to Iley, and the next morning on to Ethandun, where the Viking army was waiting.

**explain-1**: Gathering an army from three shires in one place, then marching at once, gave the Vikings little warning. The Chronicle gives the route; where Iley lay is not certain.

## **event-2**: The shield walls form
Both armies form their shield walls: overlapping round shields, spears levelled, the best men in the front rank.

**explain-2**: A shield wall was strong while it held together. Battles were usually decided when one side’s line gave way, not by single heroes.

## **event-3**: Missiles
Before the lines meet, arrows, spears and throwing axes fly across the gap.

**explain-3**: Throwing weapons and bows are known from the period. The sources do not describe them at Ethandun; this chapter is a reconstruction.

## **event-4**: The clash
Alfred’s army closes with the whole Viking army. Asser writes that he fought fiercely, with a dense shield wall.

**explain-4**: Asser’s Latin phrase is densa testudine, a close-packed wall of shields. It is the only detail of the fighting that a near-contemporary source gives.

## **event-5**: A long struggle
The two lines push, stab and hold. Asser says the fighting went on for a long time.

**explain-5**: In a shield-wall fight the men at the front were relieved by those behind. Exhaustion, losses and nerve decided when a line would break.

## **event-6**: The Viking line breaks
At last the Viking army gives way and flees. The West Saxons cut down many of them as they run.

**explain-6**: Most killing in battles of this kind happened after a line broke, as men fled. The number of dead is not recorded.

## **event-7**: Pursuit to the stronghold
Alfred pursues the survivors to their stronghold and takes the animals and men he finds outside it.

**explain-7**: The Chronicle says only “the fortress”. It is usually taken to be Chippenham, but some historians argue for a hill fort near the battlefield.

## **event-8**: The siege
For fourteen days the West Saxons besiege the stronghold. Hungry, cold and afraid, the Vikings ask for peace.

**explain-8**: The Vikings gave Alfred hostages and swore oaths, and promised to leave his kingdom. Unusually, they gave hostages without taking any in return.

## **event-9**: Peace and baptism
Three weeks later Guthrum and thirty of his chief men came to Alfred and were baptised, with Alfred as Guthrum’s godfather.

**explain-9**: Guthrum took the Christian name Æthelstan. His army left Wessex and settled in East Anglia, which he ruled as king until his death about 890.

## **outcome**
- Alfred’s army broke the Viking Great Army at Ethandun and besieged the survivors until they made peace.
- Guthrum was baptised with Alfred as his godfather, and his army left Wessex for East Anglia.
- Wessex survived, and Alfred’s kingdom became the core of the later kingdom of England.
- The size of the armies, the number of dead and the exact place of the battle are not known.
