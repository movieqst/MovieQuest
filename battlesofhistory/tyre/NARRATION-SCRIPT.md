# Siege of Tyre: narration script (DRAFT 2)

This is what the narrators will read.

**Sources:**
- Our TYRE-ROUND-2-REVISED.md (17 chapters, Parts F–H), with each chapter's **"Verified correction" paragraph taking priority** over the older bullets above it.
- TYRE-ROUND-2-CORRECTIONS.md (the Greek-text check of Arrian 2.15–25, Diodorus 17.40–46 and Plutarch *Alexander* 24–25), followed for sections, numbers and wording. Curtius Book IV comes from Part F of the revised dossier, where the Latin was opened directly.
- TYRE-MAP-UNCERTAINTIES.md for what the map can and cannot show.
- The ancient writers: **Arrian**, *Anabasis* Book II; **Diodorus** of Sicily, *Library* Book XVII; **Curtius** Rufus, *History of Alexander* Book IV; **Plutarch**, *Life of Alexander* 24–25. All four wrote centuries after 332 BCE, from earlier histories that are now lost.
- The geology: Brocard et al. 2024, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 324 (read only through our dossiers).

**The approach:**
- **One reenactment, about seven months,** from the start of the mole to the fall of the city in midsummer 332 BCE.
- **Arrian is the main track** for the order of events, as the map is. Where Diodorus tells a different story (the storm, the first breach, the death of Admetus), the script says "Diodorus tells it differently" and never blends the two.
- **Every detail from one writer only is named** (*Arrian*, *Diodorus*, *Curtius*, *Plutarch*). "Established" is used only where the writers agree, or where it is modern and secure.
- **The Tyrians** are given their reasons, their skill and their losses. The killing at the end is told plainly, by each author's numbers, without gore and without praise.
- **No army sizes.** No ancient writer gives a reliable count of Alexander's army at Tyre or of the city's defenders, and the script says so.

**Tags:**
- *(Arrian)*, *(Diodorus)*, *(Curtius)*, *(Plutarch)*: the detail comes from that writer only (or the writers named).
- **established**: the ancient writers agree, or it is secure modern knowledge.
- **disputed**: the writers disagree, or the story is doubtful.
- **estimate**: a number we round or convert (stades into metres, for example).
- **reconstruction**: our own placing of something no source pins down.
- **geology: Brocard 2024**: from the modern study of Tyre's coastline.

**Clocks.** No dates and no clock times. Arrian dates only the fall: the Athenian month of Hecatombaeon, about July–August 332 BCE. Every other chapter reads "Early in the siege", "Late in the siege", "Three days later" and so on. "Midday" for the Tyrian sortie is Arrian's own word. The season of the fleet's arrival is not given by any source, so it is not named.

## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Siege of Tyre, fought in 332 BCE on the coast of what is now
Lebanon. On one side was Alexander the Great, king of Macedon, with his army and, later, a fleet of ships from the cities
that had joined him. On the other was Tyre, a Phoenician city built on an island, with its walls, its harbours and its
fleet. The siege lasted about seven months.

## Introduction

### 1. SIEGE OF TYRE
- In 332 BCE, Alexander of Macedon tried to take the island city of Tyre. To reach it, his men built a road of stone and earth across the sea. The Tyrians fought back by sea, by fire and with machines of their own. After about seven months, the city fell. *(established)*
- That road across the sea is called a mole: a bank of stones and earth built out into the water. Sand later piled up against it. The island of Tyre is joined to the mainland to this day. *(established; geology: Brocard 2024)*

### 2. After Issus
- In late 333 BCE, Alexander defeated the Persian king, Darius the Third, at Issus, near today's border between Turkey and Syria. Then he marched south, down the coast of Phoenicia, the land of the great trading cities of the Levant. *(established)*
- One by one, most of the Phoenician cities came over to him. Aradus did, and Byblos. The people of Sidon invited him in. *(Arrian)*
- Tyre also sent envoys to meet him on the road. Among them was the son of the king. They said that Tyre would do whatever Alexander commanded. *(Arrian)*
- King Azemilcus himself was away, at sea with his ships in the Persian fleet. *(Arrian)*
- Alexander had a reason to want every port on this coast. The Persian fleet was mostly Phoenician and Cypriot ships. If the ports were his, Arrian has him say, that fleet would have nowhere to go, and the road to Egypt would be open. *(Arrian)*

### 3. The island city
- Tyre stood on a rocky island, a little way off the coast. Diodorus says four stades of sea lay between them: about seven hundred metres. *(Diodorus; the metres, estimate)*
- A modern study of the coast reconstructs the island as about one and a half kilometres long and six hundred metres wide. That is a best estimate, not a measured shoreline. *(estimate; reconstruction; geology: Brocard 2024)*
- High walls ran all round it. On the side facing the mainland, Arrian says they were about a hundred and fifty feet high, built of large stones set in mortar. *(Arrian)*
- The city had two harbours: one facing north, toward Sidon, and one facing south, toward Egypt. *(established)*
- Its oldest and holiest place was the temple of its god Melqart, whom the Greeks called Heracles. Arrian describes it as the oldest temple that people remembered. On the mainland opposite stood an older settlement, called Old Tyre. *(Arrian; Diodorus)*
- Tyre was a city of seafarers. Its colonists had founded Carthage, in North Africa. *(established)*
- Diodorus calls the Carthaginians the Tyrians' descendants, and the Tyrians hoped Carthage would help them. *(Diodorus)*

### 4. The refusal
- Alexander said he wished to enter Tyre and sacrifice to Heracles in his temple. *(Arrian; Curtius)*
- The Tyrians gave their answer. Arrian reports it like this: they would do anything else he asked, but they would let neither Persians nor Macedonians into their city, while the war was still undecided. *(Arrian)*
- Curtius adds an offer. There was a temple of Hercules on the mainland, at Old Tyre, the envoys said. Alexander could sacrifice there. *(Curtius)*
- The writers explain the refusal differently. In Arrian, the Tyrians say they want to stay out of a war between two great powers. Diodorus says the Tyrians wanted to please Darius, trusted in their island, and hoped for help from Carthage. *(disputed: Arrian; Diodorus)*
- Arrian says Alexander sent the envoys back in anger. *(Arrian)*
- Curtius alone adds that heralds sent to urge peace were killed and thrown into the sea. No other surviving writer tells this. *(Curtius; disputed)*

### 5. The two sides
- How many men Alexander had at Tyre, no ancient writer reliably says. Nor do they count the city's defenders. This page gives no army numbers. *(established)*
- What Alexander had was the army that had won at Issus. What he did not have was a fleet that could face Tyre's. *(Arrian)*
- Tyre had walls, engines, and, Diodorus says, eighty triremes. A trireme was a warship powered by three levels of rowers, with a bronze ram at the bow. It also had smaller boats that could carry archers, slingers and catapults. *(Diodorus)*
- The Tyrians voted to send their children, their women and their old people to Carthage. Diodorus says only a part of them got away before the siege closed in. Curtius tells a similar story. *(Diodorus; Curtius)*
- Diodorus says the Tyrians mocked Alexander. Did he think he could overcome Poseidon, the god of the sea? *(Diodorus)*

### 6. How this page tells it
- No one who was at Tyre left an account that survives. Our four main writers lived hundreds of years later: Diodorus of Sicily, in the first century BCE; Curtius, probably in the first century CE; Plutarch, around 100 CE; and Arrian, in the second century CE. They used older histories that are now lost. *(established)*
- They often disagree: about what happened, in what order, and how many died. When they do, this page tells you who says what. *(established)*
- After each line you will see a tag. A writer's name tells you which ancient account we are following. "Established" means the sources agree. "Disputed" means they do not. "Estimate" marks a rounded number, and "reconstruction" marks our own best placing on the map. *(established)*
- The map follows Arrian's order of events. The shape of the island comes from a modern study of the coast. The exact places of the walls, harbours and temple are not known: the map shows our best placing, and an area, not a point, where no one can be sure. *(reconstruction)*

## The reenactment (332 BCE)
All the chapters play on the siege map. The island's outline follows the modern reconstruction; the harbours, temple and walls are drawn as symbols, not surveyed lines. *(reconstruction)*

1. **The mole begins** · Early in the siege
   Alexander decides to build a wide causeway of stone and earth out to the island. Arrian says he dreamed that Heracles took him by the hand and led him into the city, and his seer, Aristander, said that Tyre would fall, but only after great labour. Plutarch tells the dream differently: Heracles held out his hand to him from the walls and called to him. His men start to build the mole out from the mainland, toward the city. Diodorus says they pulled down Old Tyre for its stones, and that people from the nearby towns were brought in to carry them. Arrian describes the water: shallow and muddy near the shore, and about three fathoms deep, a little over five metres, close to the city. Arrian explains that stakes drove easily into the mud, and the mud held the stones together. Diodorus gives the width of the mole as two plethra, about sixty metres. Near the shore, the work goes quickly. *(Arrian; Diodorus; Plutarch; the metres, estimate)*

2. **The sea fights back** · Early in the siege
   The further the mole reaches, the deeper the water and the closer the walls. Now the builders are within range. Arrian says the Tyrians still ruled the sea. From their triremes they row up along the sides of the mole and strike at the workers. From the walls, they shoot down on them. Many of the workers have little protection. Alexander answers with screens to shield them, and builds two towers at the head of the mole, covered with animal hides, with engines on top to shoot back. *(Arrian; Diodorus)*

3. **The fireship** · Early in the siege
   The Tyrians make a weapon of their own. They take an old horse transport, a wide ship, and fill it with dry wood, pitch and sulphur. On two masts they hang cauldrons of things that burn. They weigh down the stern, so that the bow rides high. With a wind blowing toward the mole, their triremes tow the ship in, and the men aboard set it alight and get away. The ship drives against the head of the mole, and both towers catch fire. Tyrian boats close in, tear up the stakes along the mole and burn the engines that the fire had missed. This is Arrian's story. Alexander orders the mole made wider, so that it can carry more towers, and the work begins again. *(Arrian)*

4. **The storm** · Early in the siege, in Diodorus
   Diodorus does not mention the fireship. Instead he describes a powerful north-west wind that smashed much of the mole. Alexander had whole trees cut in the mountains and brought down, branches and all, to rebuild it. Diodorus also tells of fear inside the city. Some Tyrians said that their god Apollo meant to leave them for Alexander, so they bound his statue with golden chains. Plutarch tells a similar story, but in his version they tied the statue with ropes and nailed it to its base. How the storm fits into Arrian's order of events, the surviving accounts do not tell us. *(Diodorus; Plutarch; disputed)*

5. **The fleet changes sides** · Middle of the siege
   Alexander goes to Sidon to gather ships. The kings of Aradus and Byblos hear that their cities are now in his hands. They leave the Persian fleet, and bring their ships to him: about eighty Phoenician ships, with the ships of Sidon. Ten come from Rhodes, three each from Soli and Mallus, in Asia Minor, and ten from Lycia. There is one Macedonian ship: a penteconter, a ship of fifty oars, under Proteas. Not long after, the kings of Cyprus sail in with about a hundred and twenty more. Arrian's numbers come to well over two hundred ships. Plutarch gives two hundred. Now Alexander, too, has a fleet. Many of its crews are Phoenician, like the Tyrians themselves. *(Arrian; Plutarch; the total, estimate)*

6. **Ten days in the mountains** · While the fleet was fitted out
   While the ships are made ready, Alexander leaves Sidon. He takes some squadrons of cavalry, the archers, the Agrianians, light troops from the hills north of Macedon, and the hypaspists, the foot guards who fought close to the king. He marches into the Antilebanon mountains, against the Arab peoples there. Some places he takes by force; others come to terms. Within ten days he is back in Sidon, where four thousand Greek mercenaries have arrived to join him. Plutarch, quoting an older writer named Chares, tells a story from this march. Alexander's old tutor, Lysimachus, could not keep up, and Alexander stayed behind with him, cut off from his army for a night, at great risk. It is Plutarch's story, written centuries after the event. *(Arrian; Plutarch)*

7. **The blockade** · After the fleet arrived
   Alexander sails for Tyre with his fleet in battle order. The Tyrians had meant to fight at sea. When they see how many ships are coming, they stay in harbour. They block both harbour mouths with their triremes. At the north harbour, the Phoenicians ram three of them, and the crews swim ashore. Then Alexander sets his watch. The Cypriots, under the admiral Andromachus, guard the harbour toward Sidon. The Phoenicians guard the harbour toward Egypt, on the far side of the mole, close to Alexander's own tent. Alexander now tries to cut the city off by land and by sea. *(Arrian)*

8. **Stones, divers and chains** · Middle of the siege
   Alexander now brings his engines by sea. He mounts them on horse transports and slow triremes and moves them up to the walls. But many stones lie in the water at the foot of the walls, and they keep the ships away. Who put them there, Arrian does not say. The Macedonians anchor and try to haul them out. Tyrian ships, shielded with protective covers, row in and cut the anchor ropes. Alexander guards his anchors with shielded boats of his own. Then Tyrian divers swim out under the water and cut the ropes again. At last the Macedonians use chains instead of ropes. From the mole, they loop ropes around the stones, drag them clear, and drop them into deep water. *(Arrian)*

9. **Machines against machines** · Late in the siege
   On the walls, the Tyrians answer engine with engine. Diodorus describes their devices. Spinning wheels, divided into sections, meant to knock aside the bolts and stones shot at them. Soft padding, and hides stuffed with seaweed, to soak up stones. Nets to snag the attackers, and hooked tridents on lines to drag them off the bridges. Long poles with blades to cut the ropes of the rams. And sand, heated red-hot in bronze shields and thrown down on the men below. Diodorus says it ran in under their armour. Curtius describes the hot sand too. Diodorus adds that the Tyrians built a second wall behind the first. Where it ran, no one knows. *(Diodorus; the hot sand, also Curtius)*

10. **The midday sortie** · Late in the siege, at midday
    The Tyrians plan a surprise. Behind sails stretched across the mouth of the north harbour, they man thirteen of their best ships. Three are quinqueremes and three are quadriremes, warships larger than triremes, with more rowers to each oar. Seven are triremes. At midday, when many of the sailors outside have left their ships for their daily needs, and Alexander has gone back to his tent, they row out quietly, in single file. They fall on the Cypriot ships at anchor. At the first charge they sink the quinquereme of King Pnytagoras of Salamis, in Cyprus. They also sink the ships of Androcles of Amathus and Pasicrates of Curium, and drive others onto the shore. But Alexander comes back sooner than usual. He rows round the island with his own ships. The Tyrians on the walls shout and signal to their ships to come back. Only a few make it back in time. Many of the Tyrian ships are disabled, and two are captured at the harbour mouth. Few of their men are killed: most swim back into the harbour. *(Arrian)*

11. **The first breach** · Late in the siege
    Now the rams go to work. On the side facing the mole, the wall is too strong for them to break. On the side toward Sidon, they fail too. On the side facing south, toward Egypt, they shake the wall until part of it falls. Alexander sends men across on bridges from the ships. The Tyrians drive them back without much trouble. Diodorus tells it differently. In his account, a stretch of wall about thirty metres long falls, *(estimate)* and the attackers burst in, but are thrown out, and the Tyrians rebuild the gap that same night. After this, he says, the mole reached the wall. *(Arrian; Diodorus)*

12. **The final assault** · Three days later
    Alexander waits for calm weather. Then he brings the engine ships back to the south wall, and the rams open a wide breach. Two ships carry bridges. On one, commanded by Admetus, are the hypaspists. On the other, the infantry battalion of Coenus. At the same time Alexander sends triremes against both harbours, and ships with archers and catapults circle the walls. The bridges go down onto the rubble. Admetus is the first man onto the wall. A spear strikes him, and he falls dead. Alexander follows him up with his own men, and they take the wall. Diodorus tells this part differently too. In his account, Admetus died earlier, from the blow of an axe, in an attack that failed. That night, he says, Alexander thought of giving up the siege. In his account the city was finally entered near its shipyards. Plutarch adds a story about the day itself. Aristander had promised that Tyre would fall within the month. Plutarch says that on the last day of the month, with the city still standing, Alexander ordered that day counted as only the twenty-eighth, and pressed the attack. *(Arrian; Diodorus; Plutarch)*

13. **The harbours are forced** · The same day
    At the south harbour, the one facing Egypt, the Phoenician squadron breaks through the booms, the floating barriers across its mouth. Inside, they ram the Tyrian ships afloat and drive others ashore. At the north harbour, which Arrian says had no boom at all, the Cypriots sail straight in, and enter the city from that side. The attackers now have several ways into the city: the south wall and both harbours. *(Arrian)*

14. **Inside the city** · The same day
    The Tyrians fall back from the walls. Many gather to make a stand at a shrine of Agenor, a hero of Tyre. Arrian says the Macedonians were enraged, by the long siege, and because the Tyrians had killed Macedonian prisoners on the walls, in sight of the camp, and thrown them into the sea. That is Arrian's explanation for their anger, not an excuse for it. Diodorus says the Tyrian fighters blocked their narrow streets and fought until almost all of them were cut down. Curtius says that sailors from Sidon, serving in Alexander's fleet, hid Tyrians aboard their ships and carried them to safety. *(Arrian; Diodorus; Curtius)*

15. **The sanctuary** · Hecatombaeon, July–August 332 BCE
    Some Tyrians reach the temple of Heracles, Melqart's temple. Among them are King Azemilcus, now back in his city, and the leading men of Tyre. With them are envoys from Carthage, who had come to honour Heracles, by an ancient custom, in their mother city. How or when the king came home from the Persian fleet, no source says. Arrian says Alexander spared those who had taken refuge in the temple. Arrian dates the fall of Tyre to the Athenian month of Hecatombaeon: about July or August of 332 BCE. *(Arrian)*

## The outcome
- Tyre fell after about seven months. Diodorus, Plutarch and Curtius give that length. Arrian dates the fall, but does not say how long the siege took. *(Diodorus; Plutarch; Curtius)*
- Each writer gives his own numbers for the Tyrians, and they cannot be added together. *(established)*
- Arrian says about eight thousand Tyrians were killed, and about thirty thousand were sold as slaves, Tyrians and foreigners caught in the city. *(Arrian)*
- Diodorus says more than seven thousand were killed in the street fighting. Afterwards, he says, Alexander had the young men, no fewer than two thousand, hung up and killed. The women and children were enslaved, and more than thirteen thousand captives were left. *(Diodorus)*
- Curtius says six thousand fighting men were killed in the city, and two thousand were crucified. He adds that fifteen thousand were saved, hidden by the Sidonians. That is his number for those rescued, not for all who survived. *(Curtius)*
- On Alexander's side, Arrian counts Admetus and twenty hypaspists killed in the final assault. Across the whole siege, he says, about four hundred Macedonians died. *(Arrian)*
- Then Alexander made his sacrifice to Heracles in the temple he had asked to enter at the start. His army marched past in armour, and his fleet sailed past in review. There were games and a torch race in honour of Heracles. In the temple he dedicated the engine that had broken the wall, and the sacred ship of Heracles that his fleet had captured. *(Arrian)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- Earlier, during the siege, Arrian says, envoys from Darius had come with an offer of peace. Alexander turned it down. *(Arrian)*
- Alexander marched south. Gaza resisted him too. After Gaza, he entered Egypt. *(established; Arrian)*
- Some of the people Tyre sent away before the siege reached Carthage. Diodorus is not consistent: in one place he says only a part of them got away, in another that most of the captives had been taken to Carthage. *(Diodorus; disputed)*
- Curtius says the Carthaginians had sent word that they could give no military help. How many reached safety, no one can say. *(Curtius; disputed)*
- Tyre did not disappear. It was resettled and rebuilt, and became a busy port again under later Greek kings and under Rome. Most of the ruins on the old island today are Roman and Byzantine. *(established)*
- The mole changed the coast for good. Sand carried along the shore piled up against it, and over the centuries the island became a peninsula. Diodorus says the city "became a peninsula" during the siege itself. Modern geologists say the sandy neck grew over centuries, and that a sandy bank under the water had helped the builders from the start. *(Diodorus; geology: Brocard 2024)*
- Today Tyre, called Sour in Arabic, is a city in southern Lebanon. Its ancient ruins were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. The town stands on the sand that grew against Alexander's mole. *(established)*
