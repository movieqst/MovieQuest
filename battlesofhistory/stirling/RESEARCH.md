## 1. Background

### Edward I's conquest of 1296
- Edward I invaded after King John Balliol renounced his homage; the **sack of Berwick** began on **30 March 1296** **[est]** (WP "Sack of Berwick"; MISC).
- Contemporary figures for townspeople killed at Berwick range from about **4,000 to 17,000** **[prim, disp, estim]** (as summarised by WP "Sack of Berwick"; the individual chronicles were not identified in this round).
- The **Battle of Dunbar** destroyed the Scottish feudal host and led to the English occupation of the Lowlands **[est]** (MISC great-castles; WP). The usual date, **27 April 1296**, did not appear in any search result **[unver]** (KNOW).
- John Balliol abdicated and was stripped of his royal insignia in July 1296 (the "Toom Tabard" story) **[unver for the detail; est for the abdication]** (KNOW).
- **The Ragman Roll.** Most oaths of fealty to Edward were sworn at a **parliament at Berwick on 28 August 1296** **[est]** (BOB; WP "Ragman Rolls"). The number of names is counted differently: about **1,500**, about **1,650**, or **1,870** **[disp]** (BOB; WP; clan genealogy sites).
- The names on the Roll include men who fought on both sides in 1297. A "Wallace" entry (not William) is often discussed **[unver]** (KNOW).

### English administration
- **John de Warenne, Earl of Surrey** (born c.1231) was made Edward's lieutenant/keeper (often called "Guardian") of Scotland **[est]** (WP; BRIT).
- **Hugh de Cressingham** was **Treasurer** of Scotland; **William de Ormesby** was **Justiciar** **[est]** (WP "Hugh de Cressingham"; MISC "Raid on Scone").
- Surrey spent much of the time from late 1296 to summer 1297 in the north of England, saying the Scottish climate was bad for his health **[prim, unver]** (said to be Guisborough; KNOW).
- Cressingham was hated in Scotland for heavy taxation and was also tight-fisted with royal money (see 1297 letters below) **[est]** (WP; DNB).

### The risings of 1297
- **Lanark, May 1297:** Wallace killed **William Heselrig (Hazelrig), sheriff of Lanark** **[est]** (WP "Action at Lanark"; MISC). One source gives **3 May** **[unver]**.
  - The motive given in Blind Harry (revenge for the death of Wallace's wife or lover) is **[later]**, Blind Harry c.1470s (WP; MISC).
- **Scone, late May or June 1297:** Wallace and **Sir William Douglas** attacked the Justiciar Ormesby at Scone; Ormesby was warned and fled **[est]** (MISC "Raid on Scone"; WP). Dates differ **[disp]**.
- **Irvine, July 1297:** the noble rising (Robert Bruce, the Steward, Bishop Wishart and others) ended in the **Capitulation of Irvine**, usually dated **7–9 July 1297** **[est for event; disp for date]** (WP "Capitulation of Irvine"; irvinescotland.info). Wallace and Moray did not submit **[est]** (WP).
- **Moray in the north:** **Andrew Moray** (son of Sir Andrew Moray of Petty, who was a prisoner in England after Dunbar) escaped from captivity in Chester and raised the north, attacking **Urquhart Castle** **[est for the rising; unver for the Chester escape]** (WP "Andrew de Rait"; avoch.org; KNOW for Chester). By August he held most of the north-east (Inverness, Elgin, Banff, Aberdeen area) **[unver]** (KNOW).
- **Cressingham's letters, July–August 1297** **[prim]**: a letter to Edward dated **23 July 1297** reports Wallace "with a great company" in **Selkirk Forest** (DNB; POMS). On **5 August** Cressingham told Edward to discount a report from the north as "false in many points and obscure" (search summary of a modern account). These letters are printed in Stevenson, *Documents Illustrative of the History of Scotland* vol. ii (1870) **[unver]** (KNOW).
- One letter is said to report that Cressingham had a muster of **300 horse and 10,000 foot** at Roxburgh that was sent away as not needed, so the army at Stirling was smaller than it could have been **[prim, disp]** (Cressingham's letter as cited by Armstrong, via search summary; details unver).
- By late August Wallace (from the south) and Moray (from the north) had joined forces near **Stirling**, at the main crossing of the Forth between the south and the north of Scotland **[est]** (HES; WP).

### The Steward and Lennox negotiations
- When the English army reached Stirling, the Steward, Lennox and others asked Surrey to wait while they tried to bring the Scots to terms **[prim]** (Guisborough, as quoted by HES).
- They are said to have promised to come back with about **40 horsemen**; they returned with only a few men **[prim, disp]** (Warfare History Network, MISC; the number not confirmed in HES snippet).
- In the battle they waited with their men hidden in **woods near "the Pows"** (the Pow burns, low ground east of the bridge), then **killed fleeing English and took the baggage train** **[prim]** (Guisborough, as quoted by HES; also MISC).
- One modern account says they only watched and withdrew **[disp]** (MISC).

### Troop types
- **Scots:** mostly **spearmen** on foot, from Moray's northern following and Wallace's men of the south and Selkirk Forest **[est]** (HES; SMITH). Some **armoured knights and light horsemen** **[est]** (HES), and some **archers** **[est]** (HES). The dense spear formation is called the **schiltrom** **[est]** (WP; MISC), though at Stirling Bridge the Scots attacked rather than stood **[est]** (HES).
- **English:** **mounted knights and men-at-arms** (heavy cavalry) and **foot soldiers**, including **Welsh** infantry and archers, and **crossbowmen from the Stirling Castle garrison** **[est for cavalry and foot; unver for crossbowmen]** (HES; MISC).
- **Welsh:** Guisborough says the Welsh and other foot crossed early in the morning and were called back **[prim, unver]** (KNOW; see §4). HES lists **300 Welshmen** among the dead **[estim]** (HES snippet).

## 3. The battlefield

**The ground in one paragraph.** Stirling Castle sits on its crag on the **south** side of the Forth. Below it the river winds in tight **loops** across flat, wet carse land. The English came from the south, through Stirling. Across the river, a **causeway** about a mile long ran over the soft ground from the **north end of the bridge** to **Causewayhead**, at the foot of the **Abbey Craig**. The Scots waited on the high ground at the north end of the causeway **[est]** (HES; WP; BT). The ground on either side of the causeway was boggy and partly tidal; the loops of the Forth hemmed in the English bridgehead **[est]** (HES; MED19).

### The old wooden bridge
- The present Old Bridge was built in the **1400s or 1500s**, replacing a series of timber bridges **[est]** (HES-OB).
- **Archaeology:** two masonry piers reported in **1905** were relocated and surveyed, and **two more found by probing** under a sandbank; all **four lie on one straight line diagonally across the Forth**, just upstream of the Old Bridge **[est]** (PAGE; CAN). They are **stone-reinforced piers of a multi-span wooden bridge** **[est]** (HES scheduling text via search).
- Attempts to date one pier failed **[est]** (PAGE abstract). The link to **1297** is therefore an **inference**: Canmore says the piers "probably" belong to the bridge destroyed after the battle **[disp]** (CAN).
- The **burgh seal of Stirling** (recorded 1296) shows a bridge with **eight piers**; Page argues the four found are among those eight **[prim for the seal; disp for the match]** (PAGE; CAN).
- Survey dates differ: early 1990s (Canmore) or 1996–97 with sonar (scotlandsoldestbridges.co.uk) **[disp]**.
- The bridge was so narrow that **two horsemen could only just cross side by side** **[prim]** (Guisborough, quoted by HES).
- **Location debate.** In the 19th and early 20th centuries some placed the bridge at **Kildean**; in **1906 Cook** proposed the present favoured site, "the only location at Stirling for which there is documentary or archaeological support" **[est]** (HES via search; CAN). A Canmore note argues it would be "extravagant" to suppose a second bridge at Kildean **[est]** (CAN). A modern lecture calls Kildean "a myth" **[est]** (search result title).

### The causeway, the Craig and the Scottish position
- Fighting took place on and beside an **earthen causeway** from the Abbey Craig to the north end of the bridge **[est]** (WP; HES).
- Traditional view: the Scots were on the **Abbey Craig** **[est]** (HES). Alternative: they were a little west on **Spittal Hill**, where Murray Cook has found the line of the road leading to the causeway **[disp]** (WP snippet; only one secondary source seen).
- Recent digs: 2012 excavations at Bridgehaugh produced pottery put at the **13th/14th century** **[est]** (CAN). A later dig at the University of Stirling looking for the medieval road mainly found a road abandoned around **1750** **[est]** (inkl.com news summary).

### Tide
- **Murray Cook's theory:** the Forth's tidal range was **1–2 m higher** in the Middle Ages, based on the excavated **water gate/wharf at Cambuskenneth Abbey** (GUARD Archaeology), so much more of the battlefield flooded at high tide **[disp]** (MED19). He suggests the English formed up where the rear ranks stood in rising water and pushed forward, and that the river could have risen **"four metres"** behind them **[disp]** (SO21; press coverage via MED19).
- No contemporary source mentions the tide **[est]** (SO21 summary).

### The ford
- **Lundie** offered to take a force over a ford upstream and come behind the Scots **[prim]** (Guisborough via WP; HN; HES).
- The ford was **about two miles upstream** **[prim, estim]** (BT; MISC) and wide enough for **sixty horsemen abreast** **[prim, unver]** (WP snippet; Guisborough). HES adds that Lundie wanted **500 horse and some foot** **[unver]** (KNOW; not in any snippet).
- **Which ford?** Kildean is the traditional candidate; the distance fits Drip or somewhere between **[disp]** (CAN; KNOW). HES notes only a **post-medieval** ford is known at Kildean **[est]** (HES via search).

## 4. The battle in sequence

**Calendar and light.** 11 September 1297 (Julian) = **18 September** in the modern (Gregorian) calendar **[est]** (calendar arithmetic: 7-day difference in the 13th century). At Stirling that gives sunrise about **06:00** and sunset about **18:15** in GMT (solar time a few minutes later) **[estim]** (computed; not from a source).

Notes on individual episodes:
- **Surrey oversleeping and the knightings.** Widely repeated from Guisborough but **no search result confirmed** it **[unver]** (KNOW). Must check the text.
- **Bridge collapse trap.** Blind Harry says a carpenter ("John the Wright") hid in a cradle under the bridge and pulled a **roller pin** at Wallace's horn signal, collapsing it **[later]** — "highly improbable" (MM; WP "The Wallace"). The contemporary sources say the Scots seized the bridge end, and the English broke the bridge afterwards **[est]**.
- **Cressingham's skin.** The Lanercost Chronicle says Wallace had a **broad strip** made into a **baldric (sword belt)** **[prim]** (Lanercost via WP). Scalacronica says the Scots flayed him and **made thongs** of his skin "in token of their hatred" without naming Wallace **[prim]** (Scalacronica via WP). Other versions say **horse girths** **[later]** (MISC). One older account sees "no good foundation" for the story **[disp]** (MISC). For an educational site: treat as a hostile English story that may have a basis.
- **Scalacronica** was written by **Sir Thomas Gray** (1350s–60s); his father, also Thomas Gray, is said to have been at Stirling Bridge and left for dead **[unver]** (KNOW).

## 5. Casualties and aftermath

### Losses
- English: **100 cavalry and 5,000 infantry** killed **[prim]** (Guisborough via WP; MISC). HES gives "roughly **100** men-at-arms and about **5,000** foot, including **300 Welshmen**" **[estim]** (HES snippet). Armstrong calls Guisborough's figures a "wild exaggeration" **[disp]** (via search summary).
- Scots: no reliable figure **[est]**. Few notable dead other than Moray's wound **[unver]** (KNOW).

### Andrew Moray
- **Mortally wounded** at Stirling Bridge **[est]** (WP "Andrew Moray"; avoch.org).
- **Death date unknown**; usually given as **by November 1297** **[disp]** (WP; avoch.org).
- The **Lübeck letter** of **11 October 1297**, sent from **Haddington** to the merchants of **Lübeck and Hamburg**, is issued in the names of "**Andrew de Moray and William Wallace, leaders of the army of the kingdom of Scotland**" **[prim]** (SAS). Some argue this proves Moray was alive; others that his name was kept for authority **[disp]** (WP talk page; avoch.org). Haddington is [unver] (KNOW).
- A letter of protection to **Hexham Priory** dated **7 November 1297** also bears both names **[prim]** (HEX). Whether it was a protection is [unver].
- His burial place is unknown; Avoch or Fortrose (Ross) is local tradition **[later, unver]** (avoch.org; KNOW).

### Wallace as Guardian
- Wallace was **knighted** and became **Guardian of Scotland** in the name of King John Balliol **[est]** (BRIT; WP).
- When and where: late 1297 or early 1298 **[disp]**. A tradition puts the knighting at the **Kirk o' the Forest, Selkirk** **[later, unver]** (KNOW). A charter of **29 March 1298 at Torphichen** styles him Guardian **[prim, unver]** (KNOW).

### The raid into northern England
- From about **October to November 1297** the Scots ravaged **Northumberland and Cumberland** for several weeks **[est]** (MCN; MISC).
- They reached the outskirts of **Newcastle**, where refugees sheltered **[est]** (MCN summary); burned **Alnwick** town **[disp]**; and pillaged as far as **Cockermouth** **[est]** (MCN summary; aubreyresearch).
- **Hexham Priory** was plundered; canons said to have been forced to sing mass at spear-point **[prim, disp]** (Hexham/Guisborough story via search). A historian quoted in 2024 says there is **no evidence Wallace himself was there** **[disp]** (pressreader 2024).
- **Stirling Castle** surrendered to the Scots after the battle; Thweng was captured there **[unver]** (KNOW).

### Falkirk, 1298
- **22 July 1298:** Edward I in person defeated Wallace at **Falkirk**; English and Welsh archers broke the schiltroms **[est]** (BRIT; WP).
- Wallace resigned as Guardian afterwards, **by December 1298** **[est for resignation; disp for date]** (BRIT; WP).
- Wallace was captured in **1305** and executed in London on **23 August 1305** **[est]** (BRIT; KNOW for the day).

## 6. Weapons, armour, clothing, heraldry (for the artists)

**Golden rule:** dress everyone as late-13th-century northern Europeans. Scots and English looked very much alike; the difference was **wealth** (how much metal) and **heraldry**, not national costume.

### Scots, 1297
- **Spearmen:** long spears (often given as about **12 ft / 3.7 m** or more) **[estim, unver]** (KNOW; MISC); a small round **targe** or none; a **sword or long knife** (dagger) for some **[unver]** (KNOW).
- **Body:** padded **aketon/gambeson** (quilted jack) for the better-off; ordinary men in **linen shirt, wool tunic to the knee, hose, hood, cloak**; **iron cap** or **kettle hat** for some **[unver]** (KNOW; general period knowledge).
- **Knights and lords** (Moray, a few others): **mail hauberk, mail chausses, surcoat with arms, great helm or iron cap, heater shield, lance, sword** **[unver]** (KNOW).
- **Archers:** Scots archers are recorded at Falkirk (Selkirk Forest bowmen); at Stirling Bridge, "some archers" **[est]** (HES). Short or ordinary self bows **[unver]**.

### English, 1297
- **Knights/men-at-arms:** **mail** from head to foot; perhaps **coat of plates** under the surcoat for a few **[unver]**; **great helm** (flat or sugar-loaf top) or **cervellière/kettle hat**; heater **shield** painted with arms; **surcoat** in heraldic colours; horses with cloth **trappers** for the richest; **lance, sword, mace** **[unver]** (KNOW; general period knowledge).
- **English foot:** spears, bows, crossbows; padded jacks, iron caps **[unver]** (KNOW).
- **Welsh:** light infantry with **spears and bows**; lightly dressed; often shown barefoot or in rough cloth **[unver]** (KNOW; Gerald of Wales's 12th-c. description).
- **Crossbowmen** of the garrison **[unver]** (MISC).

### Anachronisms to avoid
- **Kilts** — the belted plaid is 16th-century; the small kilt is 18th-century **[est]** (KNOW). No tartan.
- **Blue face paint (woad)** — *Braveheart* invention **[est]**.
- **No bridge in *Braveheart*'s Stirling battle**; the film is not a guide to anything here **[est]**.
- **Full plate armour** — later (14th–15th c.) **[est]**.
- **Claymores** (two-handed Highland swords) — later **[est]**.
- **Massed English longbow volleys** — that is Falkirk 1298 and later; at Stirling Bridge the archers could not be brought to bear **[est]** (HES).
- Wallace as a giant Highlander — he was a Lowlander, probably from the west **[est]**.

## 7. Episodes for narration

1. **"Two horsemen side by side."** An army trying to squeeze across a bridge built for carts. [prim] Guisborough (HES).
2. **The friars and the reply.** "We have not come here to make peace but to do battle." [prim] Guisborough; wording [disp].
3. **The road not taken.** Lundie offers to take horsemen over the wide ford and come behind the Scots; Cressingham wants it done quickly and cheaply. [prim] Guisborough (WP; HN).
4. **The earl who overslept.** Men cross at dawn and are called back because Surrey is asleep; then knightings by the bridge. [prim, unver].
5. **"As many as we can overcome."** The Scots wait on the hill, watching the column, and choose their moment. [prim] Hemingburgh/Guisborough (search).
6. **Thweng's escape.** A Yorkshire knight cuts his way back across the bridge against the tide of men. [est] (DNB).
7. **The broken bridge.** Surrey breaks the bridge behind him and rides for Berwick. [est]
8. **Cressingham's skin.** A grim story told by English chroniclers. [prim] Lanercost, Scalacronica; [disp] as fact. Handle with care for a young audience.
9. **Two men changing sides.** The Steward and Lennox hide in the woods by the Pows, then fall on the fleeing English and the baggage. [prim] Guisborough (HES).
10. **The price of victory.** Moray, the co-commander, dies of his wounds within weeks; his name still heads the letter to Lübeck. [est]; [disp] on timing.
11. **The tide (modern theory).** Rising water behind the English rear. [disp] (MED19; SO21). Present as "one archaeologist thinks".
12. **The legend of the pin.** Blind Harry's carpenter pulling the pin to collapse the bridge. [later] — present as legend.

## 10. Disagreements and unknowns

1. **Army sizes.** No contemporary muster for either army. Modern figures range from ~2,500 to ~18,000 English and ~5,000 to ~8,000 Scots. The "9,000 v. 5,300–6,300" set traces to a 2014 *BBC History Magazine* piece, not checked.
2. **English losses.** Guisborough's 100 horse and 5,000 foot may be inflated (Armstrong).
3. **Exact bridge position.** The four-pier line just upstream of the Old Bridge is favoured, but the 1297 date of the piers is inferred, not proven. "180 yards upstream" (WP) versus "just upstream" (Canmore).
4. **Kildean.** Old rival site for the bridge; now generally rejected.
5. **Which ford** Lundie meant: Kildean, Drip, or elsewhere ("two miles upstream").
6. **The Scottish position.** Abbey Craig (traditional) or Spittal Hill (Cook).
7. **Role of the tide.** Cook's theory, from Cambuskenneth wharf evidence; not in any chronicle.
8. **Surrey oversleeping and the early recall.** In Guisborough by repute; not checked.
9. **Wallace's reply.** Exact words differ between translations.
10. **The bridge broken or burnt**, and by whom (Surrey's order is the usual reading). Blind Harry's collapse trap is legend.
11. **Thweng's nephew**: escaped with him (DNB) or killed (older history).
12. **Cressingham's flaying**: told by hostile English chroniclers in different forms (baldric, thongs, horse girths).
13. **Steward and Lennox**: attacked the fugitives, or only watched? How many men?
14. **Moray's death date**: after 11 October or 7 November 1297, or earlier with his name used afterwards.
15. **When and where Wallace was knighted and made Guardian.**
16. **Whether Wallace was personally at the sack of Hexham.**
17. **Time of the Scottish attack.** No firm clock time in the sources seen.
18. **Arms of Moray, Wallace, Thweng, Lennox, the Steward** — all from memory, unchecked.
