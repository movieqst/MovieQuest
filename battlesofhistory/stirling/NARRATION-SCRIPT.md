## Welcome
Welcome to Battles of History. Today we are learning about the Battle of Stirling Bridge, fought on the 11th of September
1297, between the Scots under William Wallace and Andrew Moray and the English army of King Edward the First.

## Introduction

### 1. BATTLE OF STIRLING BRIDGE
- On the 11th of September 1297, a Scottish army waited on the high ground above the River Forth while an English army crossed a narrow wooden bridge towards it. *(established)*
- Most of what we know comes from English chroniclers, who wrote within a few years and did not like the Scots. This introduction separates what they say from what historians have pieced together. Watch the colour of each tag. *(reconstruction)*

### 2. A kingdom conquered
- In 1296 King Edward the First of England invaded Scotland. His army sacked the town of Berwick and destroyed the Scottish army at Dunbar. *(established)*
- The Scottish king, John Balliol, gave up his throne. Edward took the Stone of Destiny from Scone, where Scottish kings were crowned, and made the Scottish lords swear loyalty to him. *(established)*
- He left Scotland in the hands of his officials: the Earl of Surrey as his lieutenant, and Hugh de Cressingham as treasurer, who was hated for his taxes. *(established)*

### 3. The risings of 1297
- In May 1297 William Wallace, a man from a lesser Scottish landholding family, was reported to have killed the English sheriff of Lanark. His rebellion spread through southern Scotland. *(established; his family and the details, disputed)*
- In the north, Andrew Moray, heir to a great family of the Moray Firth, returned from English captivity and led a growing rebellion. His forces attacked English garrisons and threatened their hold on the north. *(established)*
- By the end of the summer the two had joined forces near Stirling. *(established)*

### 4. The key to Scotland
- Stirling Castle stands on a rock above the River Forth. Below it, the river winds in great loops across soft, wet ground. *(established)*
- Here was the lowest bridge over the Forth, the main road between the south of Scotland and the north. Whoever held it held the way into the north. *(established)*
- Across the river, a narrow causeway ran over the marshy meadows to the foot of a steep hill, the Abbey Craig. The Scots waited there. *(established; the exact Scottish position, disputed)*

### 5. The armies
- No one counted either army. Guisborough says the English had fifty thousand foot, and the Scots forty thousand. Historians agree these figures are impossible. *(Guisborough; disputed)*
- We do not know the exact strength of either army. Modern historians suggest several thousand men on each side. The English had far more heavily armoured cavalry, with Welsh and English foot soldiers; the Scots relied mainly on spearmen on foot. *(estimate)*
- The Scots were led together by Wallace and Moray. The English were commanded by the Earl of Surrey, an old soldier who had won at Dunbar the year before. *(established)*

### 6. The bridge
- The bridge of 1297 was made of timber, so narrow that only two horsemen could ride side by side. *(Guisborough)*
- Archaeologists have found the remains of an earlier medieval bridge just upstream of today's stone Old Bridge: four stone piers still lie in the river. This is thought to be the site of the 1297 crossing, although the remains have not been securely dated to the battle. *(established; the date, disputed)*
- The battle map is painted onto the real course of the river, traced from the ground. Where the armies stood on it is our reconstruction. *(reconstruction)*

## The reenactment (11 September 1297)
The first two chapters play on the Scotland map; the rest play on the battle map.

1. **The English march north** · Late August 1297
   Surrey gathers his army in the Borders and marches north through Edinburgh to Stirling, where the English hold the castle. The Scots are already waiting across the river. *(established; the route, reconstruction)*

2. **Wallace and Moray** · Early September
   Wallace comes from the south and Moray from the north. They join their forces at the crossing of the Forth, and take up a position on the high ground near the Abbey Craig, looking down on the bridge. *(established)*

3. **Waiting** · 10 September
   Two Scottish lords serving with the English, James the Steward and the Earl of Lennox, ask Surrey to wait while they try to talk the Scots into surrender. They come back with nothing. *(Guisborough)*

4. **A false start** · 11 September, dawn
   Guisborough tells a strange story: thousands of Welsh foot soldiers crossed early in the morning, only to be called back because Surrey had not yet risen. Whether this really happened is uncertain; it may have been told to make Surrey look foolish. He also describes Surrey knighting young men before the battle. *(Guisborough; disputed)*

5. **The friars** · Morning
   Surrey sends two friars across to offer the Scots peace. According to Guisborough, Wallace sends back his answer: the Scots have not come to make peace, but to fight, and free their kingdom. *(Guisborough: the words are his, not a record)*

6. **Lundie's advice** · Morning
   Sir Richard Lundie, a Scottish knight serving the English, warns against forcing the bridge. According to Guisborough, he offers to take horsemen and foot across a nearby ford to come at the Scots from behind. Where that ford was is unknown. Cressingham, impatient at the cost of the campaign, urges an attack straight over the bridge, and Surrey agrees. *(Guisborough; the ford's place, unknown)*

7. **Two by two** · Later that morning
   The English begin to cross, two horsemen abreast. Cressingham is among those who cross. On the far bank they gather beside the causeway, moving aside to leave the crossing open for those behind, with marshy meadows all around. *(Guisborough; where they formed up, reconstruction)*

8. **The Scots attack** · Later that morning
   The Scots watch and wait. When a large part of the English army is across and cut off from the rest by the river, they come down from the high ground and block the way back to the bridge. Those who have crossed are trapped. *(Guisborough; the exact moves, reconstruction)*

9. **The trap** · During the fighting
   On the far bank the English horsemen have no room to charge on the soft ground. Many are killed. Others try to swim the Forth in their armour and drown. *(Guisborough; the ground, reconstruction)*

10. **Cressingham** · During the fighting
    Hugh de Cressingham, the hated treasurer, is killed in the fighting. English chroniclers later wrote that the Scots stripped skin from his body as an insult to their defeated enemy. *(established for his death; the details, chronicle and disputed)*

11. **Thweng** · During the fighting
    Sir Marmaduke Thweng, a Yorkshire knight, cuts his way back across the bridge, one of the very few to escape. Surrey makes him keeper of Stirling Castle. *(Guisborough)*

12. **The bridge** · After the fighting
    The bridge can no longer be crossed. Accounts disagree about how: Guisborough says Surrey ordered it destroyed as he retreated; the later Scalacronica says the Scots broke it. We cannot be certain which is right. *(disputed)*

13. **The rout** · After the fighting
    Surrey rides south for Berwick with the rest of his army. The Steward and the Earl of Lennox, who have been waiting in the woods, now turn on the fleeing English and seize their baggage. *(Guisborough)*

## The outcome
- The Scots won a complete victory. Guisborough says a hundred English knights and five thousand foot soldiers died, figures that may be too high. No one recorded the Scottish losses. *(Guisborough; estimate)*
- Andrew Moray was wounded at Stirling Bridge and later died, probably in November 1297. His name appears before Wallace's on an official letter of the 11th of October, so his leadership was still recognised after the victory. *(established; the date of his death, disputed)*

## The aftermath (debrief)
- On the 11th of October, at Haddington, Moray and Wallace wrote to the merchants of Lübeck and Hamburg that their ships could once again trade safely in Scottish ports. The letter survives. *(established)*
- The Scots raided deep into the north of England. Wallace was knighted and named Guardian of Scotland, ruling in the name of the absent King John. *(established; the dates, disputed)*
- Edward the First came north himself. On the 22nd of July 1298 he defeated Wallace at Falkirk. *(established)*
- Stirling Bridge showed how careful use of ground and timing could defeat a powerful army. Seventeen years later, at Bannockburn, a few miles away, Scottish infantry would win another great victory against an English army. *(established)*
- Blind Harry's poem of about 1470 tells of a carpenter who pulled a pin to collapse the bridge. It is a legend; no early source mentions it. *(later account)*
