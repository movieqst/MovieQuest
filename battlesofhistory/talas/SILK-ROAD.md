## What "the Silk Road" was
- **The name is modern.** Ferdinand von Richthofen coined *Seidenstraße* ("Silk Road") in 1877. Nobody in 751 called it that. *(established)*
- **It was a network, not one road.**
  - It was a web of caravan routes from China to the Mediterranean, joined to sea routes through the Indian Ocean.
  - Most goods went short distances, passed from market to market. Few traders ever made the whole journey. *(established; Hansen 2012, Whitfield 2019)*
- **It carried more than silk:**
  - Ferghana horses, silver, glass, paper, spices, slaves, furs and jade.
  - Ideas and religions too: Buddhism, Manichaeism, the Church of the East (Nestorian Christianity) and Zoroastrianism. Islam came later. *(established)*
- **The Sogdians were its great merchants.** They came from Samarkand and Bukhara, between the two empires that fought at Talas. Sogdian communities lived all along the route, as far as Chang'an. *(established; de la Vaissière 2005)*
- **Silk was money.** The Tang paid their garrisons in the Tarim Basin partly in bolts of silk. The documents found at Turfan and Dunhuang show this. *(established; Hansen 2012)*

## The map in 751
- **Tang China** held the Hexi Corridor and the oases of the Tarim Basin.
  - The **Protectorate of Anxi** at **Kucha** ruled the "Four Garrisons": Kucha, Khotan, Kashgar and Karashahr. At some times Suyab was counted instead.
  - The **Protectorate of Beiting** ruled north of the Tian Shan.
  - The Tang capital, **Chang'an**, was perhaps the largest city in the world. Its Western Market was full of Sogdian and Persian traders. *(established)*
- **The Abbasid Caliphate** took power from the Umayyads in **750**, a year before Talas. Its armies in the east were based at **Merv** in Khurasan.
  - **Baghdad did not exist yet.** It was founded in 762, so the map shows **Kufa**, the first Abbasid seat. *(established)*
- **The Tibetan Empire** fought the Tang for the Tarim and the Pamir passes. In 747 **Gao Xianzhi**, the Tang commander at Talas, led an army over the Pamirs to Gilgit ("Little Balur") to cut Tibet off from the west. *(established)*
- **The steppe:**
  - The **Türgesh** khaganate had broken up in the 740s.
  - The **Karluks** held the land north of the Tian Shan. Their turn against the Tang decided Talas.
  - The **Uyghur Khaganate** rose in Mongolia in 744.
  - The **Khazars** ruled the steppe north of the Caspian. *(established)*
- **The Byzantine Empire** held Constantinople and Anatolia. Syria, with Antioch and Damascus, had been Muslim since the 630s. *(established)*

## Where Talas sits
- **Talas** (modern Taraz, Kazakhstan) lies on the **northern route**: Turfan, Beiting, Suyab, Talas, Chach (Tashkent), Samarkand.
  - This route runs along the north side of the Tian Shan, through the grasslands where nomads and oasis towns met. *(established)*
  - The UNESCO World Heritage listing "Silk Roads: the Routes Network of the Chang'an–Tianshan Corridor" (2014) includes sites in this valley and at Suyab (Ak-Beshim). *(established)*
- **The battle was on the frontier.** It was fought in July 751, about halfway between Chang'an and the Mediterranean, at the point where the Tang and Abbasid spheres met. *(established)*

## After Talas
- **The Tang pulled back for a different reason.** They did not leave Central Asia because of Talas. The **An Lushan rebellion** of 755 pulled their armies home.
  - Tibet then took the Hexi Corridor; Dunhuang fell around 786.
  - The Tang never returned west of the Pamirs. *(established)*
- **Du Huan.** A Tang officer taken prisoner at Talas, he travelled through the Caliphate. He came home **by sea to Guangzhou in 762** and wrote an account of what he saw, the *Jingxingji*. Fragments survive. *(established)*
- **The paper story:** that Chinese prisoners from Talas brought papermaking to Samarkand.
  - It comes from an 11th-century writer, al-Tha'ālibī. *(later account)*
  - Paper was already known in Central Asia before 751. *(disputed)*
  - Narrate it as "the story goes".

## Map choices (and why)
- **Routes:**
  - The main routes are the ones every atlas of the period shows.
  - The orange branches are the main known passes and side routes.
  - The dashed lines are lesser or seasonal routes: Gilgit, Khwarazm to the Khazars, Lanzhou to Lhasa.
  - Lines join city to city in straight segments, so they show **connections, not exact tracks**.
- **City names** are those of 751, with the modern name after a slash where it helps. Coordinates are the modern sites.
- **The powers are labels only, with no borders.** In 751 the frontiers moved year by year, and much of the land between them was nobody's.
