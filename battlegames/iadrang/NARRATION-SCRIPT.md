## intro-1.mp3
*Intro screen 1: BATTLE OF THE IA DRANG*

BATTLE OF THE IA DRANG. 14–18 November 1965 , Central Highlands, South Vietnam. The first battle between the United States Army and the regular army of North Vietnam: three days in a clearing below a mountain, and one more on the trail to the next clearing. Both armies wrote it up, and they disagree about almost everything except that it happened. This introduction tags what each side says and what historians accept. Watch for the coloured tags.

## intro-2.mp3
*Intro screen 2: A new kind of war*

A new kind of war. In 1965 the United States sent its own ground forces into South Vietnam. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), the first division built around the helicopter, arrived at An Khe in the Central Highlands in September. North Vietnam had sent whole regiments of its regular army, the People’s Army of Vietnam, down the Ho Chi Minh trail to the Highlands. Their plan, by their own account, was to cut South Vietnam in two from the Cambodian border to the coast. Each side wanted to test the other. The Americans wanted to find the regulars and destroy them with helicopters, artillery and aircraft. The North Vietnamese wanted to learn how to fight the Americans, and to kill enough of them to matter.

## intro-3.mp3
*Intro screen 3: Plei Me*

Plei Me. On 19 October the PAVN 33rd Regiment besieged the Special Forces camp at Plei Me, forty kilometres south-west of Pleiku, while the 32nd Regiment waited to ambush the relief column. The siege failed, and the regiments pulled back west toward the Chu Pong massif and the Cambodian border. General Westmoreland ordered the 1st Cavalry Division to pursue. For three weeks its brigades leap-frogged west by helicopter, found caches and hospitals, and fought small actions. On 14 November the 3rd Brigade sent one battalion into the Ia Drang valley to look for the enemy at the foot of the mountain. The Vietnamese account says the B3 Front had gathered three regiments near the Chu Pong to rest and to prepare a second attack on Plei Me, and that the 66th Regiment had arrived from the trail only days before.

## intro-4.mp3
*Intro screen 4: The American army*

The American army. The 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry, Custer’s old regiment, under Lieutenant Colonel Harold G. Moore: 431 men on the day, in three rifle companies, a weapons company with mortars, and a headquarters. Rifle companies carried the new M16 rifle, M60 machine guns and M79 grenade launchers. Its strength was what it could call on: sixteen Huey transports, rocket-firing helicopters, two batteries of 105 mm howitzers at LZ Falcon eight kilometres away, fighter-bombers on call, and, for the first time in close support, B-52 bombers. Its weakness was the lift. Sixteen helicopters carried about a company each trip, and a trip took half an hour. The whole battalion needed four hours to land, and whoever was on the ground first would be alone.

## intro-5.mp3
*Intro screen 5: The North Vietnamese army*

The North Vietnamese army. The B3 Front under Chu Huy Man held three regiments of regulars near the Chu Pong: the 32nd and 33rd, tired from Plei Me, and the fresh 66th, with the H-15 Viet Cong battalion. On the mountain above X-Ray were the three battalions of the 66th and part of the 33rd. The Vietnamese account gives the field command to Nguyen Huu An, deputy commander of the Front, who watched the helicopters land from the slope and ordered an attack within the hour, before the Americans could dig in or bring the rest of their battalion. Their method was to get so close that American artillery and aircraft could not be used: in An’s phrase, to grab the enemy by the belt. Assault companies with AK-47s, grenades and rocket launchers, heavy weapons on the slope, and a withdrawal into the mountain when the fight turned. How many fought at X-Ray is not recorded. American estimates put about two thousand North Vietnamese on the mountain against 450 Americans on the first day.

## intro-6.mp3
*Intro screen 6: The ground*

The ground. Ia means river in the Jarai language. The Ia Drang runs west from the Chu Pong into Cambodia. At the foot of the mountain lay a clearing the size of a football field, named X-Ray on the map: scrub, elephant grass, trees up to thirty metres tall, and termite mounds taller than a man. A dry creek bed ran north and south a hundred metres west of the clearing, between it and the mountain. It became the front line. It was the dry season, forty degrees in the sun, and the grass burned where the napalm fell. Our map is a reconstruction: the clearing, the creek bed and the slope are placed from the American sketch maps, with the mountain off the western edge.

## intro-7.mp3
*Intro screen 7: The gamble*

The gamble. On the morning of 14 November Moore flew over the valley, chose X-Ray from three possible clearings, and ordered twenty minutes of artillery, rockets and air strikes on its edges. At 10:48 the first eight Hueys touched down with B Company and the battalion commander. A kilometre away, on the slope, two regiments of the North Vietnamese army were resting. Whether the Americans had landed next to the enemy by luck or by design, the fight that followed decided how both armies would fight the rest of the war. Choose how to see it: watch the reenactment as the sources describe it, or take command of either side and try to do better.

## event-xray-1.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 1: Prep fires and the first lift*

Twenty minutes of artillery, rockets and air strikes fall on the edges of the clearing. At 10:48 the first eight Hueys land B Company and the battalion commander. The clearing is quiet. Moore sends squads into the trees to look.

## explain-xray-1.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 1: Prep fires and the first lift*

Why land one company into the unknown? The sixteen helicopters could carry about a company a trip, and a trip took thirty minutes. Moore chose to land the lead company and let the rest follow rather than wait for a bigger lift that did not exist. The prep fires were meant to keep any enemy heads down while the first troopers got off the aircraft.

## event-xray-2.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 2: A prisoner*

B Company captures a deserter: an unarmed North Vietnamese soldier who has eaten only bananas for five days. He says there are three battalions on the mountain that want very much to kill Americans. Moore orders B Company toward the mountain to find them before they find the clearing.

## explain-xray-2.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 2: A prisoner*

The prisoner changed everything. Moore now knew he had landed beside a large force and that the rest of his battalion was still half an hour away. His choice was to push B Company out toward the creek bed and the mountain, to meet the enemy away from the landing zone where the helicopters had to come in.

## event-xray-3.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 3: A Company lands*

The second and third lifts bring A Company and part of C. A Company takes the creek bed to the south-west of the clearing. B Company reaches the creek and starts up the finger of the mountain beyond it. The first shots are heard from the slope.

## explain-xray-3.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 3: A Company lands*

The creek bed ran north to south between the clearing and the mountain, a natural trench a metre deep. Holding it meant the enemy had to cross open ground to reach the landing zone. Everything the battalion did for the next two days was built on that line.

## event-xray-4.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 4: First contact*

B Company’s lead platoon meets North Vietnamese soldiers coming down the slope and the fight begins. The 9th Battalion of the 66th Regiment is attacking toward the clearing. The 2nd Platoon under Lieutenant Herrick chases a withdrawing squad to the right and loses touch with the rest of the company.

## explain-xray-4.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 4: First contact*

Both accounts agree that the first attack came from the 9th Battalion, launched within two hours of the landing. The Vietnamese account says An ordered it as soon as he was sure where the Americans were; the American account says the battalion had been resting and had to be roused. Either way the attack ran into B Company on the finger rather than into the clearing.

## event-xray-5.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 5: The lost platoon*

Herrick’s platoon is cut off on a knoll a few hundred metres beyond the creek, surrounded by the rest of the 9th Battalion. Herrick is killed, then the sergeants who take over, until Sergeant Ernie Savage is in command with the radio. He calls artillery onto the slope around him and keeps calling it for the next twenty-six hours.

## explain-xray-5.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 5: The lost platoon*

Twenty-nine men went up the finger; nine were killed and thirteen wounded. The platoon survived because its position on the knoll was good, because the North Vietnamese could not bring enough men against it without walking into the artillery Savage directed, and because the enemy had bigger targets. Both battalions tried to reach it that afternoon and both failed.

## event-xray-6.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 6: Attack from the south*

C Company has landed and spread out along the south of the clearing when the 7th Battalion of the 66th Regiment attacks it out of the trees. Mortar rounds fall on the landing zone. Helicopters land under fire with ammunition and lift out the wounded. Air strikes and artillery hit the attackers in the open ground south of the creek.

## explain-xray-6.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 6: Attack from the south*

The 7th Battalion came in from the south, aiming at the clearing itself. It met C Company in the open and the full weight of the artillery at LZ Falcon and the fighter-bombers overhead. The American account counts this as the attack that was broken by firepower; the Vietnamese account says its companies pressed in close enough that the Americans shelled their own perimeter.

## event-xray-7.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 7: Into the creek bed*

A and B Companies attack across the creek toward the lost platoon and are stopped by machine guns on the finger. Lieutenant Walter Marm runs at a gun behind a termite mound and destroys it with a grenade and his rifle, and is shot through the face; he will receive the Medal of Honor. At dusk Moore pulls both companies back to the creek bed.

## explain-xray-7.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 7: Into the creek bed*

Why pull back? The attack had gained a hundred metres at a cost the battalion could not repeat, and darkness was an hour away. A perimeter that could be held through the night mattered more than the platoon on the knoll, which was still alive and still had artillery. It is the hardest decision in the American account, and Moore defends it in detail.

## event-xray-8.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 8: Reinforcements*

B Company of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, lands at dusk and takes the east and south-east of the perimeter. The reporter Joe Galloway comes in on one of the last helicopters. The battalion digs in around the clearing with the creek bed as its western wall. Every man knows the enemy will come in the night.

## explain-xray-8.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 8: Reinforcements*

The perimeter now had five rifle companies and the mortars, with artillery registered on every approach. The North Vietnamese account says An had decided to let the night pass with probes and to attack with everything at first light, when the aircraft could not yet see and the Americans would be tired.

## event-xray-9.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 9: The first night*

Flares hang over the clearing. The lost platoon is attacked three times in the night and holds, with Savage walking the artillery in to within fifty metres. Probes hit C Company and the creek bed. At the command post the radios count the rounds: by morning the batteries at LZ Falcon have fired more than four thousand.

## explain-xray-9.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 9: The first night*

Night fighting favoured the North Vietnamese, who could move close without being seen from the air, but the artillery did not need to see. The platoon on the knoll was ringed with pre-planned fires, and the perimeter had flares from a C-123 overhead. Both sides spent the night preparing for the morning.

## event-xray-10.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 10: Dawn assault*

At first light the 7th Battalion and the H-15 Viet Cong Battalion come out of the trees at C Company from the south and south-east, hundreds of men at a run. They reach the foxholes. Captain Edwards is shot; every platoon leader in C Company is killed or wounded. The fight is at grenade range along the whole southern arc.

## explain-xray-10.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 10: Dawn assault*

This was the main North Vietnamese effort of the battle, and the Vietnamese account treats it as the moment the Americans nearly broke. The American account agrees that C Company lost half its strength in an hour and that the line held only because the mortars, the reserve and every available rifle were thrown into it.

## event-xray-11.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 11: Broken Arrow*

Lieutenant Charlie Hastings, the air force controller, sends the code word Broken Arrow: an American unit is about to be overrun. Every aircraft in South Vietnam is diverted to X-Ray and stacked overhead. Napalm falls so close that two canisters hit the command post, burning soldiers and cooking off ammunition. By ten o’clock the assault is broken and the survivors are pulling back into the trees.

## explain-xray-11.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 11: Broken Arrow*

Broken Arrow put more than two hundred fighter-bomber sorties over a clearing the size of a football field. The North Vietnamese account says its battalions were caught in the open by the aircraft as they tried to close, and admits that the second morning cost the 66th Regiment heavily. The American account counts the bodies in front of C Company by the hundred.

## event-xray-12.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 12: Relief*

A Company of the 2nd Battalion lands in the morning. At noon Lieutenant Colonel Tully’s 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry, marches into the clearing from the east after a two-hour walk from LZ Victor. The perimeter now holds three battalions.

## explain-xray-12.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 12: Relief*

The relief came overland rather than by air because the landing zone was still under mortar fire and the helicopters were needed for ammunition and the wounded. The walk showed that the valley east of X-Ray was empty: the North Vietnamese were all on the mountain side.

## event-xray-13.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 13: The lost platoon rescued*

Three companies walk up the finger behind a wall of artillery and reach the knoll. Savage and the survivors have held for twenty-six hours. The dead and wounded are carried down to the clearing and flown out.

## explain-xray-13.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 13: The lost platoon rescued*

The rescue met little resistance: the 9th Battalion had pulled back into the mountain after the morning. The Vietnamese account barely mentions the platoon; in the American account it is the heart of the story.

## event-xray-14.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 14: Arc Light*

B-52 bombers from Guam drop their loads along the Chu Pong massif: the first time the strategic bombers are used in support of troops in contact. The mountain shakes for five days. The North Vietnamese command post moves higher up the slope.

## explain-xray-14.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 14: Arc Light*

Arc Light strikes were planned a day ahead and fell on map squares, not on targets anyone could see. Their effect on the regiments is disputed: the American account assumes heavy losses in the mountain; the Vietnamese account says the units had already dispersed and the bombs fell on empty jungle and on the wounded.

## event-xray-15.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 15: The second night*

The second night is quieter. Patrols find the enemy dead in the grass and the trees. The 8th Battalion of the 66th Regiment, held out of the fight so far, moves into position south of the clearing.

## explain-xray-15.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 15: The second night*

Why attack again? The Vietnamese account says the 8th Battalion was fresh and the Front wanted one more blow before the Americans left. The American account says the attack hit the strongest part of the perimeter, where B Company of the 2nd Battalion had spent two days digging.

## event-xray-16.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 16: The last assault*

Before dawn on the third day the 8th Battalion attacks B Company of the 2nd Battalion from the south-east. Artillery and flares catch the assault companies in the open; by half past six it is over. At five to seven the perimeter fires a mad minute into the trees and provokes one last rush, which is cut down.

## explain-xray-16.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 16: The last assault*

The mad minute, every weapon firing for sixty seconds at first light, was meant to trigger any enemy waiting to attack. It did. The American account counts the last attack as the clearest case of the battle: a battalion attacking dug-in infantry under artillery in the open, and losing.

## event-xray-17.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, event 17: The battalion flies out*

The valley is quiet. The 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry, is flown to Camp Holloway; Moore leaves last. Seventy-nine of its men are dead and 121 wounded. The 2nd Battalion and Tully’s battalion stay the night and will march out in the morning, one to LZ Columbus and one to a clearing named Albany.

## explain-xray-17.mp3
*LZ X-Ray, explain this moment 17: The battalion flies out*

Why march rather than fly? Another Arc Light strike was due on the Chu Pong and the clearing was inside its safety line, so the two battalions had to be clear of it by midday. The march to Albany, four kilometres through grass and scrub, is the subject of the second part.

## outcome-xray.mp3
*LZ X-Ray: historical outcome (debrief)*

The landing zone held for three days and the 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry, was flown out. The United States counted 79 of its own dead and 121 wounded at X-Ray. American officers counted 634 North Vietnamese bodies around the perimeter and estimated 1,215 killed in all, with six prisoners taken. The counts were made under fire and the estimate is not a count. Vietnamese histories give far lower figures for their own dead and claim many more Americans killed. Neither side’s figures for the other can be checked. Both sides claimed victory. The Americans had held the ground and inflicted heavy losses with firepower; the North Vietnamese had closed with a US battalion and fought it for three days without being destroyed. The next day the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, marched toward LZ Albany and was ambushed. That is the second part of this battle.

## event-albany-1.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 1: The march to Albany*

On the morning of 17 November the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, with A Company of the 1st Battalion, 5th Cavalry, marches north-east from X-Ray toward a clearing called Albany, four kilometres away: about 400 men in a column half a kilometre long, through elephant grass and scrub, in the heat.

## explain-albany-1.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 1: The march to Albany*

The battalion had to be clear of X-Ray before a B-52 strike on the Chu Pong. Its commander, Lieutenant Colonel McDade, had held the battalion for three weeks; most of its men had not fought at X-Ray. The column moved without artillery registered ahead of it, and with no air cover on call, which the American account treats as the first mistake of the day.

## event-albany-2.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 2: Prisoners*

The recon platoon captures two North Vietnamese soldiers near a small clearing. The column halts in the grass while they are questioned. McDade calls his company commanders forward to the head of the column to plan the last leg. The men, awake for three nights, sit down and some fall asleep.

## explain-albany-2.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 2: Prisoners*

Calling the commanders forward left every company in the middle of the column without its captain when the attack came. The two prisoners said they were deserters; the American account suspects they were scouts. By the Vietnamese account the 8th Battalion’s own reconnaissance had already found the column.

## event-albany-3.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 3: The ambush*

The 8th Battalion of the 66th Regiment and a battalion of the 33rd strike the column from both sides at once, in the grass, at a range of a few metres. The column is cut into pieces. C Company, in the middle, is shattered; the mortars of D Company are overrun; the head of the column fights its way into the Albany clearing with the command group.

## explain-albany-3.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 3: The ambush*

The Vietnamese account calls it a meeting engagement that its battalion turned into an ambush by attacking at once; the American account calls it an ambush of a column that had let its guard down. Both agree that the fight became hundreds of separate fights in grass taller than a man, where no one could see more than a few metres or tell friend from enemy.

## event-albany-4.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 4: Fighting in the grass*

The head of the column forms a perimeter in the Albany clearing. In the grass the middle of the column fights in squads and pairs. Air strikes and napalm come in an hour after the first shots, so close that they burn Americans as well as North Vietnamese. The rear, A Company of the 5th Cavalry, forms its own circle and holds.

## explain-albany-4.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 4: Fighting in the grass*

Air and artillery could not be called at first because no one could say where the Americans were. When the strikes came they fell on the grass between the perimeters, where the fighting was mixed. The Vietnamese account says the aircraft forced its battalions to break off before they could finish the column.

## event-albany-5.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 5: Reinforcement at dusk*

B Company of the 2nd Battalion, back from X-Ray, is flown into the Albany clearing at dusk through fire. A company from LZ Columbus marches in to the rear perimeter. Between the two perimeters the grass is full of wounded men who cannot be reached.

## explain-albany-5.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 5: Reinforcement at dusk*

Only two Hueys could land in the small clearing at a time, and the company came in by platoons under mortar fire. Its arrival secured the clearing for the night. It could not reach the men in the grass, who were half a kilometre away in the dark among the enemy.

## event-albany-6.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 6: The night*

Through the night flares light the grass. The American account says North Vietnamese soldiers moved through the field killing the wounded; the Vietnamese account does not mention it. Survivors crawl toward the perimeters. Artillery falls on the grass between them all night.

## explain-albany-6.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 6: The night*

Neither perimeter could send patrols into the grass without losing them. Soldiers in the open played dead or fought alone. The killing of the wounded is reported by several American survivors and by the reporter who reached Albany the next day, and is denied or passed over on the other side.

## event-albany-7.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 7: Morning*

At first light the North Vietnamese are gone. The battalion sweeps the grass and finds its dead: 155 killed and 124 wounded in one afternoon and night, the worst single loss of the war for an American battalion. The Americans count 403 enemy bodies in the field.

## explain-albany-7.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 7: Morning*

Albany is the battle that neither side put at the centre of its story. The American account gave it a few lines in the first reports and it was not fully told until the survivors spoke years later. The Vietnamese account counts it as the victory of the campaign: a US battalion caught in the open and cut to pieces.

## event-albany-8.mp3
*LZ Albany, event 8: The end of the campaign*

The battalions are flown out over the next two days. The 1st Cavalry Division counts the campaign a victory; so does the North Vietnamese army. The lessons each side drew would shape the next ten years.

## explain-albany-8.mp3
*LZ Albany, explain this moment 8: The end of the campaign*

The Americans concluded that airmobile infantry backed by artillery and aircraft could go anywhere and win, and measured success by the body count. The North Vietnamese concluded that they could fight the Americans by closing fast and leaving before the aircraft came, and that American losses mattered in a way their own did not. Both were right about the other.

## outcome-albany.mp3
*LZ Albany: historical outcome (debrief)*

The column lost 155 killed and 124 wounded on 17 November, the heaviest loss of any American battalion in a single action of the war. American officers counted 403 North Vietnamese bodies in the grass; the Vietnamese account does not give its losses at Albany. The Vietnamese account treats Albany as the decisive success of the campaign and the proof of fighting close. For the whole campaign from Plei Me to Albany the Americans counted 305 of their own dead and estimated more than 3,000 North Vietnamese killed. Vietnamese histories give far lower figures for their own losses. Westmoreland took the Ia Drang as proof that search-and-destroy with helicopters and firepower would win. Hanoi took it as proof that it could fight the Americans and outlast them. The war went on for nine more years.
